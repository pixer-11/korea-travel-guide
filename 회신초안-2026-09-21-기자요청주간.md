### English draft (~310 words)

Hi — Pixer here, editor of Wander Atlas. We publish destination guides plus hour-level
crowd measurements for named venues across 20 countries, so 48-hour itineraries are the
exact problem we work on.

The thing most 48-hour itineraries get wrong: they are sequenced by map proximity when
they should be sequenced by hour of day.

Our dataset covers 805 venues in 20 countries, measured 23 July to 20 September 2026. Of
the 580 venues that have a reliably quiet weekday hour, 84% (488) have one between 07:00
and 11:00. The most common reliably-busy weekday hour is 13:00, which holds for 184 of the
441 venues that register busy hours at all. A two-day trip contains roughly four usable
early-morning hours, and they are the scarcest resource on the whole plan.

Three named venues, weekday readings:

- Fushimi Inari Taisha, Kyoto — busy continuously 09:00 to 16:00; quiet at 07:00 and again
  20:00 to 22:00. One of the few sites where evening is a genuine second window, because
  the mountain paths are not gated.
- Nara Park, Nara — busy 10:00 to 16:00; quiet at 07:00 and 20:00 to 22:00. It is an hour
  from Kyoto, so the 07:00 slot only works if it is the day's first move, not its second.
- Gwangjang Market, Seoul — busy 11:00 to 15:00; quiet at 09:00 and 19:00 to 21:00. Most
  48-hour Seoul plans put it at lunch, which is the worst hour of its day.

The rule we would give a reader: one 07:00 to 08:00 anchor per day, indoor or ticketed
items at 12:00 to 15:00, and 19:00 to 21:00 treated as a second sightseeing window rather
than dinner only.

Crowd data measured by BestTime.app. Method and the full venue list:
https://wanderatlasguides.com/tools/best-time/

### English draft (~290 words)

Hi — I'm Pixer, editor of Wander Atlas. I'm a Korean writer living in Vietnam, and
holidays and festivals are a standing beat for us: 171 of our guides cover dated events,
across 19 countries.

What I would want to say about holiday travel, from living inside one: for most Asian
lunar holidays the traveler's real problem is closures, not crowds. Tet in Vietnam is the
clearest case. Visitors arrive expecting a public spectacle and find a city that has gone
home. Family kitchens replace restaurants, small shops shut for anywhere from three to
seven days depending on the household, and the streets that were impassable on the last
market day before the holiday are quiet on the holiday itself. Seollal in Korea behaves
the same way. Golden Week in Japan inverts it — everything open, everything full — which
is why lumping "Asian holidays" together misleads people.

Two things a listener could act on. First, the useful question before a holiday trip is
"what is open on my dates," not "how busy will it be"; we built a page for exactly that.
Second, the day to travel for is the day *before* the holiday. Markets run at their
fullest and most generous, and that is when the food traditions are visible in public. On
the holiday itself the tradition has moved indoors, into homes you are not in.

Happy to talk through Tet, Seollal and Chuseok specifically, including what a visitor can
and cannot join.

https://wanderatlasguides.com/tools/whats-closed/
