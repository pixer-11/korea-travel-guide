## Four nights to close the jazz year

Jazzfest Berlin comes at the end of the jazz calendar. The 2026 edition was set for **October 29 to November 1**, with four days of programming organised by the Berliner Festspiele, the federal arts organisation behind several of the city's major festivals.

The organisers call it the finale of the jazz year, and the timing explains why. Summer festival season is long over and clubs are back to their regular weeks. Then for one long weekend, the city's jazz audience usually gathers in one place.

Berlin is dark by late afternoon at this time of year, because the clocks go back the weekend before. So evening concerts start when the streets outside already feel like night. That suits a festival that stays indoors.

## From Berliner Jazztage to Jazzfest

This is one of Europe's oldest jazz festivals. It began in 1964 as the **Berliner Jazztage**, founded under the critic and producer Joachim-Ernst Berendt. It later took the name Jazzfest Berlin and became part of the Berliner Festspiele.

Through the Cold War decades, West Berlin used the festival to put American jazz next to new European improvised music. Arguments about what counts as jazz have followed it ever since. Recent editions keep that spirit with commissions, cross-genre projects and international bills rather than a parade of greatest hits.

That makes the programme notes worth reading. Plenty of the most talked-about sets come from names that are not yet widely known.

## The Schaperstraße stage

The festival has long been based at the **Haus der Berliner Festspiele**, Schaperstraße 24, in Wilmersdorf. Architect Fritz Bornemann designed it, and it opened in 1963 as the theatre of the Freie Volksbühne. The building is postwar modernist, low and horizontal, with a glass front onto a small garden.

Inside there's a large raked auditorium, so sightlines are generally good from most seats, plus foyers where people gather between sets. In past years the festival has also used other rooms and partner venues around the city, so the Haus was not necessarily the only 2026 location.

## Getting to Wilmersdorf

The Haus der Berliner Festspiele is easy to reach by public transport:

- **U Spichernstraße** (U3 and U9) is a short walk away. The Bundesallee exits lead toward Schaperstraße.
- **U Hohenzollernplatz** (U3) is also within walking distance.
- **Kurfürstendamm** and the Kaiser-Wilhelm-Gedächtniskirche are one or two stops north, which helps for anyone staying in City West.

Berlin's BVG network covers the U-Bahn, S-Bahn, trams and buses on a single ticket system. On weekends the U-Bahn runs all night, so a late set doesn't mean missing the last train home. On weeknights, night buses take over after the trains stop.

Driving into this residential part of Wilmersdorf after dark isn't worth it. Street parking is tight and the train is quicker.

## Tickets, cloakrooms and festival manners

Tickets were sold through the **Berliner Festspiele**, on its official website and at its box office. Prices were set per concert, and seated and standing formats could differ. The programme mixed larger evening concerts with smaller sets.

A few habits are standard at Berlin concerts:

- **The Garderobe matters.** German concert halls expect heavy coats to go to the cloakroom rather than to your seat. Late October is coat weather.
- **Doors close on time.** Late entry to seated concerts is often restricted until a break between pieces, so latecomers can end up stuck in the foyer.
- **Quiet passages stay quiet.** Audiences here listen closely, especially during free improvisation. Applause after solos happens, but the room sets the cue.
- **Cash still helps.** Cards are now widely accepted in Berlin, but smaller bars and Spätis nearby may still prefer cash.
- **Paper BVG tickets need validating.** Single tickets bought as paper tickets need stamping in the machines on the platform. Inspectors do check, and the fine is not a fun festival souvenir.

## Making a long weekend of it

Four days of programming left room to mix main-stage concerts with the rest of the neighbourhood. Wilmersdorf is a quiet, residential district with plenty to do on foot.

- **Ludwigkirchplatz**, a short walk north, is a leafy square lined with cafés and restaurants. It works well for an early dinner before a concert.
- **Kurfürstendamm** has the department stores and the bombed-out spire of the Kaiser-Wilhelm-Gedächtniskirche, kept as a war memorial.
- **KaDeWe**, the large department store on Tauentzienstraße, has a food hall on its upper floor, a warm place to spend a grey afternoon.

Berlin's year-round jazz clubs are close. **A-Trane** in Charlottenburg and **Quasimodo** under the Delphi cinema near Zoologischer Garten are long-running clubs in the same part of town. They run separate programmes from the festival.

## Planning around late autumn

Late October into early November is usually cold and often damp in Berlin. Light goes early in the day. Layers work better than one heavy jacket, since auditoriums get warm once they fill.

Neither October 31 nor November 1 is a public holiday in Berlin, so shops and transport keep their normal hours. Brandenburg, the state that surrounds the city, does observe Reformation Day on October 31, which affects day trips to Potsdam.

Hotels around City West and Wilmersdorf are within a short ride of the Haus der Berliner Festspiele. This is also a busy stretch for trade fairs and autumn city breaks.
