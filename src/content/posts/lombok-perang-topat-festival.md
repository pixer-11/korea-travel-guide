## Rice cakes as ammunition

The ammunition here is breakfast food. Topat, Lombok's name for ketupat, is rice cooked inside a small pouch of woven young coconut leaf. At Perang Topat, thousands of these pouches get thrown across a temple courtyard by people who, on any other day, pray in different ways.

The "war" is between Balinese Hindus and Sasak Muslims, Lombok's two largest communities, who share the Pura Lingsar complex. Nobody is trying to win. The point of the ritual is the shared act of throwing, and the 2026 festival on December 15 carries that meaning of harmony between the two faiths.

## A date set by the Sasak calendar

The date isn't fixed on the Western calendar. Perang Topat traditionally falls on the full moon of the seventh month of the Sasak calendar, so it moves between November and December from year to year. In 2026 it takes place on December 15.

It sits inside the larger Pujawali temple festival at Lingsar. That means the throwing is the climax of a longer day of offerings, processions and prayer. If you only turn up for the rice fight, you miss most of the ceremony.

This is a religious ceremony in an open temple compound rather than a concert, and no ticketing platform is attached to it. West Lombok's regency tourism office handles visitor-facing information about the festival.

## Pura Lingsar, where two faiths share a wall

Pura Lingsar is one of the few temple complexes in Indonesia built for more than one religion. It sits among rice fields in Lingsar village, in West Lombok regency. The compound dates from the 18th century, when Balinese rulers controlled western Lombok.

Inside, the complex divides into two parts at different levels:

- **Pura Gaduh**, the Hindu temple, with shrines facing both Bali's Gunung Agung and Lombok's Gunung Rinjani.
- **The Kemaliq**, a shrine sacred to the Wetu Telu, a Sasak tradition that blends Islam with older local belief.
- **A spring-fed pond** in the Kemaliq area, home to sacred eels that visitors traditionally feed with hard-boiled eggs.

The spring that feeds the pond is what gave the place its name and its role. Water for the surrounding rice fields starts here, and that is why the festival is so closely tied to farming.

## When the waru flower falls

The throwing doesn't start at a set clock time. By tradition it begins in late afternoon, at the moment locals call *rarak kembang waru*: when the waru (hibiscus tree) flower drops. That puts it near the end of the afternoon prayers, after offerings have been carried through the compound.

Before that, processions bring the topat in, often stacked in tall arrangements alongside fruit and other offerings. Once the signal comes, the compound fills with pouches flying in every direction. It is loud and fast, and it stays good-natured. Spectators near the edges usually get hit too.

Afterward, many farmers gather up spent topat and carry them home. They scatter them in their paddies or bury them at the edges, as an offering for fertile soil and a good harvest. The fragments lying in the courtyard are the most important part of the day to them.

## Getting to Lingsar

Lingsar is about 30 minutes by road east of central Mataram, the provincial capital. From Lombok International Airport near Praya the drive is roughly an hour. From Senggigi on the west coast, allow around 45 minutes.

There's no rail on Lombok and public bemo routes to Lingsar are sparse. Visitors usually come by hired car with driver, taxi or ride-hailing app, or by rented scooter. On festival day the lanes around the village fill with worshippers and vehicles. Most drivers drop passengers at the edge of the crowd and wait somewhere outside the village.

Most international visitors stay in Mataram or Senggigi and treat Lingsar as an afternoon trip. The Gili Islands are too far for a quick return on the same evening once you add the boat crossing.

## Moving through the temple courtyard

This is a working place of worship first, so behaviour follows temple rules.

- **Sarong and sash.** Entering Pura Lingsar requires a sarong and a selendang (temple sash) tied at the waist. Sarongs are customarily lent or rented at the entrance, and many visitors bring their own.
- **Shoulders covered.** Sleeved tops are standard for men and women in Balinese and Sasak temples.
- **Menstruation.** By Balinese Hindu custom, women who are menstruating do not enter the inner temple areas.
- **Offerings first.** Don't step over or on the small offerings placed on the ground. Never stand in front of people who are praying to get a photo.
- **Donation box.** Temples like this usually ask for a donation at the gate rather than charging admission. The amount is up to you, and you give it in cash.

The space gets tight during the throwing. Photographers who want clean shots usually find a raised spot along the courtyard walls. Phones and cameras do take a hit, and topat leave sticky rice on everything. Shoes that can get muddy are a better choice than sandals that slip off in a crush.

Locals see the throwing as a blessing, not a nuisance. Getting hit is part of taking part, so don't throw hard at people who are still praying.

## Around Lingsar on festival day

The temple is in a cluster of water sites east of Mataram, and these are easy to add to the same day.

- **Taman Narmada.** A 18th-century royal garden with terraced pools, laid out as a miniature of Gunung Rinjani and its crater lake. It is a short drive south-east of Lingsar.
- **Pura Suranadi.** A Hindu spring temple in the forested hills further east, with its own sacred eels and cool bathing pools.
- **Mataram's Cakranegara district.** Home to Pura Meru, Lombok's largest Hindu temple, and the evening food stalls nearby.

Many visitors see Narmada in the morning, when the Lingsar ceremonies are still building, and then head to the temple for the afternoon. The return drive to Mataram after the throwing is slow because the whole crowd leaves at once. Most people just wait it out at a warung in the village.
