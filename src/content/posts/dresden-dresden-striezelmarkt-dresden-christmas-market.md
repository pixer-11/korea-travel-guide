## Six centuries on one square

The Altmarkt has held this market since 1434. That makes the Striezelmarkt Germany's oldest Christmas market, and the 2026 edition is its 592nd. It runs from November 25 to December 24, 2026.

For a month, the broad square in Dresden's Altstadt fills with rows of wooden huts. The Kreuzkirche stands at its southeast corner, and the Kulturpalast closes off the north side. The market sits inside the old town, so the Frauenkirche and the Elbe riverfront are only a few minutes' walk away.

## Reaching the Altmarkt

The square is easy to reach on foot or by tram.

- **Tram:** the Altmarkt stop sits on the square's edge, and the Pirnaischer Platz and Postplatz stops are a short walk away.
- **From Dresden Hauptbahnhof:** walk north along the pedestrianised Prager Straße. It takes about 15 minutes and ends at the market's southern side.
- **From the Neustadt:** cross the Augustusbrücke, pass the Frauenkirche and Neumarkt, then head south to the Altmarkt.

Driving into the old town during Advent is slow, and city-centre parking garages fill up. Most visitors use the tram, or they stay within walking distance in the Altstadt or around Prager Straße.

## Striezel: the cake behind the name

The market is named after its pastry. *Striezel* is the old local word for Stollen: a heavy, loaf-shaped Christmas bread made with butter, raisins and candied peel, then covered in powdered sugar. Dresdner Christstollen is a protected regional product, and bakers from the city and its surroundings sell it all over the market.

Stollen is usually sold whole or in thick slices, wrapped in paper. A whole loaf is dense and keeps for weeks, which is why so many visitors carry one home in their luggage.

The **Dresdner Stollenfest** is the market's big set piece. It is traditionally held on the Saturday before the second Sunday of Advent. A giant Stollen weighing several tonnes is paraded through the old town to the Altmarkt, where it is cut with an oversized ceremonial knife and sold off in pieces. The organisers publish the date of each year's parade on the official programme.

## Plum men, pyramids and Erzgebirge craft

Much of what's on sale comes from the Ore Mountains (Erzgebirge), the wood-carving region south of Dresden. Expect to see:

- **Nutcrackers and smoking men** (*Räuchermännchen*), carved figures that puff incense smoke from their mouths
- **Schwibbögen**, arched candle-holders made for windowsills
- **Christmas pyramids**, tiered carousels turned by the heat of candles
- **Herrnhut stars**, many-pointed paper and plastic stars from Upper Lusatia

The market has its own giant Erzgebirge step pyramid. Its tiers of carved figures turn slowly above the stalls, and it is one of the main landmarks in the square. People often agree to "meet at the pyramid" because everyone can find it.

Then there are the **Pflaumentoffel**. These are small chimney-sweep figures made from dried prunes on wooden sticks, with a little paper ladder and a top hat. They are the market's oldest symbol of good luck and a cheap, very local souvenir.

## At the Glühwein counter

Drinking at the Striezelmarkt follows a set routine, and knowing it in advance saves fumbling at the hatch.

You order Glühwein (mulled wine) or a non-alcoholic punch, and it comes in a ceramic mug. The price you pay includes a deposit, the *Pfand*, for the mug. If you take the mug back to any stall in the same system, you get the deposit back. If you keep it, it's yours. The Striezelmarkt mug design changes every year, so many people keep one as a dated souvenir.

A few habits help:

- Order in German if you can: *"Einen Glühwein, bitte"*. Ask for *"mit Schuss"* to get a shot of rum or amaretto added.
- Many stalls take cards now, but cash is still common at the smaller craft and food huts. Paying in coins makes your turn quicker.
- There is rarely a formal queue. People stand in a loose crowd at the counter, so hold your place, make eye contact, and order once you're at the front.
- Most people eat standing at the high tables between the huts. Using a table for a while is fine; leaving your empty mug on it is not, because the stall loses its mug.

Try *Bratwurst* in a bread roll, *Quarkkeulchen* (fried curd-cheese pancakes) and *Handbrot*, a baked bread filled with ham and cheese.

## Tickets, timing and how long to stay

Entry to the Striezelmarkt is free and you don't need a ticket. The city of Dresden runs the market and publishes the daily opening hours and the stage programme on its official site. December 24 is the last day, and as on Christmas Eve everywhere in Germany, the market closes earlier than usual.

Allow an hour and a half to two hours for one slow loop with food, a drink and some browsing. The square is at its most crowded on weekend evenings and on the Stollenfest Saturday, when the parade draws large crowds into the old town. Visitors who want to look at the carvings in peace usually come on a weekday, while it's still light.

Dresden in late November and December is cold and often damp. The square is paved and exposed, so layers and waterproof shoes help more than a heavy coat you can't sit down in.

## Other Advent markets in the old town

The Striezelmarkt is the oldest market in town, but not the only one. Many visitors combine several in an evening:

- **Advent on the Neumarkt**, the market around the Frauenkirche, with the rebuilt baroque square as its backdrop
- **The medieval market in the Stallhof**, the long courtyard of the Residenzschloss, with costumed traders and torches
- **The Augustusmarkt** on the Hauptstraße in the Neustadt, across the Augustusbrücke

From the Altmarkt you can walk to all of them in 5 to 15 minutes. One route links them all: Altmarkt to Neumarkt, through the Stallhof, then over the river.
