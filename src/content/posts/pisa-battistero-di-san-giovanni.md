## What you're looking at

This is Italy's largest baptistery, started in 1152 and not finished for two centuries, which is why the lower half is Romanesque arches and the upper half sprouts Gothic gables and pinnacles. The dome, a mismatched pairing of a hemisphere over one half and a cone over the other, was actually built by two different roofs a hundred years apart, which is why the exterior profile looks slightly lopsided from the piazza.

Inside, the octagonal font by Guido Bigarelli dates to 1246, ringed by ambo panels carved by Nicola Pisano in 1260, his figures showing an early break from stiff medieval carving toward something closer to classical movement. Pisano's pulpit here predates the more famous one his son Giovanni carved for the Cathedral next door.

## The acoustics, and why everyone waits for them

The dome's shape creates a natural echo chamber, and the building's custodians demonstrate it by singing a few sustained notes that hang in the air for close to ten seconds. This happens on a rough half-hourly schedule through the day, not on demand, so budget time to wait if you arrive between demonstrations.

Stand near the font, not by the entrance, for the fullest effect; the sound rolls up into the dome and back down over the whole room. It's the reason many visitors rate this above the Duomo itself.

## Getting in and timing it right

The baptistery keeps long hours, 9:00 AM to 10:30 PM every day of the week, which is unusually late for a religious building and worth using if you want thinner crowds.

- Buy tickets online or at the ticket office behind the Duomo; single-site tickets exist but most visitors buy the combined pass covering the Baptistery, Camposanto, and one museum
- Climbing to the upper gallery costs extra and is where you get the best view down onto the font and the best sense of the acoustics
- Bags larger than a small daypack usually need to go in the free lockers near the ticket office

Early morning, right after 9:00 AM, and the couple of hours before closing are calmer; midday sees tour groups arriving in waves timed to bus schedules from the coach parks outside the walls.

## Making the most of five minutes inside

This building draws heavy year-round traffic given its position on one of Italy's most photographed lawns, so don't expect solitude. Go in with a plan: walk the font first, then climb to the gallery if you bought that ticket, then wait out one acoustic demonstration before leaving.

Skip flash photography near the carved panels; the marble surfaces are original thirteenth-century work, not restorations. Combine the visit with the Cathedral and Camposanto on the same ticket rather than doubling back later, since all three sit within a hundred metres of each other on the same lawn.
