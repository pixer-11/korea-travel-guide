## Sunday, December 6, 2026

Macau's biggest road race falls on a Sunday. The Galaxy Entertainment Macao International Marathon takes place on December 6, 2026. It offers a full marathon, a half marathon and shorter distances, and all of them start from the Olympic Sports Centre Stadium.

December is a good month for running in Macau. The humid summer is long gone by then, and early winter days on the Pearl River Delta are usually cool and dry. That suits a race where a lot of the course is exposed, out on bridges and waterfront roads with no shade.

## Three bridges, one small city

Macau is tiny, so the route doesn't spread out. It stacks up. The summary for this race says runners cross Macau's bridges and the historic peninsula, and here that means some of the course is high above open water.

Three road bridges link Taipa to the peninsula:

- **Governador Nobre de Carvalho Bridge**, the original arched crossing, locally called the "old bridge"
- **Friendship Bridge** (Ponte da Amizade), the longer eastern span
- **Sai Van Bridge**, the double-decker with the lower deck enclosed against typhoon winds

On a bridge you're climbing a long, steady ramp, and you hear your own footsteps more than the crowd. Wind comes across the estuary from the side, and there's nowhere to hide from it. Then you're down on the peninsula, among hotel towers, waterfront promenades and narrower streets. Few marathons switch scenery that fast.

## The Olympic Sports Centre Stadium start

The start is at the Macau Olympic Sports Centre Stadium in Taipa. It's a full athletics stadium with stands, a running track and a big apron outside. On race morning that apron becomes the holding area, where runners queue for bag drop and toilets before filing into their start pens.

The stadium works as the race hub because it's big enough for several distance groups to gather at once. Because the full, half and shorter races share one venue, early arrivals tend to crowd together. Runners typically get there well before their wave so they aren't caught in the bag-drop queue.

## Getting to Taipa

Most international runners come into Macau through Hong Kong. The usual routes are:

- **Ferry** from Hong Kong to Taipa Ferry Terminal or the Outer Harbour Ferry Terminal on the peninsula
- **Hong Kong–Zhuhai–Macao Bridge** shuttle bus, which arrives at the Macau border port
- **Macau International Airport**, which is itself in Taipa, a short taxi ride from the stadium

The Taipa Line of the Macao Light Rapid Transit has a **Stadium** station beside the sports complex. Road closures for the race change the traffic pattern across Taipa and the bridges on the day. Runners staying in Taipa or Cotai can often reach the start on foot, and that's why many entrants book hotels on that side of the water.

## Where spectators gather

Watching costs nothing, because the course runs on public roads. Supporters tend to split into two groups. One stays near the stadium for the start and finish. The other crosses to the peninsula to catch runners on the waterfront stretches.

Nobody can watch from the bridges themselves, since they're closed to traffic and pedestrians during the race. The approaches on either side are where the noise builds. A runner who has just come off a long, quiet span tends to hear that cheering the loudest.

## Race morning, the Macau way

Macau's crowds and payment habits work a little differently from most race cities. These are the details that trip up first-timers.

- **Money.** The local currency is the pataca. Hong Kong dollars are widely accepted, but change usually comes back in patacas. Local e-wallets such as MPay are common at convenience stores.
- **Cheering.** You'll hear "gaa yau" (加油, literally "add oil") shouted from the roadside. It means "keep going", and runners shout it back to each other on the bridges.
- **Languages.** Signs are in Chinese and Portuguese, the city's two official languages, with English common around the hotels and the race venue. Street names on the peninsula appear in both scripts, which helps when you're meeting someone after the finish.
- **Post-race food.** Finishers usually leave the stadium in search of congee, noodles or a pork chop bun rather than a sit-down brunch. Casual Cantonese breakfast places open early and turn tables fast.

Everyday road manners in Macau are orderly. People queue in lines at bus stops and ferry gates, and pushing is frowned upon. The same goes for the bag-drop queue and the start pens.

## After the finish: Taipa Village and the old town

Taipa Village is a walk or short ride from the stadium, so many runners go there to recover. **Rua do Cunha** is its narrow pedestrian lane of bakeries and snack shops, known for almond cookies and pork jerky. Just beyond it are the **Taipa Houses**, a row of mint-green Macanese villas facing what used to be the waterfront.

If your legs are still working, cross to the peninsula. **Senado Square** has wave-patterned Portuguese paving underfoot. A few minutes uphill, the stone facade of the **Ruins of St. Paul's** stands alone at the top of its wide staircase. Every step of that staircase feels it after 42 kilometres.

Cotai's resort strip, including the title sponsor's Galaxy Macau, is also close to the stadium. Plenty of finishers spend the afternoon there with nothing more strenuous planned than a pool and a long lunch.
