## White tents along the Willamette

The market sits right against the river. Rows of white pop-up canopies line the grass and paths of Tom McCall Waterfront Park. The steel bulk of the Burnside Bridge runs overhead at the north end, and the Willamette slides past a few steps east.

Walk in and you are in narrow aisles between booths. Tables hold ceramics, leather goods, jewelry, prints and clothing, and the person behind each table is usually the one who made what's on it. Somewhere nearby a busker or a small stage act is working the crowd, because entertainment is part of the program.

This is a well-known Portland fixture, not a secret. It has run since 1974 and is often described as the largest continuously operating outdoor arts and crafts market in the United States. Expect company, and plan around it.

## The makers' rule

This is the detail that sets it apart. The market's founding principle is that vendors sell what they make themselves. Stalls are juried, so resellers of imported souvenirs are not the point here.

In practice, you can ask how something was made and get a real answer. A potter can tell you about the glaze. A woodworker can explain the joinery. That conversation is half the value of buying here, and most vendors welcome it when the booth isn't crowded.

What you'll typically find:

- Handmade jewelry and metalwork
- Pottery, glass and woodwork
- Screen-printed shirts, posters and art prints
- Leather bags, belts and wallets
- Soaps, candles and body products
- Kids' toys and clothing

Oregon has no sales tax. The price on the tag is what you pay, which makes budgeting easy.

## Lunch from the carts

The food section is a cluster of carts and booths rather than a single hall. It's classic Portland street eating: a mix of cuisines served from trailers and counters, eaten standing up or at whatever seating is out that day.

The easy move is to browse first and eat second. Or split it: grab something early to carry while you walk, then return for a proper meal. Because vendors change over the season, treat the food as a walk-past-and-choose exercise, not a fixed menu to research ahead.

Waterfront Park itself is your dining room. Take your plate to the river wall or onto the lawn and watch the boats pass under the Burnside Bridge.

## Getting to Naito Parkway

The address is 2 SW Naito Pkwy, on the Old Town edge of downtown. Transit beats driving here.

- **MAX Light Rail:** Skidmore Fountain station, on the Blue and Red lines, stops at SW 1st Avenue a short walk from the market.
- **On foot from downtown:** head east toward the river and follow the waterfront path north to the Burnside Bridge.
- **By car:** downtown garages and street meters apply, and Saturday spaces near the waterfront fill up. Park a few blocks inland and walk.

Coming from the east side, the walk across the Burnside Bridge drops you almost on top of the market.

## Saturday means Saturday

Don't let old information catch you out. The market's name has always said Saturday, but for years it also ran on Sundays, and plenty of listings still say so. The current schedule shows Saturday only, 10am to 5pm, with every other day marked closed.

Confirm on the market's official site before you commit a Sunday to it. It's also a seasonal market, traditionally running from spring through the holiday season before closing for winter, so check dates if you're visiting in January or February.

Portland's weather matters too. The market runs under canopies, and a light rain doesn't empty it. Bring a hood or a compact umbrella instead of skipping the trip.

## Working the aisles without the elbow fight

A little strategy makes it much easier. The aisles are narrow, and a slow crowd builds around the most photogenic booths. These habits help:

- **Come at opening.** Arriving right at 10am usually gets you easier browsing before the lunch crowd fills in.
- **Do a lap first.** Walk every row once without buying, then go back. The same style of item often appears at several booths.
- **Carry a tote.** Many vendors bag purchases, but a single sturdy bag beats a fistful of small ones.
- **Pay how the booth prefers.** Most vendors take cards, while some smaller stalls also like cash. A few small bills cover you either way.
- **Ask before photographing work.** These are original designs, and makers reasonably care about copying.
- **Step aside to talk.** If you're chatting with a vendor, stand at the side of the booth so other shoppers can see the table.

Buskers and street performers work for tips. If you stop and watch a full set, drop something in the case.

## Around the market in Old Town

The market sits in a part of Portland that rewards an extra hour or two. Just west, at Ankeny Plaza, the Skidmore Fountain dates to 1888 and is one of the city's oldest public artworks. The cast-iron building fronts nearby are part of the Skidmore/Old Town historic district.

Voodoo Doughnut's original shop is a few blocks inland on SW 3rd Avenue, and the line is usually visible from the corner. A short walk north leads to Lan Su Chinese Garden in Chinatown, a walled Suzhou-style garden that feels a world away from the market.

To the south, the waterfront path runs along Tom McCall Waterfront Park past the Hawthorne and Morrison bridges. It's a flat, easy walk to digest lunch with the river on one side and the downtown skyline on the other.

One thing to get right: the Portland Farmers Market is a different event. Its main Saturday market is at Portland State University on the south side of downtown. If you want produce stalls, that's where to go. If you want handmade goods under the Burnside Bridge, you're in the right place.
