## A palace you read from the bottom up

The building came first. Before it held artefacts, this cream-coloured Italianate villa on Tariq al-Horreya was a private mansion built for a wealthy Alexandrian merchant in the 1920s. Later it served as a consulate, and today it is one of the city's main museums. A small walled garden separates it from the traffic.

Inside, the floors run in historical order. The basement holds the Pharaonic period, the ground floor the Graeco-Roman city, and the upper floor the Coptic, Islamic and modern eras. Walk down first and climb as you go, and you move forward through several thousand years of the same coastline.

## What waits on each floor

The basement is the most atmospheric part. The lighting is low, and there is a reconstructed burial chamber alongside statues, sarcophagi and funerary objects. It is the closest you'll get to tomb-style display without leaving Alexandria.

The ground floor is the city's own story. Look for:

- Statues and portrait heads from Ptolemaic and Roman Alexandria
- Pieces recovered from underwater excavations in the harbour and Abu Qir Bay
- Tanagra-style figurines, pottery and everyday objects from the ancient city

Upstairs the mood shifts. Coptic textiles and icons give way to Islamic coins, ceramics and metalwork. The final rooms cover the last royal family and the 20th century. The labels are in Arabic and English, so you can manage without a guide.

## Planning around the crowds

The museum is currently open 9am–4:30pm every day this week. Hours in Egypt can change around holidays and during Ramadan, so check the Ministry of Tourism and Antiquities listing before you set out.

On weekdays it is usually calmest from 9am to 10am. At weekends the quiet windows are 9am–11am and 3pm–4pm. Avoid weekends between 11am and 3pm, when school groups and families fill the narrow basement rooms.

Plan on 1.5 to 2 hours. If you arrive at 3pm on a weekend, you have enough time for the basement and ground floor before closing, but the upper floor will feel rushed.

## Getting through the door

Taxis and ride-hailing apps are the easiest way to get there. Give the driver "El Mathaf El Qawmi, Tariq al-Horreya" (National Museum, Horreya Road). The street address is 131 El-Shaheed Galal El-Desouky in Bab Sharqi.

A few things that make entry smoother:

- Bags go through an X-ray scanner at the gate, so carry as little as you can.
- Ticket prices and any camera or phone-photography rules are posted at the ticket window. Read them before you pay, because policies at Egyptian museums change.
- Flash is not welcome near the textiles and painted pieces upstairs.
- Guards sometimes offer to point things out, and they expect a small tip (baksheesh) afterwards. Saying "la, shukran" (no, thank you) politely is fine if you'd rather wander alone.

Dress modestly. You don't have to cover your head, but shoulders and knees covered is normal in Alexandria and draws less attention on the walk in.

## Walking on from Horreya Road

The museum makes a good first stop on a day in central Alexandria, because it gives you the context for the ruins nearby. Kom el-Dikka, with its Roman theatre and excavated baths, is a short walk away in the same part of town. You'll recognise the Graeco-Roman layers there straight after seeing them in the display cases.

The Bibliotheca Alexandrina and the Corniche are a short taxi ride north. If you still want more antiquities, the Catacombs of Kom el-Shoqafa and Pompey's Pillar are further southwest. Together they make a full day.

For lunch, head toward Raml Station and the streets behind the Corniche. Old cafés and bakeries line the area around Saad Zaghloul Square, and the walk from the museum takes you through the downtown blocks of 19th- and 20th-century apartment buildings.
