This is an annual book fair built around readings. The 2026 edition had a strong English-language programme, which matters for anyone who doesn't read Georgian. An English session lets a visitor follow along, ask a question and leave with something they can actually read on the plane home.

## What the English-language programme means for you

The English strand is the reason many international visitors come. Georgian literature has its own script and a long tradition, and most of it reaches outsiders only through translation. A fair that programmes in English puts visitors in the room with that conversation, not outside it.

In practice, a festival-goer who doesn't read Georgian usually does three things:

- picks English-language sessions from the printed or online schedule
- looks for Georgian authors in English translation on the stalls
- uses Georgian-language readings to hear the language aloud, even without understanding it

That last one rewards the effort. Spoken Georgian has clustered consonants and a rhythm unlike any language around it. Hearing a poet read it is a different experience from seeing it on a sign.

## A city that writes in its own alphabet

Georgian script is the first thing that makes a book fair here feel unlike one in Berlin or London. The modern alphabet, Mkhedruli, has 33 letters with rounded, looping shapes and no capital letters. UNESCO added the living culture of Georgia's three writing systems to its intangible heritage list in 2016.

The national epic is Shota Rustaveli's *The Knight in the Panther's Skin*, written in the 12th century. Rustaveli's name is everywhere in the city: on the main avenue, the national theatre and a metro station. The poem is a staple of Tbilisi bookstalls, often in several translations.

Georgia also had its moment on the world publishing stage in 2018, as Guest of Honour at the Frankfurt Book Fair. That push brought far more Georgian writing into English and German. Those translations are exactly what an international reader goes looking for at Tbilisi Book Days.

Tbilisi is easy to move around on a fair weekend. The metro is cheap, frequent and deep underground, with long escalators. Line 1 runs through the centre with stops at Liberty Square and Rustaveli, the two stations nearest the old-town end of Rustaveli Avenue.

Ride-hailing apps, Bolt above all, are how most visitors cover the distances the metro doesn't. The ride usually beats waving down a street taxi and haggling over the fare.

## Moving through a Tbilisi book fair

A few habits make a Tbilisi book fair smoother.

- **Paying:** cards and contactless work widely in central Tbilisi. Smaller stalls and secondhand sellers often prefer cash in Georgian lari, so many visitors carry some.
- **Language:** a "gamarjoba" (hello) and "madloba" (thank you) goes a long way with stallholders, even if the rest of the chat is in English.
- **Signings and readings:** attendees typically drift towards a session room before it starts. The popular names fill seats quickly.
- **Luggage:** book-buyers flying out usually bring a foldable tote. Hardbacks in an unfamiliar script are heavy, and Georgian editions are hard to find abroad.

Friday 13 November was the working-day session. Locals with jobs tend to come at the weekend, so a Friday at a fair like this usually leaves more room to browse the stalls.

## Dry Bridge and the booksellers of Rustaveli Avenue

The fair is only part of a bookish weekend here. The Dry Bridge market, near the Mtkvari river, is Tbilisi's open-air flea market. Sellers lay out Soviet-era books, old maps, enamel badges and paintings on blankets and folding tables. It is at its fullest at weekends, which matched the fair's Saturday and Sunday dates.

Rustaveli Avenue is the city's main boulevard. Along it stand the Rustaveli Theatre, the Georgian National Museum and the old Parliament building. Prospero's Books, an English-language bookshop with a café in its courtyard, sits on the avenue and is a long-standing stop for readers.

Together they make an easy loop: a book fair, a walk down Rustaveli and a browse at Dry Bridge, all in a day.

## November in Tbilisi

Mid-November is late autumn here. Days are cool, evenings are colder, and grey, damp spells are common. Fair-goers dress in layers they can peel off indoors, because halls full of people and stalls warm up fast.

The shoulder season works for visitors. The summer crowds have gone from the old town and the sulphur baths in Abanotubani. By mid-November the grape harvest is over, and the new wine starts to appear in wine bars and restaurants. Visitors to a three-day fair like this often give it one full day and spend the rest of a long weekend on the city.
