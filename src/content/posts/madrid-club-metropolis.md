## Why Club Metrópolis matters

Few addresses in Madrid carry as much visual weight as the corner of Alcalá and Gran Vía, where the domed, statue-topped Edificio Metrópolis has anchored the skyline since 1911. Club Metrópolis takes that landmark and turns part of it into a working hospitality project: a boutique hotel paired with six distinct gastronomic concepts and a private members' club, all developed by Grupo Paraguas, the Madrid restaurant group behind several well-regarded dining rooms in the capital. For the group's founders, Marta Seco and Sandro Silva, this is a first foray into running a hotel rather than just restaurants, and the ambition shows in the scale of the project — six separate food and drink concepts under one roof is unusual even by Madrid's dense hospitality standards.

## Where it is and how to get there

The Edificio Metrópolis sits directly on Plaza de Cibeles-adjacent Gran Vía, at its junction with Calle de Alcalá — arguably the single most iconic intersection in Madrid, a few steps from the Banco de España roundabout and its ornate fountain. The nearest metro stop is **Banco de España (Line 2)**, with an exit that puts you almost directly beneath the building's dome. **Sevilla (Line 2)** and **Gran Vía (Lines 1 and 5)** are both within a five-to-ten-minute walk and useful alternatives if Banco de España's exits are closed for works, which happens periodically. From Puerta del Sol or Plaza Mayor, it's an easy 10–15 minute walk up Alcalá. Because the building sits at a major traffic circle, taxis and ride-shares generally drop off on Calle de Alcalá itself rather than directly on Gran Vía.

## What's inside

Rather than a single restaurant, Club Metrópolis is structured around six gastronomic concepts alongside the boutique hotel rooms and a private members' club — a format closer to a small hospitality campus than a conventional hotel restaurant. Exact names, cuisines, and pricing for each concept were still being finalized around the opening, so it's worth checking the latest details before you go rather than relying on secondhand menus. What is established is the pedigree: Grupo Paraguas has built a reputation in Madrid for polished, design-conscious dining rooms, and this project is being positioned as their most ambitious to date, using the building's landmarked architecture — ornate façades, high ceilings, and the corner dome itself — as a backdrop rather than working around it.

## The building itself

Even without booking a table, the Edificio Metrópolis rewards a slow walk-by. Designed by French architects Jules and Raymond Février and completed in 1911, it's crowned by a statue of Ariadna and a bronze phoenix, and its dome is one of the most recognizable silhouettes in the city, frequently lit for national celebrations and New Year's Eve broadcasts. Understanding that Club Metrópolis now occupies part of a protected, century-old landmark explains a lot about the project's tone: expect period detail preserved alongside contemporary design rather than a stripped-back modern build.

## When to go

Gran Vía and the Cibeles area are busy at almost any hour, but early evening — roughly 7 to 8pm, before Madrid's late dinner rush kicks in around 9:30–10pm — tends to be calmer for a first look at the building and a chance at walk-in space in any bar or lounge areas. Weekday visits avoid the heavier weekend foot traffic along Gran Vía. Given the members' club component, some areas may require an invitation, membership, or advance reservation rather than being open to casual walk-ins, so it's worth clarifying access levels for each concept beforehand.

## How to visit like a local

Madrileños treat Gran Vía as a thoroughfare to move through purposefully, not linger on — so if you want to blend in, walk with intent rather than stopping mid-pavement for photos; step into the plaza near Banco de España or across at Cibeles fountain for that. Reservations for in-demand new openings in Madrid are typically made online or by phone rather than by walking in, and given the scale of this project, booking ahead — especially for anything branded as the members' club — is the safer approach rather than assuming space will be available. Payment by card is standard across Madrid's upscale dining scene, and tipping is modest, generally rounding up or leaving 5–10% rather than the larger tips expected elsewhere. The most common visitor mistake nearby is confusing the Edificio Metrópolis with the similarly grand Círculo de Bellas Artes or Banco de España building across the intersection — all three anchor the same junction but are separate institutions, so double-check you're heading to the right entrance on Alcalá.
