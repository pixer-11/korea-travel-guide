## A triangle at the end of Elgin Street

Most visitors cross it on the way to Parliament Hill. Confederation Square is the wedge of granite and traffic where Elgin Street meets Wellington, and most people treat it as an intersection. Stand still for ten minutes and it becomes the city's ceremonial centre.

The National War Memorial rises from the middle on a tall grey granite arch. Bronze figures push through the arch from below: soldiers, nurses, airmen and a gun crew hauling a field piece. Winged figures of Peace and Freedom stand on top. The memorial's formal name is *The Response*, and King George VI unveiled it in 1939.

## The Tomb and the sentries

Set into the ground in front of the arch is the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier. It holds a Canadian soldier from the First World War whose remains were brought home from near Vimy Ridge in France. It's a low, dark sarcophagus with a bronze helmet, sword and branches on the lid. People walk right past it without looking down.

In the warmer months, from spring until Remembrance Day, Ceremonial Sentries stand guard at the Tomb during the day. They wear full dress uniform and change over on a fixed routine. Check the Canadian Armed Forces Ceremonial Guard schedule before you go if you want to see a change of sentries.

## What sits around the edges

Turn in a slow circle and the square explains how Ottawa is laid out. Planner Jacques Gréber designed it in the late 1930s to tie the government district to the canal and the east side of downtown.

- **Château Laurier**: the copper-roofed railway hotel to the northeast, beside the canal
- **Former Central Post Office**: the stone building on the north side, facing the memorial
- **National Arts Centre**: the concrete-and-glass complex along the southeast edge, on Elgin
- **Rideau Canal**: the stretch running beneath the square, with the Ottawa Locks dropping down to the Ottawa River just north
- **Sparks Street**: the pedestrian street starting a block west
- **Parliament Hill**: a few minutes west along Wellington Street

Take the stairs down toward the canal and you'll find the Valiants Memorial. It's a ring of busts and statues of Canadian military figures from across the country's history, and plenty of people who cross the square never see it.

## Manners at the memorial

This is a working memorial, and Ottawa treats it as one. A few habits will keep you on the right side of things.

- Don't sit, stand or lean on the Tomb, and keep bags and coffee cups off it.
- Give the sentries room. Don't talk to them, step into their line or pose right beside them while they're on duty.
- Keep your voice down and keep music off on the memorial platform, even when the square is quiet.
- If a wreath-laying or a ceremony starts, stand back and wait. Most locals stop walking until it's over.

On 11 November the square fills for the national Remembrance Day ceremony. Once the official part ends, a tradition takes over: people line up to pin their red poppies on the Tomb. If you're in Ottawa that week, joining that line is one of the most moving things you can do in the capital. Arrive early, because the streets around the square close.

## Getting there and getting around

The square is easy to reach and you'll almost certainly walk it. On the O-Train Line 1 (Confederation Line), Parliament and Rideau stations are both a few minutes' walk away. From Rideau station, head west along Rideau Street and cross the Plaza Bridge over the canal.

The address is 53 Elgin St, but you'll never need it. Head for the war memorial and you're there. Traffic loops right around the memorial, so cross at the signals rather than cutting across the roadway.

## Fitting it into a day

Because the square is open around the clock, it suits whatever gap your day leaves. Early morning means few people and long light on the granite. In the evening, the memorial is lit and the Château Laurier glows behind it.

A walk that works well: start at the square, go down the Ottawa Locks to the river, climb back up past the Château Laurier, and cross into the ByWard Market. Or go west along Wellington to Parliament Hill and come back along Sparks Street. Either loop takes a relaxed couple of hours, with this square as the place where the city's streets meet.
