Most people rushing between Angkor Wat and Ta Prohm never turn off for Srah Srang, and that's exactly why it's worth the detour. This is a man-made reservoir, about 700 metres long, dug in the 12th century to supply water and mark royal ceremony, not to be photographed by crowds.

You reach it via a sandstone landing terrace on the western bank, guarded by stone lions and a pair of naga serpents whose bodies form the balustrade. The steps lead straight down to the water's edge, angled so that sunrise lands directly across the reservoir in front of you. Behind you, low sugar palms and dry dipterocarp forest ring the water on three sides.

## The sunrise that isn't Angkor Wat's

Everyone with a 5am alarm in Siem Reap heads for the reflecting pools at Angkor Wat. Srah Srang gets a fraction of that crowd, even though the view across open water, framed by the naga terrace, is arguably calmer and easier to actually enjoy.

The sun comes up over the reservoir itself rather than behind a temple silhouette, so the payoff here is more about stillness and reflection on the water than a postcard shot of towers. Bring a flashlight or your phone light for the short walk from the road to the terrace; there's no lighting installed.

## Getting there and reading the site

Srah Srang sits inside the Angkor Archaeological Park, on the small road connecting Banteay Kdei and Pre Rup, east of the main Angkor Wat-Angkor Thom loop. Most visitors reach it by tuk-tuk or hired car as part of the "grand circuit," often paired with Banteay Kdei directly across the road.

There's no ticket booth here specifically; your standard Angkor Pass, checked at the park's main entrances, covers it. A visit takes 20 to 30 minutes if you're just here for the terrace and the view, longer if you sit and wait out a sunrise.

## What you're actually looking at

The reservoir was built during the reign of Rajendravarman in the 10th century and later rebuilt under Jayavarman VII in the 12th, which is the era that gives it its current stone terrace. Historians believe it served both practical irrigation purposes and ceremonial ones, possibly used for royal bathing rituals or as a staging point for barges during festivals.

Unlike the moats around Angkor Wat or Angkor Thom, Srah Srang has no temple rising from its center. What you get instead is open water, sky, and one architecturally serious embarkation point. It rewards visitors who like ruins for their engineering and atmosphere rather than carved narrative panels.

## Timing your visit around the park, not the crowds

Because Srah Srang opens at 5:00 AM and closes at 5:30 PM daily, sunrise is genuinely accessible here, not a special exception. Arriving right at opening gets you the terrace nearly empty, since most tour groups are funneled toward Angkor Wat or Sras Srang's more famous neighbors first.

Midday sun flattens the reflection and brings heat with little shade around the water, so it's a rougher window than dawn or the couple of hours before closing. Late afternoon, closer to 4 or 5pm, is a quieter second option if you can't manage the early start.

## Pairing it with Banteay Kdei

The practical move locals and drivers use is to combine the two sites in one stop. Banteay Kdei, the Buddhist monastery temple directly across the road, takes 30 to 45 minutes to explore and pairs naturally with a sunrise or late-afternoon look at Srah Srang.

- Ask your tuk-tuk driver for "Srah Srang and Banteay Kdei together," it's a standard combination on the grand circuit
- Wear sandals or shoes you don't mind getting dusty; the terrace steps and shoreline are uneven sandstone
- Skip bottled drinks vendors expecting a crowd, there's rarely one set up here, so carry your own water
- Respect the terrace itself; the naga balustrade is original stonework, not a modern replica for climbing on
