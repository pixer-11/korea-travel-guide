## The week the city turns yellow

Saigon swaps its traffic for flowers. In the days before Tet Nguyen Dan, pavements across Ho Chi Minh City fill with potted *mai vàng*, the yellow apricot blossom that southern families put in the living room for luck. Kumquat trees heavy with orange fruit get strapped to the backs of motorbikes, and red banners go up over shopfronts.

Tet is the most important holiday in Vietnam. It marks the first day of the lunar calendar, a time to clear debts, clean house, honour ancestors and start the year on the right foot. For a visitor, the city shows a side you never see the rest of the year. It is louder for a while, and then it goes oddly quiet.

## The 2027 calendar, day by day

The 2027 national holiday runs from **February 4 to February 10**. **New Year's Day (Mùng Một) is February 6**, opening the Year of the Goat, which Vietnamese call the year of *Mùi*.

The week has a shape that most families follow:

- **February 4–5:** final shopping, flower buying and travel home to the provinces. Markets are at their most crowded.
- **Night of February 5 (Giao Thừa):** New Year's Eve, with ancestor offerings and fireworks at midnight.
- **February 6:** Mùng Một, the first day. People visit close family and pagodas.
- **February 7–8:** visits to wider family, friends and teachers.
- **February 9–10:** businesses slowly reopen and the city refills.

## Nguyen Hue Flower Street and the Binh Dong boats

Two places carry the Tet feeling in this city. In recent years, **Nguyen Hue Flower Street** (Đường hoa Nguyễn Huệ) has been laid out along the pedestrian boulevard that runs from the riverfront to the colonial-era City Hall. It fills with flower installations, lanterns and large figures of the year's zodiac animal. Families in *áo dài* come to pose for photos, and in the evenings the boulevard is shoulder to shoulder.

For something older and rougher, go to the **Binh Dong wharf flower market** in District 8. There, along the Tau Hu canal, growers from the Mekong Delta moor their boats and sell straight from the deck. Locals call the arrangement *trên bến dưới thuyền*, "on the wharf, under the boat".

Mai trees, chrysanthemums and marigolds are passed hand to hand up onto the quay. Selling winds down on the eve of Tet, when growers want to get home. **Tao Dan Park**, near Ben Thanh Market, also traditionally hosts a spring flower show.

## Giao Thừa: the midnight hinge

The night of February 5 is the moment the year turns. At home, families set out a tray of fruit, *bánh tét* and incense for the ancestors. *Bánh tét* is the south's cylindrical sticky rice cake, wrapped in banana leaf.

In the city centre, the crowds gather along the Saigon River for the midnight fireworks. These are traditionally the official display of the night.

Straight after midnight, many people head to a pagoda to light incense and ask for a good year. Two of the most visited are:

- **Jade Emperor Pagoda** (Chùa Ngọc Hoàng) in Da Kao, District 1. It is a Taoist temple, dark inside, with carved wooden figures and dense coils of incense.
- **Thien Hau Temple** on Nguyen Trai Street in Cholon, District 5. This 18th-century Cantonese temple is dedicated to the sea goddess, and spiral incense cones hang from its ceiling.

The pagodas stay busy through the morning of Mùng Một. Smoke builds up in the courtyards, and visitors queue to shake fortune sticks.

## Tickets, venues and what actually closes

Tet is a holiday, not a ticketed festival. There is no single organiser, no lineup and no gate. Nguyen Hue Flower Street, the riverside fireworks, the flower markets and the pagodas are all open public spaces. The "venue" is the whole city, centred on the old District 1, District 5's Cholon and the canal banks of District 8.

What changes is what's open. Many family-run restaurants, street stalls and small shops shut from New Year's Eve for several days, and Ben Thanh Market traditionally closes on the first days of the year. Hotels, chain coffee shops, convenience stores and big malls usually keep trading.

Restaurants that stay open often add a Tet surcharge to the bill. It is common practice, and it is usually printed on the menu or the bill itself.

## Lucky money, first footsteps and other Tet manners

Tet has its own etiquette, and visitors who follow even part of it get a warm reception. The phrase to learn is *Chúc mừng năm mới*, "Happy New Year". People say it to everyone during the first days.

- **Lì xì:** adults give children and elderly relatives small red envelopes of lucky money, in crisp new notes. A guest invited into a home often brings envelopes for the kids.
- **Xông đất (first-footing):** the first visitor through the door after midnight is thought to set the family's luck for the year. Families choose that person carefully, so guests usually wait to be invited rather than turning up at a house on the morning of Mùng Một.
- **Colours:** red and yellow are lucky. Many people avoid all-black or all-white outfits, which are linked to mourning.
- **Words and actions:** arguing, sweeping out the house and talking about bad luck are avoided on the first days of the year.
- **At pagodas:** cover shoulders and knees, keep your voice low near the altars and step around people kneeling to pray.

Most street vendors and smaller places take cash only. Ride-hailing apps such as Grab handle card payments, and they are how most visitors get around during the holiday.

## Moving through a half-empty Saigon

The run-up and the holiday itself feel like two different cities. Before Tet, millions of workers leave Ho Chi Minh City for their home provinces. Train seats, coach tickets and domestic flights out of Tan Son Nhat in the days before New Year are usually the hardest to get of the whole year, and prices rise to match.

Once the exodus is over, streets that are normally jammed with motorbikes go quiet. Many visitors find the first days of the year the easiest time all year to walk District 1. Ride-hailing still works, but drivers are fewer, so waits are longer and fares higher than usual.

Many travellers end up splitting the week. They spend the eve and New Year's Day in the city for the fireworks and pagodas. Then they make a slower trip out to the Mekong Delta, where the flowers sold at Binh Dong are grown, once services pick up after the first three days.
