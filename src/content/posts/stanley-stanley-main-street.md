## A street with the sea on one side

Stanley Main Street runs along the edge of Stanley Bay. One side is the water, held back by a low seawall. The other is a row of restaurants and bars, most with tables spilling onto the pavement and facing out across the bay.

It's a short, flat walk, not a destination you spend a whole day on. What makes it worth the trip is the setting. Few places in Hong Kong let you eat this close to open water without a highway or a harbour ferry lane in the way.

The street is also quieter than you might expect for somewhere this well placed. Plenty of visitors come to Stanley for the market and leave without walking the extra few minutes to the waterfront.

## Getting to the south side

There's no MTR to Stanley, so you'll take a bus or minibus over or through the hills. That ride is half the appeal. Your main options:

- **Bus 6 or 6X** from Exchange Square in Central, climbing over Wong Nai Chung Gap and past Repulse Bay
- **Bus 260**, a faster express from Central via the Aberdeen Tunnel
- **Bus 973** from Tsim Sha Tsui, if you're staying in Kowloon
- **Green minibus 40** from Causeway Bay

For the scenic version, sit on the top deck of the 6 on the right-hand side heading south. Most routes drop you at the Stanley Village bus terminus. From there, walk downhill through the lanes of Stanley Market and you'll come out onto Main Street and the bay.

Tap an Octopus card on every bus and minibus; it saves fumbling for exact change, since drivers don't give it.

## Murray House and the Blake Pier end

Walk to the western end of the strip and the architecture changes abruptly. Murray House is a three-storey Victorian building with stone columns and wide verandas. It was built in 1846 in Central as officers' quarters.

In the 1980s it was taken apart to make way for the Bank of China Tower. Its stones were numbered and stored, then reassembled here on the Stanley waterfront. Look closely and you can still see the numbers on some blocks. Today it houses restaurants on its upper floors.

Just beyond it, a pier juts into the bay under an old iron-framed roof. That roof is the pavilion from the original Blake Pier in Central, moved here too. Stand at the end and you get the clearest view back along Main Street, with the hills of the peninsula behind.

## Eating and drinking along the strip

Most of Main Street is given over to sit-down restaurants and bars. The mix leans towards Western menus and relaxed pub-style spots, with outdoor seating that makes the bay the real draw.

Prices sit on the mid-range to pricier side for Hong Kong, largely because you're paying for the view. Menus are usually posted outside, so you can compare as you walk before committing to a table.

Expect this to be a place for a long lunch or a drink at sunset rather than a quick snack. If you want something cheaper and faster, the lanes of Stanley Market and the streets up towards the bus terminus have more casual options.

## The 9pm window on weekends

On weekends the street has a clear rhythm. From 1pm to 8pm it's at its busiest, with day-trippers moving between the market, the beach and the waterfront tables. Try not to turn up mid-afternoon on a Saturday expecting a seaside seat; that's when you're most likely to wait.

The quietest weekend hour is 9pm to 10pm. By then the tour groups and families have mostly taken the bus back to town. The water goes dark, the lights along the promenade and Murray House come on, and the tables thin out.

Keep an eye on the clock, though. The street is listed as open until 10pm, so a 9pm arrival gives you roughly an hour. Plan for a drink or dessert rather than a full dinner, and check the last bus times back to Central before you settle in.

## Taking a table by the water

A few habits will make your visit smoother.

- **Walk the whole strip first.** It takes only a few minutes, and the best outdoor seats vary by time of day.
- **Ask for outside.** Many places have indoor and terrace seating; say so when you're greeted rather than waiting to be offered.
- **Check the bill for a service charge.** Sit-down restaurants in Hong Kong commonly add 10%, so extra tipping isn't expected. Rounding up for good service is fine but optional.
- **Cards are widely accepted** at restaurants here, though an Octopus card still covers your transport and small shop purchases.
- **Book ahead on weekend afternoons** if you've set your heart on a particular terrace.

On weekdays you can generally just walk up and ask. English is widely spoken along the strip, and menus are typically bilingual.

## Beyond Main Street

Stanley rewards an extra hour or two if you have it. All of these are within walking distance:

- **Stanley Market**, the covered lanes of clothing, souvenirs and art stalls you pass on the way down
- **Tin Hau Temple**, a small shrine near Stanley Plaza, dating from the 18th century
- **Stanley Main Beach**, east of the market, where dragon boat races are held during the Tuen Ng Festival
- **Stanley Military Cemetery**, a quiet, shaded site on Wong Ma Kok Road with graves from the colonial era and World War II

A good loop is to arrive by bus, browse the market, walk Main Street to Murray House and the pier, then circle back along the waterfront. Allow two to three hours for the lot, longer if lunch turns into a long one.
