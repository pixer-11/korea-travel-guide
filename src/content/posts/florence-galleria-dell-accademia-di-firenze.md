## Why go

You come to the Galleria dell'Accademia for one reason, and that reason is five metres tall. But the museum's real trick is how it makes you wait for him. You enter through rooms of gilded Byzantine-style altarpieces and Florentine Gothic panels, and just when you've settled into a slower rhythm, the corridor opens and there he is — David, lit from above by a skylight added specifically to show him the way Michelangelo intended, seen in raking Tuscan daylight rather than torchlight. It's a staged reveal, and it works even when you know it's coming.

## What to see

Walk the Hall of the Prisoners first, even though David pulls your eye down the corridor the whole time. These four unfinished figures — the Prigioni — show Michelangelo's non-finito technique: bodies straining to emerge from raw marble blocks, chisel marks still visible, torsos twisting free while limbs remain trapped in stone. Art historians still debate whether he left them incomplete on purpose or simply moved on to other commissions; either way, they're some of the most revealing works in the building because you can watch the artist thinking. Beyond them stands the original David (the ones outdoors at Piazza della Signoria and Piazzale Michelangelo are copies), carved from a single flawed block of Carrara marble that two earlier sculptors had already rejected. Look at the tension in his right hand, the vein work, the deliberately oversized head and hands — proportions meant to read correctly from below, since the statue was originally commissioned for a cathedral buttress high above street level. Beyond David, the museum holds a strong collection of Florentine Gothic and early Renaissance panel paintings, a hall of plaster models by Lorenzo Bartolini and other 19th-century sculptors, and a lesser-visited collection of Russian icons upstairs that most tour groups skip entirely — worth ten quiet minutes if you want a break from the crowds around the sculpture halls.

## Getting there

The Accademia sits at Via Ricasoli 58/60, in the university quarter a few minutes' walk north of the Duomo — follow Via Ricasoli straight up from the cathedral and you'll hit the entrance in about eight minutes on foot. It's easily combined with Piazza San Marco (with its Fra Angelico frescoes at the Museo di San Marco) and the Duomo complex on the same morning, since all three sit within a compact walking radius. There's no dedicated metro in central Florence, so most visitors arrive on foot from wherever they're staying, or via one of the ATAF city buses that stop near Piazza San Marco.

## When to go

This is consistently one of the most-visited museums in Italy, and the queue on Via Ricasoli without a reservation can run well over an hour, especially in spring and summer. The measured crowd pattern backs this up: weekends stay busy from 10am right through to 6pm, so the calm window is early, between 8am and 9am at opening. Arriving right when the doors open is the single best move you can make here — you'll often have the Prisoners hall nearly to yourself before the tour groups arrive. Outside peak season (roughly November through February, excluding holidays), the whole museum breathes easier all day.

## How to visit like a local

Book your timed-entry ticket online well ahead — this is standard practice for anyone in Florence who isn't a first-time tourist, since walk-up tickets mean standing in a line that wraps around the block. Entry is ticketed by time slot, so show up at your slot rather than early; security and bag-check take a few minutes. Payment for tickets is almost entirely online or by card at the counter — cash-only queues are increasingly rare here. Once inside, resist the urge to plant yourself directly in front of David for ten minutes with your camera; the polite local rhythm is to circle him slowly from multiple angles, then step back to the benches at the edge of the rotunda to sit and look properly, freeing up the sightline for others. The most common visitor mistake is treating this as a ten-minute photo stop and missing the Prisoners and the painting galleries entirely — budget 60 to 90 minutes minimum. No flash photography is permitted anywhere near the sculptures, and guards enforce it.

## Nearby afterward

Once you're done, Piazza San Marco is two minutes away for a coffee stop, and the leather markets around San Lorenzo are a short walk south if you want to keep exploring on foot rather than doubling back toward the Duomo crowds.
