## An Italian table in Lyon's food hall

Lyon has a lot of serious food, and much of it sits under one roof. The Halles de Lyon Paul Bocuse is a long, low modern building on Cours Lafayette. Inside, rows of glass-fronted counters sell oysters, sausage, cheese, pastry and wine, and many of them have stools where you can eat.

Since March 31, 2026, one of those addresses has been Lello. It is the Italian newcomer in a hall known mostly for French produce, and it was being called an address to watch within weeks of opening.

Antonello Cabras, a chef already well known in Lyon, runs it. He describes his cooking as sincere and convivial, which tells you what kind of meal to expect: a relaxed meal at the market, not a tasting menu with ceremony. Lello is three businesses under one name: a restaurant, a caterer and a fine-food shop.

## Finding it inside the Halles Paul Bocuse

The Halles are at 102 Cours Lafayette in the 3rd arrondissement, on the east bank of the Rhône. They sit between the Presqu'île and the Part-Dieu business district. From outside, the building looks plain and functional, which is normal for a market built in 1971. All the interest is inside.

Getting there:

- **Metro:** Line B to Place Guichard – Bourse du Travail, then a short walk north to Cours Lafayette.
- **Train:** Lyon Part-Dieu station is roughly a ten-minute walk east, so the Halles work well before or after a TGV.
- **On foot from the Presqu'île:** cross the Rhône on the Pont Lafayette and walk straight along Cours Lafayette.

Once you're inside, walk the aisles slowly. Stalls are packed closely together, and the market's posted directory is the quickest way to find a particular counter.

## Restaurant, caterer and épicerie in one

Lello's three-in-one setup is the most useful thing to understand before you go. You don't have to come for a full sit-down meal. Because it is also a fine-food shop, you can go in just to buy Italian products and take them home or back to your hotel.

The catering side is aimed at people in Lyon planning a gathering. If you're staying in an apartment or hosting a group, ask about it directly. We weren't given a menu or a product list, so we won't guess at dishes. The counter itself shows you what's available that day.

This kind of flexibility fits the Halles well. Lyonnais come here to shop for food, and they often stay for a plate and a glass of wine. A place where you can do both suits that habit.

## Why an Italian chef in Lyon's food hall matters

The Halles carry Paul Bocuse's name. They were renamed in 2006 to honour the chef most associated with Lyonnais cuisine. For decades, eating here has meant French market classics, and Saint-Marcellin cheese and Lyonnais charcuterie are two of the products the market is best known for.

An Italian table run by a recognised local chef adds something new to that line-up. Lyon has always had close links to Italy, with the Alps and the road to Turin not far away. Lello gives you an Italian meal in a building that is otherwise strongly French.

It also helps if you're travelling in a group with mixed tastes. One person can have oysters at a seafood counter while another sits down at Lello. Afterwards, everyone can meet up at a pastry counter.

## Eating at a market counter, Lyon-style

The Halles have their own etiquette, and it's easy to pick up:

- **Greet first.** Say "Bonjour" before you ask for anything. In France, skipping the greeting comes across as rude, at a market counter just as much as at a table.
- **Ask before you sit.** Seats at market counters are limited and are often shared. Ask "C'est libre?" before taking a stool.
- **Book, or at least call.** Lello is new and already talked about, so confirm whether it takes reservations before you go. Don't count on getting a seat as a walk-in.
- **Paying.** Cards are widely accepted across French food halls. Ask "Je peux payer par carte ?" if you aren't sure.
- **Tipping.** Service is included in French bills by law. Leaving small change for good service is a nice gesture, but nobody expects it.
- **Water.** You can ask for "une carafe d'eau", which is free tap water, instead of bottled water.

Timing follows how the market works. The Halles are a shopping market first, so the lunch hours are when the counters fill up. Weekend mornings are a Lyon ritual, with families and friends standing over oysters and white wine. If you want a calmer meal, a weekday visit makes more sense than a Saturday.

## Building a Halles crawl around Lello

You'll get more out of the trip if Lello is one stop on a longer walk through the market. Before or after your meal, look for these:

- **Mère Richard:** the cheese counter best known for Saint-Marcellin.
- **Maison Sibilia:** a long-established charcuterie stall for saucisson and Lyonnais cured meats.
- **Sève:** a chocolatier and pâtissier with a counter in the hall.
- **The oyster bars:** several seafood stalls shuck oysters to order at the counter.

If you're shopping to take food away, buy the perishable things last. The Halles are a short walk from Part-Dieu station, so people often fill a bag with cheese and charcuterie before catching a train. Lello's shop counter fits easily into that routine.
