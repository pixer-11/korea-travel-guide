## Why the lean still stops you in your tracks

You've seen the photo a thousand times, so nothing quite prepares you for the tilt in person. Walk out from the narrow streets of central Pisa into the Piazza dei Miracoli and the tower seems to hang there, defying the eye, white against green lawn.

Construction started in 1173, and the ground gave way almost immediately — the tilt began before the third storey was even finished. Nearly two centuries of stop-start building later, in 1372, it stood at 56 meters at its tallest point, eight ringed storeys of white marble stacked slightly off-kilter.

## Getting there and getting oriented

The Piazza del Duomo sits at the northern edge of Pisa's old city, a 20-minute walk from Pisa Centrale train station, or a short ride on the LAM Rossa bus if you'd rather not walk it.

If you're flying in, Pisa International Airport is just 3km away — one of the closest airports to any major landmark in Europe, reachable by taxi or the PisaMover shuttle connecting to the train station.

The square itself is unmissable: alongside the tower stand the Duomo (cathedral), the Baptistery, and the Camposanto monumental cemetery, all in the same luminous white marble.

## Climbing the tower

Climbing is the whole point, and it's not just a photo op from below. You buy a timed ticket, wait for your slot, and file up 251 spiral stone steps worn smooth by centuries of footsteps.

The staircase narrows and curves oddly as you climb, your body instinctively correcting for the slant — it's disorienting in a way no photo captures. At the top, the bells (the tower is actually a campanile, the cathedral's bell tower) ring out over the rooftops of Pisa, and the view stretches to the Apuan Alps on a clear day.

- Tickets are sold for specific entry times, often days or weeks ahead in high season
- Children under 8 aren't permitted to climb, for safety reasons
- Bags and backpacks generally need to be checked before you go up
- The climb takes about 30 minutes round trip, plus queueing time

## Beyond the tower

Don't rush off once you're back on solid ground. The Duomo's interior has a striped marble nave and a pulpit carved by Giovanni Pisano that's easy to miss if you're moving fast.

The Baptistery, the largest in Italy, has an echo that the on-site guides demonstrate every so often — worth timing your visit around if you can. Combined tickets covering the tower, Duomo, Baptistery, and Camposanto are usually the better value if you plan to see more than one.

Outside the piazza's walls, the souvenir stalls thin out fast; a few blocks south toward the Arno River, Pisa feels like an ordinary Tuscan university town again.

## How to visit like a local

Pisa's Leaning Tower is one of the most visited landmarks in Italy, so this isn't a quiet find — the trick is timing, not stealth.

- **Book ahead:** Tickets are timed and do sell out, especially climb slots in summer. Reserve online rather than risk the on-site ticket office.
- **Avoid the crowds:** Weekends between 11am and 5pm draw the heaviest foot traffic in the piazza. Arrive right at 9am opening, or come in the last couple of hours before the 8pm close, for noticeably thinner crowds.
- **Pay smart:** Card payment is standard at the official ticket booths; avoid touts near the piazza entrance selling "skip-the-line" tickets at inflated prices.
- **The classic tourist mistake:** Spending your whole visit staring up from the "forced perspective" photo spot on the lawn. Fun for one shot, but the real experience is inside, climbing the stairs and standing on the tilted top gallery.
- **Etiquette:** The Duomo requires modest dress — covered shoulders and knees — same as most Italian cathedrals.

Give the whole piazza at least two hours: roughly 45 minutes for the tower climb and queue, and the rest for the Duomo and Baptistery next door.
