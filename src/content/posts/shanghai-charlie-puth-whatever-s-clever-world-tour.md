## Buying and Getting In with Your Passport

Ticketing in mainland China works differently from Europe or North America. For large concerts, real-name ticketing is the norm. Each ticket is tied to one buyer's identity document, and foreign visitors normally use their passport.

At the gate, staff usually check that the name on the ticket matches the ID in your hand. A ticket bought by a friend under their name may not get you in. Resale tickets with someone else's details can fail at exactly that point.

How it tends to work for visitors:

- **Book under your own passport number,** spelled exactly as it is on the passport.
- **Carry the physical passport** on show night. A photo of it is not the same thing.
- **Arrive early for security.** Big Chinese venues screen bags. Power banks, umbrellas, lighters and professional cameras are common restrictions, so read the venue rules before you pack.
- **Pay with a mobile wallet.** Alipay and WeChat Pay are how Shanghai pays for nearly everything, from metro rides to bottled water. Both let you link a foreign bank card, so set them up before you fly.

Chinese crowds at pop concerts often sing every chorus and wave light sticks. The heavy filming you see at Western shows happens here too. Staff may still step in if you hold a phone up through an entire song, so follow the rules for that venue.

## Moving Across Shanghai on Show Night

Shanghai is huge, and the metro is the sensible way to cross it. The network is one of the largest in the world, signs and announcements are in both Chinese and English, and the trains are clean and frequent.

You can tap through the gates with Alipay or WeChat Pay using the Metro QR code feature. That means no queue at a ticket machine as the crowd leaves.

Once you know the venue, work out two things:

- **The nearest station and line.** Note the exit number too, because big stations have many.
- **Your last train.** Metro service ends late in the evening and lines finish at different times. If the encore runs long, book a ride with DiDi, China's main ride-hailing app, which takes foreign cards and has an English interface.

Expect a surge of people at the closest station straight after the show. Walking one stop further before you get on is often quicker than queuing at the gate.

## Arriving for the Final Date

Most international visitors land at one of two airports. Shanghai Pudong International (PVG) handles long-haul flights. Hongqiao (SHA) mainly serves domestic routes and sits beside Hongqiao Railway Station, the terminal for high-speed trains from Beijing, Hangzhou and Nanjing.

From Pudong, the Shanghai Maglev runs to Longyang Road, where it connects with Metro Lines 2 and 7. Metro Line 2 also reaches Pudong directly, more slowly. Hongqiao is on Lines 2 and 10.

Entry rules for China, including visa-free and transit schemes, change from time to time. Check the current requirements for your passport with the Chinese embassy or consulate before you book. Hotels register foreign guests' passports at check-in, which is routine.

## A November Evening Around the Show

Early November is autumn in Shanghai. Days are generally mild and nights cooler, with the city's thick humidity mostly gone. Pack a light layer for queuing outdoors and the walk back to the metro.

If you fly in a day early, the classic first evening is the Bund. That's the riverside promenade of early-20th-century banks and trading houses on the Puxi bank of the Huangpu River. Across the water, the Lujiazui skyline stacks up the Oriental Pearl Tower, Shanghai Tower and the Shanghai World Financial Center.

Nanjing Road East, the long pedestrian shopping street, runs inland from the Bund towards People's Square. For a slower afternoon, the plane-tree streets of the former French Concession around Wukang Road and Anfu Road are good for walking. Both areas are a few metro stops apart, so they fit easily around a single concert night.
