## A concrete fortress on the lake

The building looks like it means business. Colin Madigan's gallery opened in 1982, and it is a hard-edged mass of bush-hammered concrete. The surface was chipped by hand until the aggregate shows through, so up close the walls feel coarse, like stone.

Inside, the rooms turn on triangles. Ceilings angle overhead, coffered and heavy, and floors step up and down between galleries. Some visitors call it Brutalist and leave it there. Spend an hour inside and you start to see how the geometry pushes you from room to room.

It is one of Canberra's most visited cultural sites. The general collection is free to enter, and major touring exhibitions are ticketed. Current prices for those are on the official website.

## Two hundred hollow logs

Start where most people do, with *The Aboriginal Memorial*. It is a forest of 200 painted hollow log coffins. They were made by artists from Ramingining and surrounding communities in central Arnhem Land and completed for 1988, the bicentenary of British colonisation.

Each pole stands for a year of occupation since 1788. You walk between them on a winding path, so the work surrounds you rather than sitting in front of you. The surfaces carry clan designs in ochre: cross-hatching, animals and bands of colour.

Give it longer than you expect. The First Nations collection around it is substantial, with:

- bark paintings from Arnhem Land
- Western Desert acrylic dot paintings
- Torres Strait Islander works
- contemporary pieces by living artists

## Blue Poles and the international rooms

The best-known painting in the building is Jackson Pollock's *Blue Poles*. The federal government bought it in 1973, and the purchase caused a national row at the time. Today it hangs as a tangle of enamel and aluminium paint, crossed by eight dark vertical bars.

The international rooms also include one of Monet's *Waterlilies* canvases, along with modern American and European work. Back in the Australian galleries, look for Sidney Nolan's *Ned Kelly* series. Kelly's black, box-shaped helmet keeps turning up across sunlit bush landscapes.

The collection rotates, so check the website if one particular work is the reason you're coming.

## Out the back: fog, sky and sculpture

The Sculpture Garden runs from the building down towards Lake Burley Griffin. Paths wind through native planting, past large bronzes and steel pieces. You can walk it before or after your gallery visit.

Two works here are unique to this site:

- **Fujiko Nakaya's fog sculpture** releases artificial mist over a marshy pond at scheduled times. Ask at the information desk when the next release is.
- **James Turrell's *Within without***, a Skyspace. You walk through a turquoise-walled pool and up a ramp into a domed chamber with an open oculus. Sit on the stone bench and the sky becomes a framed, flat field of colour.

Both depend on the weather. The Skyspace works best on a clear day.

## Getting to Parkes Place

The gallery is at Parkes Place East, Parkes, inside the Parliamentary Triangle. That puts it between Parliament House and the lake. Canberra has no metro, and the light rail runs north of the lake. Most visitors come by car, by bus or on foot.

From Civic, the city centre, it is a flat walk south across the Commonwealth Avenue Bridge, roughly 30–40 minutes. The lakeside paths are pleasant in autumn, when the trees around the lake turn red and gold.

There are visitor car parks around the Triangle, and some are paid. Signs at the car park entry show the current rates. Transport Canberra buses stop nearby; check the journey planner for current routes.

## Beating the weekend crush

The gallery is currently open 10am–5pm daily. Museum hours shift with holidays and special events, so confirm on the official site for the week you visit.

Typical crowd patterns are clear. Weekends between 12pm and 4pm are the busiest stretch, when school-holiday families and touring exhibition crowds overlap. The calmest hour is 4pm–5pm, weekdays and weekends alike. That hour is a good time for one wing, not the whole building.

A practical plan:

- Arrive at 10am opening for ticketed exhibitions. Timed entry sells out on weekends, so book online ahead.
- Spend the middle of the day in the Sculpture Garden or across at the Portrait Gallery.
- Come back at 4pm to see *Blue Poles* or the Memorial with fewer people around.

Bags larger than a small backpack usually go to the cloakroom. Card payment is standard in the shop and café. Photography without flash is generally allowed in the collection galleries, but signs often restrict it in loan exhibitions. Free guided tours run from the foyer; times are posted at the information desk.

## Around the Parliamentary Triangle

You can easily fill a day without moving the car. These are all within a few minutes' walk:

- **High Court of Australia**: right next door, and in the same concrete style.
- **National Portrait Gallery**: just across the lawn.
- **Questacon**, the national science centre: a short stroll along the lakefront.
- **Old Parliament House**: up the axis towards Capital Hill, home to the Museum of Australian Democracy.
- **Parliament House**: further up again.

A full visit to the gallery itself takes two to three hours, more if you add an exhibition. Leave time at the end for the lake edge below the Sculpture Garden. From there you can see the Captain Cook Memorial Jet across the water and the Carillon on Aspen Island.
