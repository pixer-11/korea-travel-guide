## What actually happens over the three days

Bon Om Touk marks the moment the Tonlé Sap River reverses course, flowing back toward the Mekong after the wet season. Phnom Penh's riverfront, the stretch facing the Royal Palace and Wat Phnom side of the Tonlé Sap and Mekong confluence, becomes the stage.

Daytime is dragon boat racing: narrow wooden longboats, paddled by teams of dozens, sprint the river in heats over all three days. Crews come from provinces across Cambodia, so the racing has a genuine national-competition feel, not a staged show.

After sunset the mood shifts. Illuminated boats, rigged with lights in the shape of naga serpents and other national symbols, drift along the river. Fireworks and moon-worship ceremonies close out each night, tied to the lunar calendar that fixes the festival's dates each year.

## Where to stand along the riverfront

The riverside promenade between the Royal Palace and Wat Phnom gives the clearest sightlines to both the race course and the evening boat processions. This strip is public space, with no gate and no ticket.

Crowds are usually dense here for good reason: this is where Cambodian families from across the country come to watch, making it a shared local event rather than a tourist show. The promenade typically fills early on the final evening, when fireworks close the festival.

## Schedules, closures and hotels

Dates, exact race schedules and road closures around the riverfront are set by Cambodian authorities, and the finer details shift year to year. Street closures around the palace and riverfront typically reroute traffic for the full three days.

Phnom Penh hotels near the riverfront (Sisowath Quay area) usually fill up for the festival weekend, since the neighbourhood is within walking distance of the action and avoids the traffic.

## Getting around a closed-off riverfront

Sisowath Quay and the surrounding streets close to vehicle traffic for the festival, so people usually arrive on foot or by moto/tuk-tuk dropped a few streets back.

- Walking in is easier than driving to the riverfront directly
- Spots for the illuminated boat procession usually go well before dusk
- Street food stalls along the promenade deal in cash, mostly small bills
- This is the densest crowd Phnom Penh sees all year, so pickpocketing is a known risk
- Landmarks such as Wat Phnom and the Royal Palace gate serve as common regrouping points for groups

Locals treat the festival as a homecoming weekend, and many travel back from the provinces just for it, so buses and riverside guesthouses book out fast. Vendors sell grilled and roasted foods streetside; no reservation system exists for riverfront viewing spots, which are first-come public ground.
