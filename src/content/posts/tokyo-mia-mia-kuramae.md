## A rice mill that now pours lattes

The building was working long before the coffee arrived. For about 120 years it was a rice milling shop. Today it holds the second location of Mia Mia, a café with a strong following as a community hangout.

During the day the counter turns out lattes known for their art, the kind you photograph before the first sip. In the evening the room becomes a bar. You get one address with two moods, and that's why it's worth planning around.

## Why Kuramae suits it

The name explains the fit. Kuramae means "in front of the storehouses." It refers to the shogunate's rice granaries that lined the Sumida River here in the Edo period. A café in an old rice miller's shop is about as on-theme as this district gets.

Modern Kuramae has a reputation as Tokyo's small-makers quarter. Low-rise streets mix workshops and wholesalers with independent shops selling leather goods, stationery and ceramics. It's quieter than Asakusa next door, and people come to browse slowly rather than tick off sights.

## Getting to Kuramae

Kuramae Station is the stop. It's served by two subway lines:

- **Toei Asakusa Line**: direct from Asakusa (one stop), Nihombashi and Higashi-Ginza
- **Toei Oedo Line**: handy from Shinjuku, Roppongi and Ryogoku

The two lines have separate entrances a short walk apart, so check which exit you need before you surface. You can also walk from Asakusa along the Sumida River in about 15 to 20 minutes. Look for the address on a map app first, because the side streets are easy to overshoot.

## Day cup or night glass

Choose your visit by mood. Come in daylight if the latte art is the point. It's the café's best-known draw, and you'll get good light for photos. One drink and a sit-down takes 30 to 45 minutes. Add the neighbourhood and you've got a comfortable half-day.

Come after dark and it works as an easy first stop for the evening, before dinner or a riverside walk. We weren't given a menu or bar list, so we won't guess what's poured. What matters is the switch from daytime café to bar. Confirm when that changeover happens, since new venues often adjust their hours in the first months.

## Manners for a neighbourhood café

Mia Mia built its name as a community café, and Kuramae is a residential, working district. A little restraint goes a long way.

- **Check hours and bookings first.** It's a new spot, so look at its official social accounts before you go, especially for the evening bar.
- **Don't tip.** Japan has no tipping culture, and leaving coins on the table usually just creates an awkward chase.
- **Ask about payment.** Plenty of Tokyo cafés take cards and IC cards like Suica, but not all do. Carrying some yen avoids the issue.
- **Photograph your cup, not the people.** Ask before you shoot other customers or the staff behind the counter.
- **Keep your voice down outside.** The neighbours live here, and that matters most at night.

A simple "sumimasen" gets attention, "kore o kudasai" ("this one, please") works while pointing at a menu, and "gochisōsama deshita" on the way out is always appreciated.

## Making an afternoon of it

Mia Mia Kuramae fits easily into a walk. A few nearby stops:

- **Kakimori**, the stationery shop known for custom-assembled notebooks and its fountain-pen ink bar
- **Dandelion Chocolate Factory & Café Kuramae**, a bean-to-bar chocolate maker a few blocks away
- **The Sumida River promenade**, with views of Tokyo Skytree across the water
- **Senso-ji in Asakusa**, one subway stop or a short walk north

A good loop: browse the craft shops in the late morning, stop for a latte, then walk the river to Asakusa. If you'd rather see the bar side, go the other way. Do Asakusa first, then cross back to Kuramae as the café switches over for the evening.
