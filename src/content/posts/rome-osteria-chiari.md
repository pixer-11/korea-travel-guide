## Dinner Cooked Over Flame

Some Roman meals are built in a pan. This one starts with fire.

Osteria Chiari works *alla brace*, the Italian term for cooking over embers and open flame. The ingredients are Roman, and so is the attitude: an osteria is the city's plain, unfussy tier of restaurant, meant for eating well rather than for ceremony. What sets this one apart is how directly the fire shapes the plate, and the smoky edge it leaves on the room.

It is a cozy place, and it is new. That pairing matters. You are getting a young kitchen working in an old idiom, in a district that has spent the last few decades turning warehouses and gasworks into somewhere Romans actually go out.

## Ostiense After Dark

Ostiense is not the Rome on the postcards. It runs south from the Aurelian Walls at Porta San Paolo, where the white marble Pyramid of Cestius stands beside the gate. From there Via Ostiense heads toward the sea, past railway lines, former industrial yards and big walls of street art.

The skyline marker is the Gazometro, the iron skeleton of an old gas holder that you can see from much of the district. Nearby, the Centrale Montemartini puts classical statues inside a decommissioned power station, with marble gods standing among turbines and diesel engines. It is one of the more unusual museum rooms in Rome.

Garbatella, the garden-suburb of courtyards and early twentieth-century housing blocks, sits just to the east. The area as a whole leans toward evenings: restaurants, bars and late plates rather than monuments. An osteria cooking over fire fits that character neatly.

## Reading the Fire

Fire cooking rewards a little understanding. Embers give intense, uneven heat, and an *alla brace* kitchen uses that to char the outside of whatever it cooks while keeping the inside juicy. The flavour you notice first is smoke, and it reaches more than the meat.

Roman tradition has a long grilling vocabulary. The best-known example is *abbacchio a scottadito*, milk-fed lamb chops grilled so hot they are said to "burn the fingers" as you eat them. Whether that or anything else is on the board at Osteria Chiari is for the menu to tell you. We have not been given it, so we will not guess.

What you can do is order with the fire in mind:

- Ask what is coming off the grill that day; fire kitchens often work from what is good at the market.
- Ask how the kitchen recommends a cut is cooked rather than imposing your usual preference.
- Leave room for a vegetable side, since charred greens and vegetables are part of the same tradition.
- Order a red to stand up to the smoke, or ask the staff to suggest one.

## Booking a Table and Ordering

Small, recently opened places in Rome fill fast, and cozy means few tables. Reserve before you go, and check current opening days and hours directly with the restaurant. We were not given a schedule, so treat any time you read elsewhere as something to confirm.

Romans eat late. Dinner usually begins from around 8pm, and a table at 7pm can mean a half-empty room. If you want the full atmosphere, book later; if you want a quieter meal, book early.

A few habits make the evening smoother:

- **Water:** you will be asked *naturale o frizzante?* (still or sparkling).
- **The bill:** it will not arrive until you ask. Say *il conto, per favore*.
- **Coperto:** many Roman restaurants add a small per-person cover charge. It is normal and not a tip.
- **Tipping:** not expected the way it is in North America. Rounding up or leaving a little for good service is enough.
- **Pace:** nobody will rush you off the table. An osteria dinner is meant to run long.

## Getting to Ostiense

Metro Line B is your easiest route. It runs through the district with stops at Piramide, Garbatella and Basilica San Paolo, so check the address and choose the closest. Piramide also connects with Roma Ostiense railway station and the Roma–Lido train toward the coast.

From central Rome, Line B from Termini or Colosseo gets you to Piramide in a few stops. Taxis are simple at night too, and worth it if you are coming back after the metro has stopped running.

## Building an Evening Around It

Arrive in daylight and make a loop of it. Start at the Pyramid of Cestius and walk into the Non-Catholic Cemetery beside it, where Keats and Shelley are buried under cypress trees. It is one of the quietest corners in the city.

Then follow Via Ostiense south, watching the building walls for murals. Plan museum visits around their own hours: the Centrale Montemartini and the Basilica of San Paolo fuori le Mura, one of Rome's four papal basilicas, both close well before dinner.

After eating, Ostiense and neighbouring Testaccio, across the tracks by Piramide, have plenty of bars for a last drink. Testaccio is also the historic home of Roman offal cooking, should one evening of old-school food make you want another.

## Before You Go

This is a new restaurant, so information moves. Confirm three things ahead of your visit:

- Opening days and hours
- Whether a reservation is needed for your date
- The exact street address, so you choose the right metro stop

Then come hungry, sit back and let the fire set the pace.
