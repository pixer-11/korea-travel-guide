## Eleven days in the old town

There is no single front door. Bangkok Design Week takes place from February 18 to 28, 2027, and calls itself Southeast Asia's biggest design festival. The scale supports the claim: more than 400 installations, exhibitions and markets, spread through the city's old-town districts instead of a convention hall.

That changes how the festival feels on foot. Work usually fills shophouse ground floors, old riverside godowns, small galleries and courtyards. You walk between them on uneven pavements, past noodle carts and motorbike repair shops that keep trading the whole time.

## Where the programme spreads

The Creative Economy Agency (CEA), the Thai government body behind the event, publishes the dates, district map and programme on its official site. Earlier editions clustered around Charoen Krung Road, Bangkok's first paved road, and the riverside Chinese quarter of Talat Noi.

Past programmes also reached into Yaowarat, the city's Chinatown, and the older lanes toward Rattanakosin. Expect the 2027 edition to follow the same logic of neighbourhood clusters. Each cluster takes a few hours to walk properly, so most visitors treat each one as a separate outing.

## Getting into the districts

The old town sits away from the main BTS Skytrain lines. Most people arrive by a mix of MRT, river boat and short taxi hops:

- **MRT Blue Line:** Hua Lamphong for the Talat Noi edge, Wat Mangkon for Yaowarat, Sam Yot for the older royal quarter.
- **BTS Silom Line:** Saphan Taksin station, then the river boats from Sathorn pier just below it.
- **Chao Phraya Express Boat:** Si Phraya pier lands you on Charoen Krung's northern stretch, Ratchawong at the edge of Yaowarat.
- **Short hops:** Grab cars, or motorbike taxis with drivers in numbered orange vests.

Distances between venues are short, but the lanes are narrow and car traffic crawls. Walking is usually faster than a taxi once you are inside a district.

## Tickets, payment and the free programme

Most of Bangkok Design Week needs no ticket. You walk into an exhibition, look around and walk out. The free programme is the bulk of the festival, which is why so many people treat it as a series of long evening walks.

A smaller share of talks and workshops can involve registration or a fee. The organiser's listings note which ones. Market stalls are where money changes hands:

- Thai QR payment (PromptPay) is everywhere at small stalls.
- Cash in small notes covers anyone without a Thai banking app.
- Foreign cards work mainly in cafés and shops, less often at pop-up stalls.

## Walking the lanes as a guest

People live in these streets. In Talat Noi especially, festival venues sit next to family shrines, open kitchens and homes with doors left ajar. Visitors who keep their voices down and step aside for scooters fit in without effort.

A few habits help:

- Ask before photographing residents or the inside of a home.
- Take shoes off where a venue has a shrine room or a raised wooden floor.
- Cover shoulders and knees when a programme stop is inside a temple compound.
- Throw rubbish in shop bins; public bins are scarce in the old town.

February falls in Bangkok's dry season, with afternoons often in the low-to-mid 30s °C. Many attendees split their days for that reason. They see indoor exhibitions in the afternoon, then stay for outdoor and light-based works once it is dark, which earlier editions leaned into heavily.

## Shaping your festival days

Nobody sees all 400 works. Most visitors pick one district per session and spend two or three evenings, or a full weekend, on the festival. Chasing a highlights list across the city burns more time in traffic than it saves.

The neighbourhoods make easy add-ons. Talat Noi holds So Heng Tai, a Hokkien courtyard mansion around two centuries old. Charoen Krung has River City Bangkok, a riverside mall of art galleries and antique dealers. After dark, Yaowarat Road's street-food stalls are a short walk from the Chinatown venues, and plenty of visitors end the night there.
