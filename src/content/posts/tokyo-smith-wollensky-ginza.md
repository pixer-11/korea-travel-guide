## Why go

You already know the name from Manhattan's Third Avenue, but this is the first time it's crossed the Pacific. Ginza gives it a fitting stage: marble, brass, dark wood, the low hum of a room that takes itself seriously without being stiff.

Three floors stack the experience — a street-level bar, a main dining room, and private space above. The draw is simple: USDA Prime beef, dry-aged in house, served the way New York has always done it. A wine cellar holding more than 2,000 bottles backs it up, and by evening a jazz trio or pianist usually threads through the bar downstairs.

## Getting there

Ginza is Tokyo's grid of luxury flagships, and this steakhouse sits right in that fabric. The district is served by Ginza Station (Tokyo Metro Ginza, Marunouchi, and Hibiya lines) and Higashi-Ginza Station on the Asakusa and Toei Oedo lines.

From either station it's a short walk through blocks lined with department stores and international boutiques. If you're coming from a hotel in Marunouchi or Shimbashi, it's an easy taxi ride too — useful if you're dressed up for dinner and don't want to navigate stairs and turnstiles.

## What to expect inside

Walk in and the bar hits you first: bottles stacked to the ceiling, low light, the clink of glassware over conversation. This is where the jazz plays, and it's worth arriving early enough to grab a stool here before your table is ready.

Upstairs, the dining room leans classic steakhouse — white tablecloths, a visible energy from the open kitchen, the smell of char and butter drifting between tables. The dry-aged Prime cuts are the reason people book weeks out, and the wine list is deep enough that the sommelier's recommendation is often worth taking over your own instinct.

## When to go

Evenings are when this place comes alive — that's when the jazz starts and the room fills with a mix of business dinners, celebrations, and visitors ticking off Ginza's dining scene. A trending, newly landed international name like this draws steady demand, so weekend nights book up fastest.

If you want a calmer read on the room — to actually hear the music and see the open kitchen without a crowd at your elbow — an early weeknight seating is your best bet. Ask when booking whether a bar seat or early table is available; it's often the easiest way in without a long wait.

## How to visit like a local

Reserve ahead rather than walking in — this is a marquee opening in a neighborhood full of destination diners, and tables move fast on weekend evenings. Call or book online days in advance if you can.

- Dress smart-casual to formal — Ginza dining runs polished, and this room matches that register.
- Arrive a little early and post up at the bar rather than waiting at the host stand; it's the best seat for the jazz.
- Let the sommelier guide your pairing given the size of the cellar — it's part of what sets the place apart.
- Credit cards are standard practice across Ginza's restaurant scene; carrying some cash is still a sensible backup in Japan generally.
- Don't rush the meal — a steakhouse dinner here is built as an evening, not a quick stop between shopping.

The mistake most first-timers make is treating it as a quick bite before more Ginza browsing. Treat it instead as the evening's main event, and confirm your booking and the current hours directly with the restaurant before you head out.
