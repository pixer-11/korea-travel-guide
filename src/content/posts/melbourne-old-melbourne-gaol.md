## Bluestone, Iron and Three Tiers of Cells

The walls are the colour of wet slate. Old Melbourne Gaol is built from bluestone, the dark volcanic basalt behind much of colonial Melbourne. Inside the surviving cell block it feels heavier still. Three levels of narrow cells face each other across a tall central hall, linked by iron walkways and stairs.

Most doors are low and thick. Most cells are barely wider than your outstretched arms. Look up from the ground floor and you see the whole design at once: one open space where a single warder could watch every landing. Here the architecture explains how the place worked better than any panel does.

This was Victoria's main prison from the mid-1800s until it closed in 1924. Over those decades 133 people were executed inside its walls. Today it is a museum run by the National Trust of Australia (Victoria), with self-guided and guided tours.

## Ned Kelly's Last Morning

Most visitors come for one name. Bushranger Ned Kelly was hanged here on 11 November 1880, and the gaol has never been allowed to forget it. The cell block holds the gallows area. Kelly-related displays sit alongside the cells, so the story is told in the building where it ended.

Take your time with the death masks. In the nineteenth century, plaster casts were often taken of executed prisoners' heads, partly for the pseudo-science of phrenology. A row of these pale faces lines the cells, each labelled with the person's crime and story.

They are unsettling to look at. They also show you a side of colonial justice that textbooks tend to skip. Several of the stories belong to women, and many belong to people with no fame at all, which hits harder than the Kelly legend.

## Getting Arrested Next Door

Your visit doesn't stop at the cell block. Next door is the City Watch House, Melbourne's police lock-up, which held people until 1994. Today it is part of the experience. Visitors are "charged" and put through a role-played booking in the real cells, which still carry the marks of their late-twentieth-century occupants.

It is loud, brisk and a little theatrical. Kids and teenagers tend to remember it longer than the Victorian displays. It also links the two eras: you go from gallows and plaster masks to a police lock-up that was in use within living memory.

## Beating the Weekend Midday Rush

The crowd pattern here is easy to plan around. On weekdays the calmest hour is 4pm–5pm. On weekends you have two quiet windows: 10am–11am, right at opening, and 3pm–5pm.

Avoid weekends between 12pm and 3pm. That is when the narrow walkways fill up and you end up queuing to look into a cell. The landings are tight, so a crowd changes the feel of the place more than it would in a gallery.

The catch is that 4pm–5pm is the last hour before closing. On a weekday, arrive in the middle of the afternoon so your final, unhurried hour falls in the quiet window. Plan on roughly two hours to see the cell block and do the Watch House properly.

The gaol is currently open 10am–5pm every day of this week. Hours can shift through the year and around holidays, so check the National Trust's official site before you go.

## Reaching Russell Street

The gaol is on the CBD's northern edge, close to the corner of Russell and La Trobe Streets.

- **Train:** Melbourne Central station on the City Loop is about two blocks west. Leave toward La Trobe Street and walk east.
- **Tram:** trams along La Trobe Street, including the free City Circle tourist tram, stop near Russell Street. The CBD sits inside Melbourne's Free Tram Zone.
- **On foot:** State Library Victoria on Swanston Street is a short walk away, so the two pair easily.

Driving into this part of the city is rarely worth it. Public transport drops you closer than most car parks will.

## How to Walk the Tiers

A few habits will improve your visit.

- **Go up before you go in.** Climb to the top landing first and look down the full length of the hall. Once you understand the layout, each cell makes more sense.
- **Read the cell panels slowly.** The good material is in the individual life stories, not the headline displays.
- **Pick your format at the door.** Self-guided suits people who like to linger. A guided tour adds context that the panels can't fit.
- **Mind the stairs.** The original iron stairways are steep and narrow. Hold the rails, and keep bags small so you can turn on the landings.
- **Pay ahead or at the desk.** Prices are on the National Trust's site and at the entrance. Australian venues take card readily, so you won't need cash.

The National Trust also runs evening tours on some nights, including ghost-themed ones. They are separate from daytime entry, so book them through the official site rather than expecting to join one on arrival.

Be aware that this is a place of real deaths. People do joke in the Watch House, and that's part of it, but most people go quiet near the gallows and the masks. It helps to do the same.

## The Block Around the Gaol

The gaol is part of a cluster of justice buildings. The grand former Magistrates' Court stands beside it and now belongs to RMIT University, whose city campus spreads across the surrounding blocks. That is why the footpaths outside are often busy with students rather than tour groups.

Walk west along La Trobe Street and you reach State Library Victoria, with its domed La Trobe Reading Room. The library also displays Ned Kelly's armour, a natural next stop after seeing where he died. Melbourne Central's shops and food courts are next to the library, handy if the tour runs past lunchtime.

For more of the city's past, the Queen Victoria Market is a short tram ride or walk northwest. Combined with the gaol and the library, it makes a full day in the northern CBD without needing a car.
