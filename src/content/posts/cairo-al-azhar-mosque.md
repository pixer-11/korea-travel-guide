## Marble, minarets and a thousand years of study

You step through a stone gateway and the street noise stops. In front of you is a wide courtyard of pale marble, framed by arcades of pointed arches. You cross it in socks, because your shoes are back at the gate.

Al-Azhar was commissioned in 970 by the Fatimid dynasty, the rulers who founded Cairo itself. It was Cairo's first mosque. It grew into one of the oldest continuously running centres of Islamic learning anywhere, and Al-Azhar University still carries its name.

It remains a working mosque. Students sit with books in the prayer hall, and people come in to pray between the tourists. Treat it as a place of worship that happens to take visitors.

## What to look for inside

Every dynasty that ruled Cairo added something, so the building reads like layers of rock. Look for these:

- **The courtyard (sahn):** the original Fatimid core, edged with keel-shaped arches and carved stucco.
- **The minarets:** several rise above the roofline. The twin-headed one built for Sultan al-Ghuri in the early 1500s is the easiest to pick out.
- **The Barber's Gate (Bab al-Muzayinin):** the main visitor entrance, named for the barbers who once shaved students' heads here.
- **The prayer hall:** a forest of columns under a flat ceiling, carpeted and dim after the bright courtyard.

Stand in the far corner of the courtyard and look back toward the gate. From there you see the minarets of different centuries side by side.

## Reaching it through Islamic Cairo

The mosque sits on the south side of Al-Azhar Street, facing Al-Hussein Mosque and the lanes of Khan el-Khalili. Most visitors combine all three on one walk.

The nearest metro stop is Bab El Shaaria on Line 3. From there it's a walk of about 15 minutes east through busy streets. A taxi or ride-hailing app will drop you at Al-Hussein Square, which is simpler if you're coming from Zamalek or Downtown.

Al-Azhar Street carries heavy traffic. Cross at the marked pedestrian points near Al-Hussein rather than weaving between cars.

## Planning around the listed hours

Google currently shows a very narrow schedule: Friday only, 11:00 AM – 7:00 PM, with every other day marked closed. Mosque visiting hours in Cairo change through the year and around Ramadan and religious holidays. Treat this week's listing as a warning, not a permanent rule, and confirm before you go.

If Friday is your day, remember that Friday's midday congregational prayer is the most important of the week. Non-Muslim visitors are normally asked to wait until it ends. Aim for the afternoon, comfortably inside the 7:00 PM close. In late afternoon the courtyard marble cools and the light on the arcades turns softer.

## Getting through the Barber's Gate properly

This is one of Cairo's most-visited monuments, so a few habits make it go smoothly.

- **Dress:** shoulders and knees covered for everyone. Women should bring a scarf for their hair. Wraps are sometimes handed out at the entrance, but don't count on it.
- **Shoes:** you hand them to an attendant or carry them in a bag. A small tip to the shoe attendant is customary. Have small Egyptian banknotes ready.
- **Guides:** men at the gate may offer to show you round. Agree whether it's a paid tour before you start, or politely decline.
- **Photos:** photography in the courtyard is generally fine. Don't photograph people praying, and keep voices low in the prayer hall.
- **Space:** don't walk directly in front of anyone at prayer, and keep to the edges of the carpeted area.

Any entry rules or charges for visitors are posted at the gate. Ask there rather than trusting whoever approaches you outside.

## Before and after your visit

You're in the densest stretch of medieval architecture in Cairo, so plan an hour or two either side.

- **Khan el-Khalili:** directly across the street, for brass lanterns, spice stalls and the coffeehouses along its alleys.
- **Al-Hussein Mosque:** facing the square, one of the city's most revered shrines.
- **Wikala and Mausoleum of al-Ghuri:** just west along Al-Azhar Street, where Tanoura dervish performances are held on some evenings.
- **Al-Muizz Street:** a short walk north, lined with Mamluk mosques, madrasas and sabils.
- **Al-Azhar Park:** to the east, with gardens and views back over the minarets of the old city.

Doing Khan el-Khalili first and then crossing to the mosque in the afternoon fits neatly around Friday prayers.
