Out on the end of Victoria Quay, a long, angular building juts toward the harbour mouth. Its roofline is meant to suggest sails and upturned hulls. Container cranes work one side of the water and Rottnest ferries leave from the other. This is the WA Maritime Museum, and the setting tells you what it's about: Western Australia faces the Indian Ocean and has always looked outward from here.

It's one of Fremantle's best-known attractions and is consistently well rated. Don't expect a quiet secret. Expect a big, open waterfront museum with a real submarine moored beside it.

## The yacht that broke a 132-year streak

Australia II is the reason many people come. In 1983 this 12-metre yacht beat the New York Yacht Club and ended the longest winning streak in sport. The America's Cup then came to Fremantle for the 1987 defence, which reshaped the town's waterfront.

The boat's secret weapon was the winged keel designed by Ben Lexcen, kept hidden under covers at the time. At the museum you can walk around the hull and see it. Even if you don't sail, it's worth seeing the shape that caused so much argument.

Around it, the galleries cover the parts of WA history that came by sea:

- Indian Ocean trade routes and the early Dutch encounters with this coast
- Fishing, pearling and naval history
- Migration, with the stories of people who arrived through this port
- Small craft and the state's long relationship with surf, boats and the river

## Down the hatch on HMAS Ovens

The submarine is the other half of the visit. HMAS Ovens is an Oberon-class submarine that served with the Royal Australian Navy during the Cold War. It now sits on a slipway beside the museum, and you can only go aboard on a guided tour.

Inside, it gets very narrow. You climb through round hatches between compartments and pass bunks squeezed in beside torpedo tubes. The guides explain how a crew lived for weeks without daylight.

Before you book, know this:

- The tour involves ladders, low openings and tight spaces
- It is not suitable for anyone with limited mobility or claustrophobia
- Tours run at set times and are capped in size, so check the schedule on arrival
- Tickets are separate from general museum entry; current prices are on the official site

## Getting to Victoria Quay

Getting there is easy without a car. Take the Fremantle line from Perth station to its end at Fremantle; the ride is a little over half an hour. From the station, walk west along Victoria Quay past the old cargo sheds. It's a flat walk of about 10–15 minutes.

If you're driving, follow Peter Hughes Drive onto the quay. There's parking near the museum. Bikes can come along the river path from Perth, though the last stretch is exposed to the wind.

## Timing around the Fremantle Doctor

Fremantle's weather follows a pattern. On summer afternoons a strong south-westerly sea breeze, known as the Fremantle Doctor, usually blows in. It cools the town and makes the end of the quay very windy.

That makes the museum a good afternoon plan in hot months. Do your beach or outdoor walking in the still morning. Then go inside when the breeze arrives. On a clear day, the museum's big harbour-facing windows show the ships moving in and out of the port.

At the moment the museum opens 9:30am–5pm every day of the week. Hours can change on public holidays and through the year, so check the official site before you go. The last submarine tour leaves well before closing, so don't turn up late in the afternoon if Ovens is the point of your trip.

## Reading the Welcome Walls

The Welcome Walls are outside the museum, facing the harbour. They are panels engraved with the names of migrants who came to Western Australia by sea, many of them through this port. Families add names, so the walls keep growing.

Locals often bring visiting relatives here to find a grandparent's name before they go in. You can do it in five minutes, and it explains the migration galleries better than any label. If your own family came to WA by ship, ask at the front desk how to search the records.

## Doing the museum without the bottlenecks

Most of the planning is about the submarine tour. Here's how to make the day run smoothly:

- **Book Ovens first.** When you arrive, get the next tour slot that suits you, then explore the galleries until your time comes. Tours fill up on school holidays and weekends.
- **Arrive at opening.** Getting there right at 9:30am usually means you're ahead of school groups and families coming off the Rottnest ferries.
- **Pay by card.** Like most of Australia, the museum is set up for card and tap-to-pay at the counter.
- **Dress for the sub.** Wear flat, closed shoes, and don't bring big bags if you can help it. Hatches and ladders are hard with your hands full.
- **No tipping.** Guides are staff, and tipping isn't expected in Australia.
- **Ask the volunteers.** Many gallery volunteers have a background in sailing or the navy and are happy to talk about the boats.

## Beyond the quay

The museum is the first stop on a good walk around Fremantle's West End. Within about 15 minutes on foot you'll reach:

- **The Shipwreck Galleries** on Cliff Street, a separate WA Museum site with the timbers of the Dutch ship Batavia, wrecked in 1629
- **The Round House**, the oldest surviving public building in WA, on the headland above Bathers Beach
- **Bathers Beach** and the **Fishing Boat Harbour**, where locals go for fish and chips at sunset
- **Fremantle Prison**, a UNESCO World Heritage site, a little uphill on the east side of town

If you're going to Rottnest Island, the ferries leave from Victoria Quay near the museum. A morning in the galleries and an afternoon crossing make a good day. Back in town, the cappuccino strip on South Terrace is the natural place for coffee.
