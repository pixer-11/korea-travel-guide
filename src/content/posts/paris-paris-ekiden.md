## A marathon split between teammates

The Paris Ekiden takes place on Sunday, November 1, 2026. It covers the full marathon distance of 42.195 km. Instead of one runner going the whole way, a team splits it into legs and hands over at exchange points along a route past the city's landmarks.

The format comes from Japan, where the first ekiden ran from Kyoto to Tokyo in 1917. There the baton is a cloth sash called the *tasuki*, worn across the chest and passed on at each changeover. The classic international road-relay version uses six legs: 5 km, 10 km, 5 km, 10 km, 5 km and 7.195 km.

## Entries, route and the holiday date

For an ekiden, a "ticket" means a team entry. The organiser publishes entry terms, team size, leg lengths and the exact route on its official site. This guide gives none of those details, because none were supplied to us.

Watching usually costs nothing. Spectators line the barriers on public streets, the way they do at other Paris road races.

The date matters for a few reasons:

- **Toussaint:** 1 November is All Saints' Day, a French public holiday. In 2026 it falls on a Sunday, so many shops are closed.
- **Clocks:** France turns its clocks back on 25 October 2026, so sunrise comes at roughly 8am.
- **Weather:** early-November days in Paris are usually cool, typically around 10°C, often grey and sometimes wet.

## Watching from the barriers

The exchange zones are where an ekiden is most worth watching. The incoming runner arrives, the next one waits in the box and the handover happens in seconds. Spectators usually cluster at these points rather than spreading out along the straights.

Here's how race-day Paris tends to work:

- **Cheering:** French crowds shout *"Allez !"* followed by a name or team. Calling out a team's name at a changeover gets noticed.
- **Getting around:** streets on the course close to traffic. Spectators usually move between viewing points by Métro or RER, which run on a Sunday timetable.
- **Transport tickets:** station machines take bank cards, and the RATP app sells digital tickets.
- **Cafés:** contactless card payment is close to universal. Service is included in the bill (*service compris*), so leaving small change is a courtesy, not an obligation.
- **Clothing:** standing still at a barrier on a November morning feels colder than running. Regular spectators bring a layer and something for drizzle.

## Rounding out race day

Toussaint has its own customs. Parisian families visit cemeteries with pots of chrysanthemums, the traditional flower of the day. Florists' stalls by cemetery gates are stacked with them.

Père-Lachaise in the 20th arrondissement draws more visitors than on an ordinary Sunday. Its cobbled lanes and family tombs make a quiet contrast to the barriers and finish-line noise. Montparnasse Cemetery in the 14th and Montmartre Cemetery in the 18th follow the same tradition.

Since it's a Sunday and a holiday together, sit-down lunch spots fill up and many shops stay shut. Bakeries and cafés make up most of what's open in residential streets. The open-air markets and the Seine quays are where many people spend the afternoon after the last runner finishes.
