## A white needle between the traffic

Traffic surrounds it on every side. Avenida 9 de Julio runs past in wide bands of cars, buses and the red Metrobus lanes. Avenida Corrientes cuts across, and Diagonal Norte comes in at an angle, so the Obelisk sits on a traffic island where three major routes meet.

The shaft is plain and pale, about 67 metres tall, with a small window near the pointed tip. It has no statue, no fountain and no ornament. That severity is the whole design, and it is why the monument still reads clearly from several blocks away down any of those avenues.

## What the stone remembers

It opened in 1936. The city commissioned it for the 400th anniversary of Pedro de Mendoza's first founding of Buenos Aires in 1536. The modernist architect Alberto Prebisch designed it, and it was built at remarkable speed, in a matter of weeks, which is still a point of local pride.

The facing is Córdoba white stone, from the province in the country's interior. Walk all the way around the base and you will find inscriptions on its sides. Each one marks a founding moment:

- Mendoza's first founding of the city in 1536
- Juan de Garay's second, lasting founding in 1580
- The first raising of the Argentine flag in the city, in 1812
- The federalisation of Buenos Aires as the national capital in 1880

The flag inscription is there for a reason. The church of San Nicolás de Bari stood on this spot until it was demolished to widen the avenues, and that is where the flag was first flown in Buenos Aires. The monument is a memorial to a building that no longer exists.

## Getting onto the island

Go by subte. Three lines meet in an interchange right under the plaza:

- **Line B**: Carlos Pellegrini station, along Corrientes
- **Line C**: Diagonal Norte station
- **Line D**: 9 de Julio station

The stations connect underground, so it is one of the easiest transfer points in the city. You will need a SUBE card, which is the same card used on city buses and the Metrobus. The Metrobus has its own stops on the 9 de Julio median, close by.

Crossing 9 de Julio on foot usually takes more than one light cycle. Locals wait on the medians between sections and don't sprint the full width. Do the same, even when the next signal looks close.

## When the city pours onto 9 de Julio

Most days the Obelisk is a backdrop. On a few days it becomes the centre of the country. When Argentina wins something big in football, crowds head here to sing, climb lampposts and fill the avenue. The scenes after the 2022 World Cup final were broadcast around the world. Political marches and protests often converge here too.

If you are in town for a major match, decide in advance whether you want to be in it. The crowd is joyful, dense and loud, and the streets around it close to traffic. Keep phones and wallets in front pockets either way, because pickpockets work these gatherings.

The monument is lit after dark. The city sometimes changes the lighting or puts something on the shaft to mark a cause or a date, so it can look different from one visit to the next.

## Meeting "en el Obelisco"

Porteños say "nos vemos en el Obelisco" (see you at the Obelisk) when they need a meeting point everyone can find. That's how most residents use it: as a fixed point, not a sight. A few habits make your visit easier:

- **Name a corner.** "At the Obelisk" covers a big plaza. Agree on a side, such as the Corrientes side facing the theatres.
- **Take photos from the medians or sidewalks**, not the road. The best straight-on shot looks along Corrientes or down 9 de Julio, and you get it from the crosswalk islands while the light is red.
- **Don't plan on going inside.** The interior is normally closed to the public, and the narrow access inside the shaft is not a regular attraction.
- **Watch your belongings.** It is one of the most visited spots in the city, it draws a constant crowd of tourists, and bags left open on a bench are an easy target.

There is no queue, no ticket and no gate. You can come at any hour. Early morning gives you the cleanest photos with the fewest people in the frame, though the traffic never stops.

## Pizza and marquees on Corrientes

The block-by-block appeal is on Avenida Corrientes. This stretch is the city's theatre strip, with big marquees for revues, comedies and musicals. Theatres like the Gran Rex and the Ópera are within a short walk of the Obelisk.

Corrientes is also where Buenos Aires-style pizza lives. The old pizzerias along the avenue, Güerrín among the best known, serve thick, cheese-heavy pies. Order fugazzeta, an onion-topped pizza stuffed with cheese, and look for fainá, a chickpea flatbread that locals lay on top of a slice.

Many regulars eat standing at the counter ("de parado"), which is quicker than waiting for a table. At sit-down service in Argentina, a tip of around 10% is customary. Check the menu board for current prices before you order.

## Building a walk around it

The Obelisk sits at the centre of a good half-day on foot:

- **Teatro Colón**, the city's grand opera house, is a few blocks north along 9 de Julio. Guided tours of the interior run on a schedule posted on the theatre's official site.
- **Diagonal Norte** (Avenida Presidente Roque Sáenz Peña) runs southeast from the plaza in a straight line to **Plaza de Mayo** and the Casa Rosada.
- **Avenida de Mayo**, just off that route, has old cafés including Café Tortoni.
- **Calle Florida**, the pedestrian shopping street, is a short walk east.

A sensible order is to see the Obelisk first, walk Diagonal Norte to Plaza de Mayo, then loop back along Avenida de Mayo. At night, come back to Corrientes for a show and a slice. The monument is at its most striking after dark, lit up above the traffic and the theatre signs.
