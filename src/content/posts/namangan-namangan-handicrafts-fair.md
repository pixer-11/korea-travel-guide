## Four crafts, made where you can watch

The pull here is the making. The Namangan Handicrafts Fair puts four of Uzbekistan's oldest trades side by side: ceramics, silk, embroidery and woodcarving. Artisans demonstrate their work live, so you see the process as well as the finished piece.

That matters in the Fergana Valley. Several of the country's craft towns lie within a few hours' drive of Namangan, and a lot of what Central Asia sells as "Uzbek" handicraft starts in this valley. A fair in Namangan puts that work in front of you without a week of driving between workshops.

## Reading the stalls

Each craft has its own giveaways. A little background helps you see why one piece costs more than another, even though no prices appear here.

- **Ceramics:** The Fergana Valley's best-known pottery comes from Rishtan, south of Namangan. Its signature is a turquoise and cobalt glaze traditionally made with *ishkor*, an alkaline glaze based on plant ash. Look at the brushwork on plates and bowls: hand-painted lines are slightly uneven, while transfer prints are perfectly uniform.
- **Silk:** Margilan, also in the valley, is the centre of ikat weaving. *Atlas* is all silk. *Adras* combines a silk warp with a cotton weft. Ikat is dyed before weaving, so the pattern edges blur softly. If they look crisp, the pattern was printed.
- **Embroidery:** *Suzani* are the large embroidered hangings and bedspreads of Uzbek homes. They are covered in big floral and sun-like medallions. On a hand-stitched piece, the back shows knots and stray threads. On a machine piece, the back looks almost as neat as the front.
- **Woodcarving:** Uzbek carvers work in deep geometric and floral relief, using the same vocabulary as the carved columns of old mosques and *aivan* verandas. Smaller pieces include boxes, folding Quran stands (*lauh*) and cutting boards.

Watching a demonstration is the quickest way to learn this. Ten minutes beside a carver or an embroiderer shows you where the hours go.

## Chust caps and knives: Namangan's own crafts

Namangan Region has two crafts of its own, and both come from Chust, a district town west of the city. Look for them before anything else.

The first is the **Chust *doppi***. It is a square black skullcap embroidered in white with four stylised peppers (*qalampir*), one on each side. Across Uzbekistan it reads as the classic men's cap, and many people consider the Chust version the original.

The second is the **Chust *pichoq***, a knife with a curved blade, often with a decorated handle, sold in a leather sheath. Chust knife-makers are famous across the region. Pack one in your checked luggage for the flight home, never in your carry-on.

## Buying from the maker

At Uzbek craft stalls, the price is usually a starting point. These are the things visitors tend to find useful:

- **Pay in som.** The Uzbek som is the currency, and cash is the norm at stalls and bazaars. Cards work at hotels and larger shops in the cities, but less reliably at a trestle table.
- **Bargain warmly, never hard.** Haggling is expected over larger pieces like suzani and ceramics. It goes better with a smile and a few Uzbek words. *Assalomu alaykum* is the greeting and *rahmat* means thank you.
- **Ask before photographing.** Most artisans are happy to be filmed mid-demonstration. Asking first is polite, and it often starts a conversation.
- **Ask who made it.** Many sellers are the makers, or related to them. A question about technique often gets you a demonstration, and sometimes an invitation to the workshop.
- **Get the paperwork for older pieces.** Uzbekistan restricts the export of antiques and cultural property. Sellers of vintage suzani or old carvings can usually provide a certificate. New work is generally not a problem.

On the hosting side, tea (*choy*) gets offered a lot in the Fergana Valley. Accepting a bowl while you look is normal and commits you to nothing.

## Reaching Namangan

There are three ways in from Tashkent.

**By air.** Namangan Airport takes domestic flights from Tashkent. It is the fastest option, and it skips the mountains entirely.

**By train.** Since 2016 a railway has crossed the Kamchik Pass, through the Qamchiq Tunnel, linking Tashkent with the Fergana Valley without passing through Tajikistan. Trains to Namangan are popular with visitors for the mountain scenery on the way.

**By road.** Shared taxis and private drivers take the A373 highway over the same pass. The road climbs high and can turn wintry in November, so many travellers prefer the train at this time of year.

Within the city, Yandex Go is the usual way to hail a taxi, and fares show in the app before you ride.

## Around the fair: Namangan in late autumn

A fair visit fits easily into a day or two in the city. Several stops pair well with the craft theme.

- **Mullah Kyrgyz Madrasa** (Mulla Qirg'iz madrasasi): an early 20th-century madrasa with brick and tile decoration. It shows the ornament you will see at the fair, used on a building.
- **Khoja Amin Kabri Mausoleum:** an 18th-century mausoleum faced in glazed tilework. It is a good place to compare old ceramics with the new pieces at the stalls.
- **Babur Park** (Bobur bog'i): the city's central park, best known as the home of Namangan's spring flower festival.
- **Chorsu Bazaar:** the city's main market, for dried fruit, nuts, bread and everyday crafts at street level.

To go further, many visitors spend extra days on a valley circuit through Margilan's silk workshops, Rishtan's potteries and the khan's palace in Kokand. Each town shows one of the fair's crafts where it actually comes from.
