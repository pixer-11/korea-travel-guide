## A Busan band on a Phnom Penh stage

One date, one city, one room. On December 12, 2026, Say Sue Me plays Wayfarer State motodop in Phnom Penh. Their sound is guitar pop from Busan, the Korean port city.

Expect jangly, reverb-soaked guitars over a steady, unhurried beat. Choi Sumi's vocals sit somewhere between deadpan and wistful. The songs lean on surf-rock twang and sixties girl-group melody, and they suit a warm Cambodian night better than you might expect.

## Who Say Sue Me are

The band formed in Busan in the early 2010s. Their music is tied to the city's coastline, and in particular to the Gwangalli beach neighbourhood. They are one of the few Korean indie acts to build a real following overseas, helped by a UK record deal with the independent label Damnably.

The catalogue is short enough to learn before the show:

- **We've Sobered Up** (2014), the debut, rough and sunny
- **Where We Were Together** (2018), the record that carried them abroad, with "Old Town" and "Let It Begin"
- **The Last Thing Left** (2022), a fuller, more polished album

Their lyrics are mostly in English. That makes a Phnom Penh crowd of Cambodians, expats and travellers easier to win over, and it explains why the singalongs tend to get loud.

## The "motodop" in the venue's name

The venue's name contains a piece of Phnom Penh slang. A *motodop* is a motorbike taxi: the driver rides, you sit on the back. Before ride-hailing apps arrived, motodops waiting on corners were how most of the city got around, and you still see them parked at intersections today.

That word in the name is a fair hint about how most of the audience arrives. Phnom Penh is a two-wheeled city, and gig crowds come the same way. Plenty of people ride their own scooters. Others take app-booked motorbikes, three-wheeled rickshaws or tuk-tuks, which are called *remorks* locally.

Ride-hailing apps are the simplest option for a visitor:

- **Grab** and **PassApp** both show the fare before you confirm
- Rickshaw (bajaj-style) rides are cheap and fine for short trips across town
- App motorbikes are fastest in traffic, and riders usually carry a spare helmet

For the ride home, most people book a car or rickshaw from the venue door through the same apps rather than looking for a driver out on the street.

## December nights in Phnom Penh

December is one of the best months for a show here. It falls in Cambodia's cool dry season, which usually runs from November to February. Rain is rare. The evenings are noticeably less sticky than they are in April or during the wet season.

"Cool" is relative, though. Nights stay warm, and a packed room still gets hot, so concert crowds wear light clothes. December is also peak travel season. Guesthouses along the riverfront and in the BKK1 neighbourhood fill up faster than they do at other times of year.

## Paying, riding and singing along

A few local habits make the night easier. Cambodia runs on two currencies at once. US dollars and Cambodian riel both change hands, and small change usually comes back in riel. Bars and venues in Phnom Penh often prefer cash. Many also take KHQR, the national QR payment system, if you have a local banking app.

Tipping isn't expected in Cambodia, but it is appreciated. Bar staff at expat-leaning venues are used to it. With app rides, the fare is settled in the app or in cash on arrival, and you don't haggle.

At the show itself:

- Indie gigs here are friendly and informal, and the crowd usually mingles near the stage
- Merch tables at touring indie shows tend to be cash-first
- Phone filming is common, but holding a phone up through a whole song blocks the short fans behind you
- If you rode a scooter, park it with the attendant if there is one. They usually hand you a numbered ticket.

## Around the show

Phnom Penh is a city worth a few days on either side of a gig. In the daytime, the Royal Palace and the Silver Pagoda sit on the riverfront by Sisowath Quay. The National Museum of Cambodia is next door, behind its terracotta-red Khmer facade.

The Art Deco dome of Phsar Thmei, the Central Market, is a short ride inland. The Tuol Sleng Genocide Museum and the Choeung Ek memorial take up a sober half day or more. Many visitors keep them for a separate day.

For food before the show, Bassac Lane and the streets around BKK1 are packed with small bars and restaurants. After a late finish, the riverfront promenade along Sisowath Quay is the usual place to walk off the encore.
