Every October, eleven wooden floats the height of a two-story house roll out of their storehouses in Takayama's old town and take over the streets around Sakurayama Hachimangu Shrine. This is Hachiman Matsuri, the autumn half of Takayama's twin festivals, and it draws crowds who come specifically for the floats, not the shrine itself, though the two are inseparable here.

## What the festival actually is

Hachiman Matsuri is the fall festival of Sakurayama Hachimangu Shrine, held every October 9 and 10. Its spring counterpart, Sanno Matsuri in April, belongs to a different shrine on the other side of the river, so the two festivals are not repeats of each other.

The floats, called yatai, are lacquered, carved, and hung with metalwork and textiles that some families have maintained for centuries. A few carry mechanical marionettes, karakuri, that perform on top during the procession, worked by puppeteers hidden inside using strings and levers.

Nine or eleven floats typically appear depending on the year and weather, since some stay under cover if rain threatens. Which floats parade can shift close to the date, so the exact 2026 lineup depended on conditions over the two days.

## Dates and daily rhythm

The 2026 festival was set for October 9 and 10. Day one centered on the shrine grounds, where floats assemble, with illuminated floats and lanterns planned for an evening procession.

Day two was the main daytime parade, with the full float procession moving through the old town streets accompanied by musicians, a portable shrine (mikoshi), and attendants in traditional dress. Both days usually draw large crowds, and viewing spots along the barricades fill well before the floats arrive.

- Day 1 (Oct 9): floats gather at the shrine, evening illumination and karakuri performances
- Day 2 (Oct 10): daytime float procession through the old town, mikoshi parade

Exact start times shift slightly year to year and depend on weather contingency plans, so the hour-by-hour timing varies from one edition to the next.

## Finding your spot without a ticket

There's no admission ticket for watching the procession from the street; it's a public festival and the crowds line the route for free. What costs money is entry to see certain floats up close in exhibition halls, and reserved seating if the city sets any up for the parade route.

- Best free viewing: Ojin-dori and the streets fronting Sakurayama Hachimangu Shrine
- Spectators typically settle in 60-90 minutes before a scheduled procession start for a clear sightline
- The shrine approach gets densest right before the evening floats light up on day one

Paid reserved viewing, offered by the city or shrine in some years, has varied by year and does not exist every time.

## Karakuri: the puppet show inside the parade

This is the detail that separates Takayama's festival from a simple float parade. Certain yatai carry karakuri ningyo, mechanical dolls that flip, dance, or transform mid-performance, operated by puppeteers pulling as many as thirty strings from inside the float's frame.

The karakuri performances happen at set points during the festival, usually when a float pauses in front of the shrine or at a designated stop along the route. They last only a few minutes each, and the best view is from the front of the crowd at a performance stop rather than mid-procession, where floats are simply moving past.

## Sakurayama Hachimangu Shrine and the old town setting

The festival's anchor point is Sakurayama Hachimangu Shrine, on the northern edge of Takayama's preserved old town district. The shrine itself is a short uphill walk from the merchant streets of Kami-ninomachi and Sanmachi Suji, where Edo-period wooden storefronts now house sake breweries, miso shops, and craft stores.

Outside festival days, the shrine also houses the Takayama Yatai Kaikan, an exhibition hall displaying several yatai up close year-round on rotation. The hall offers a close look at the float carving and karakuri mechanisms at leisure, since during the actual parade they're moving and roped off.

## Getting to Takayama

Takayama sits in the Gifu mountains, reached by JR limited express train from Nagoya (around 2 hours) or Toyama (around 1.5 hours), or by highway bus from Tokyo, Kyoto, and Osaka. From Takayama Station, the old town and shrine are a 15-20 minute walk, and festival crowds make taxis slow on parade days.

- Nearest station: JR Takayama Station, on the Takayama Main Line
- Walk from station to shrine: roughly 20 minutes through the old town
