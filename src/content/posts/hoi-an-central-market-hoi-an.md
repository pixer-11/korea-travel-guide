Cross the river from the old town's lantern-strung lanes and you land in An Hội, a strip of Hội An that most day-trippers skip because the postcard shot is on the other bank. Central Market Hội An sits right here, at 49 Nguyễn Phúc Tần, close enough to the water that you can hear the boat engines idling while you shop.

Don't confuse it with the big roofed produce market on Trần Phú, the one every guidebook photographs with pyramids of dragon fruit. This is a smaller, calmer operation, run more like a store than a sprawling wet market, and that distinction is exactly why it's worth the ten-minute walk across the bridge.

## Why this one and not the famous one

The main Hội An market gets the tour buses and the parked-up bicycles by 9am. This place doesn't.

With an under-the-radar reputation and a near-perfect local rating built from under 400 reviews, it's the kind of stop that hasn't been folded into every itinerary yet. That won't last forever, but for now you can walk the aisles without someone's selfie stick in your ear.

## What's actually on the shelves

Expect the standard backbone of a Hội An market stall: dried spices, conical hats, silk scarves, lacquerware, and the usual run of coffee bags and candied ginger aimed at visitors who want something packable. Look for:

- Vietnamese dried spices and chili mixes sold by weight
- Silk and linen scarves, often cheaper here than the tailor shops in the old town
- Small lacquerware bowls and chopsticks
- Vacuum-packed Vietnamese coffee, useful since it survives the flight home

Nothing here is going to be a museum piece. It's a practical stop for gifts and souvenirs, not a destination for rare antiques.

## The bridge-side rhythm

What makes this market distinct isn't the goods, it's the location. Nguyễn Phúc Tần runs along the Thu Bồn river, and the market sits close enough to the Cẩm Nam bridge that you can shop, then walk two minutes to river's edge and watch basket boats and tour skiffs pass.

Locals and market vendors here treat the river as part of the working day, not scenery. Deliveries come by boat as often as by road, so don't be surprised if the aisle you're in gets briefly blocked by someone hauling in crates from the water side.

## Getting the timing right

The market runs 9am to 9pm, seven days a week, no closed day to plan around. That's a wider window than most produce markets in Vietnam, which often wind down by early afternoon.

Because it keeps evening hours, it works well as a stop after dinner in the old town, once the lantern crowds have peaked and you want something less frantic. Arriving close to opening, before the old town's dinner rush spills across the bridge, tends to mean more room to browse and more attention from whoever's minding the stall.

## Bargaining without the performance

Vietnamese markets run on a light, unhurried version of haggling, and this one is no exception. The starting price is rarely the real price, but the back-and-forth here tends to be shorter and friendlier than at the bigger tourist market, since fewer visitors pass through and stallholders aren't running the same script forty times a day.

A few things that help:

- Ask the price, then counter with something reasonable rather than insultingly low
- Cash is the norm; small notes make the negotiation smoother on both sides
- A smile and a bit of patience goes further than a hard bargaining stance
- If a price feels fair, it's fine to just pay it. Not every stall expects a fight

Nobody here is going to chase you down the aisle. If you walk away from a price, that's usually the end of it, not a set-up for a lower offer.

## Pairing it with the rest of An Hội

Because this market sits on the An Hội side, it makes sense to treat it as one stop in a loop rather than a special trip. The neighbourhood has its own scattering of small cafés and guesthouses, quieter than the old town proper, and the riverside path gives you a walking route back toward the Japanese Covered Bridge without retracing your steps.

If you're staying on this side of the river already, this market can realistically replace a trip to the busier one, especially for scarves, spices, and packaged gifts rather than fresh produce.

## Before you go

A few practical notes worth knowing ahead of time:

- The market has no listed weekly closure, so you can build it into any day of your trip
- It's smaller than Hội An's main market, so budget less time, maybe 20 to 30 minutes rather than a full morning
- Reviews are limited in number but strongly positive, suggesting a small, satisfied crowd rather than mass tourist traffic
- Bring small denomination Vietnamese đồng notes, since bargaining works more smoothly with them

Treat it as a supporting stop on a riverside walk rather than a headline attraction, and it earns its place.
