## Why Comiket matters

Comiket — short for Comic Market — isn't a trade show with booths rented by publishers. It's a fan convention built almost entirely by the fans themselves: tens of thousands of individual "circles" (self-publishing creators) renting tiny table spaces to sell doujinshi, fan comics, original art books, and merchandise they made themselves. Twice a year it takes over Tokyo Big Sight, the angular, upside-down-pyramid convention center on the waterfront in Odaiba, and turns it into the densest concentration of otaku culture on the planet. Comiket 108, scheduled for August 14–16, 2026, will be no different in scale — expect hundreds of thousands of attendees across the run, cosplayers filling the plaza outside for photos, and lines that start forming well before the doors open.

## Getting there

Tokyo Big Sight sits on the Odaiba waterfront, reachable most directly by the Yurikamome automated line to Kokusai-Tenjijo-Seimon Station, which drops you almost at the front door. The Rinkai Line's Kokusai-Tenjijo Station is the other common approach, about a 7-minute walk. On peak Comiket days, both stations get overwhelmed, and station staff often run one-way crowd-control flows to keep people moving — follow the signage and staff instructions rather than fighting the crowd. Some attendees instead walk over from Tokyo Teleport Station or take a water bus across Tokyo Bay for a slower, less congested approach.

## What actually happens inside

The event splits across the East and West halls, with specific genres, franchises, and circle groups assigned to specific days — meaning the crowd (and what's for sale) can shift noticeably between the 14th, 15th, and 16th. Inside, you'll find long rows of tables staffed by the creators themselves, an atmosphere closer to an art fair than a corporate expo. Alongside the doujinshi circles there's usually a corporate/industry zone where publishers, game studios, and merchandise companies run their own booths with more polished displays. Outside, the plaza and surrounding walkways become an open-air cosplay stage — bring a camera, and expect that photographing cosplayers comes with an unwritten rule of asking permission first.

## Food, heat, and stamina

August in Tokyo means high heat and high humidity, and Comiket crowds moving in and out of a concrete convention center make it worse. On-site food options exist but the queues for them can be long; many veterans bring their own water and snacks and eat in the shaded areas around the venue rather than lose an hour of hall time to a food line. Big Sight's air conditioning helps once you're inside, but the walk from the station and the wait outside before doors open are the parts that catch first-timers off guard.

## When to go

If your goal is manageable crowds, mornings early in the run tend to be calmer than the afternoon rush, and the very first wave at open is when lines are longest but movement inside is still relatively free. Afternoons compress fast as day-trippers and after-work arrivals stack up. Because each day features different circle lineups, "best day" really depends on what you're there to find — check the day-by-day genre/circle assignments on the official site before picking which day(s) to attend.

## How to visit like a local

Regular attendees arrive with a plan, not just curiosity: they've checked the official Comiket catalog or app ahead of time to map which circles they want and in which hall, since wandering blind wastes hours in a venue this size. Cash is still the safest bet for doujinshi circles — many are individual creators without card readers, though larger corporate booths increasingly take electronic payment. Bring a large bag or fold-out tote, because circle purchases add up fast, and pack it flat on the way in. The biggest mistake newcomers make is underestimating the heat and the walking — treat it like a full-day outdoor festival, not an indoor expo, and pace your hall time accordingly. Queue etiquette is taken seriously: lines for popular circles form early and snake around barriers, so once you're in one, stay in it rather than stepping out and back. Above all, treat this as a living, fast-moving event — dates, hours, entry procedures, and hall maps are finalized by the organizers only in the weeks before each Comiket, so confirm everything on the official Comiket website before you travel.
