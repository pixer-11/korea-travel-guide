Botanic Park has no flowerbeds to speak of. It's grass and giant trees, nothing else. Massive Moreton Bay figs spread their limbs low over the lawn, their grey buttress roots fanning out across the ground like poured concrete. The shade underneath is deep enough that the grass thins out under the canopy.

This is the open, unfenced-feeling neighbour of the Adelaide Botanic Garden. The garden next door is for looking at plants. The park is for sitting under them.

## Where the lawn sits in the city

The park fills the corner between the Botanic Garden and Adelaide Zoo. Its address is Plane Tree Drive, the avenue of old plane trees that runs past the zoo entrance. The River Torrens runs just to the north, so you can walk from the lawn down to the riverside path in a few minutes.

You can easily fit it into a day on North Terrace. The Art Gallery of South Australia, the South Australian Museum and the State Library all line the same boulevard, a short walk west. That makes the park a natural place to rest after a morning of galleries.

## Getting to Plane Tree Drive

You won't need a car. Here's how most people get there:

- **Tram:** the North Terrace line runs east to the Botanic Garden end of the terrace. From there it's a short walk through or around the garden.
- **On foot:** from Rundle Mall, head east along North Terrace and turn up Frome Road. It's an easy, flat walk.
- **Free city bus:** the Adelaide Connector loops past the East End and North Terrace.
- **By car:** parking near the zoo and garden fills fast on weekends. Check signs for time limits.

Coming from the zoo? Walk out the gate onto Plane Tree Drive and the park is right there.

## Under the Moreton Bay figs

The figs are the reason to come. They were planted well over a century ago, and their trunks have grown into folded, ridged columns several people wide. The branches reach out sideways rather than up. That's why the shade they cast covers so much more ground than you'd expect.

Pick your spot with care. Moreton Bay figs drop small fruit, which stains picnic rugs and attracts birds. Spreading out just past the edge of the canopy gets you dappled light without the mess. Climbing the roots is tempting, but they're living trees, and the park asks visitors to treat them gently.

The rest of the park is open grass, broken up by other mature trees. There's plenty of room for a frisbee, a cricket bat or a long nap. Paths link it to the Botanic Garden, so a wander can turn into an hour among the glasshouses and themed beds next door.

## When the park becomes a festival ground

For most of the year, Botanic Park is a quiet lawn. For a few days each March, it's one of Australia's best-known festival sites. WOMADelaide, the world music and arts festival, sets up stages among the figs. Its lit-up trees and multicultural line-up are a big part of the Adelaide Festival season.

Over summer evenings, outdoor cinema seasons often screen films on the lawn, and audiences bring beanbags and picnics. The park also hosts concerts from time to time.

During these events, parts of the park are fenced off and need a ticket. Before you plan a quiet daytime visit in summer or early autumn, check the event calendar. Setup and pack-down can close areas for days on either side.

## Picnicking on the Botanic Park lawn

The park works on a few simple habits. Use them and your visit will go smoothly:

- **Bring your own food.** You won't find a food court on the lawn. Most people pack lunch, or pick up supplies from the city before walking over.
- **Take a rug or low chairs.** The grass is kept for sitting on, and the shade moves through the day.
- **Check the signs before opening alcohol.** Rules can differ across Adelaide's parklands and change during events.
- **Take your rubbish out.** Bins fill quickly on busy weekends, and the birds will find anything you leave behind.
- **Leave room for others.** On crowded weekend afternoons, people expect you not to fence off a whole stretch of shade.

Timing makes the biggest difference. On weekdays, the park stays quiet right through from 8am to 5pm, so you can take your pick of the shade. Weekends are a different story. Try not to arrive after 11am on a Saturday or Sunday, because from then until 5pm the best spots under the figs are taken. If a weekend is your only option, come at opening and aim for 9am to 10am.

## Hours and how long to stay

The park keeps set hours:

- **Monday to Friday:** 7:30am to 5:30pm
- **Saturday and Sunday:** 9:00am to 5:30pm

You can't make an early start on weekends. If you're planning a dawn walk or a pre-work coffee under the trees, go on a weekday. Event nights have separate access arranged by the organisers.

Give the park itself an hour if you're passing through, or half a day with a picnic. If you add the Botanic Garden and a riverside stroll, you have a full, low-effort day close to the CBD.

## Seasons under the canopy

Adelaide summers are hot and dry. The figs earn their keep then, because the shade under the biggest trees is much cooler than the open lawn. On summer days, morning visits make sense, and they line up with the quietest weekend window.

Autumn and spring have milder light and fewer extremes, so you can sit in the open grass too. The figs are evergreen, so the park never looks bare. In winter, the deciduous plane trees along Plane Tree Drive drop their leaves, while the figs stay dark green above the lawn.
