## Why a Russian-language show fills a Tashkent arena

Russian is still everywhere in Tashkent. You see it on shop signs alongside Uzbek and hear it on the metro. Many residents of the capital grew up with it at home or at school. That gives Russian-language pop and estrada singers a ready audience here.

Touring acts from that world book the city's sports halls regularly. Expect a mixed crowd: families, couples on a Friday night out, groups of friends who know every chorus. If you don't speak Russian, the songs still work, and the crowd's singing tells you which ones are the hits.

## Getting up to Yunusobod

Yunusobod is a large residential district north of the centre. It is mostly built of wide avenues and Soviet-era and newer apartment blocks. It is not a walk from the old-city sights. Plan on a ride.

- **Metro:** The Yunusobod line (shown in blue on the network map) runs north into the district. You can change onto it from Amir Temur Xiyoboni station in the centre. Tashkent's stations are famous in their own right, with chandeliers and carved stone, so the trip is part of the evening.
- **Taxi:** Yandex Go is the ride app most people use in Tashkent. It shows the fare before you confirm, which avoids haggling with street cabs.
- **From the centre:** By car the trip runs along the broad avenues heading north from Amir Temur Square. Traffic gets heavier in the Friday evening rush.

After the show, hundreds of people request cars at the same time outside one building. Many concertgoers walk a few blocks away from the entrance before ordering, or take the metro instead.

## A coat, a bouquet and a January night

Mid-January is Tashkent's coldest stretch. Nights often drop below zero, and snow is possible. That shapes the evening more than you might expect.

Indoor venues across the former Soviet world run a *garderob*, a staffed cloakroom where you hand over your coat for a numbered tag. Arenas usually have one too. At the end of a concert the cloakroom line can be the slowest part of the night. Some attendees carry a lighter jacket they can keep on their lap to skip it.

Then there are the flowers. At Russian-language concerts, fans often bring bouquets and walk them up to the front of the stage between songs. Singers usually stop to accept them. Seeing it for the first time can surprise visitors. Florists around Tashkent's markets and metro entrances sell bouquets all year round.

## Doors, bags and paying your way

A few habits make the night easier.

- **Security:** Sports complexes in Tashkent run bag checks at the entrance. Large bags slow the line, so a small bag or pockets is what most people use.
- **ID:** Carry your passport when you're out in Uzbekistan. Foreign visitors are expected to have it with them.
- **Money:** Uzbekistan uses the som. Local Uzcard and Humo cards are everywhere, but foreign Visa and Mastercard work less reliably at small kiosks. Carrying som in cash covers snacks and the cloakroom tip if there is one.
- **Language:** Venue staff in Tashkent generally speak Uzbek and Russian. *Spasibo* (thank you) in Russian or *rahmat* in Uzbek goes a long way.

Paper tickets and phone screens are both common at Tashkent events. Most people have theirs open on their phone before reaching the turnstile, which keeps the line moving.

## Making a weekend of it

A Friday concert leaves the weekend free for the city.

- **Chorsu Bazaar:** The domed market in the old town. It's busiest on weekend mornings, when stalls sell dried fruit, nuts, bread and spices.
- **Khast Imam complex:** Tashkent's religious centre. Its library holds the Uthman Quran, one of the oldest surviving Qurans in the world.
- **Amir Temur Square and Broadway (Sayilgoh Street):** The central square and the pedestrian street beside it, linked by metro to Yunusobod.
- **Plov for lunch:** Tashkent's rice-and-meat dish is traditionally a midday meal. The big plov centres often run out by mid-afternoon.

In January, several of these are at their quietest. Tour groups thin out in winter, and the bazaar's covered dome is a warm place to spend a cold morning.

## Where to stay for a north-side show

Most international visitors base themselves in central Tashkent, around Amir Temur Square and the Mustaqillik area. That keeps the old town and the main metro interchanges close. Getting to Yunusobod from there takes a short metro ride or taxi.

Staying in Yunusobod itself puts you near the arena but further from the sights. It suits people who are in town mainly for the concert. Either way, the metro links both areas, and app taxis run late into the night.
