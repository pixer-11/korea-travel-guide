## A week framed by two travel days

The calendar opens and closes with suitcases. The 2026 FIDE World U7-U17 Rapid and Blitz Chess Championships runs in Cairo from December 8 to December 15, 2026. Those two dates mark arrivals and departures, so the rounds themselves fall in the days between.

That shape matters if you're the parent, coach or sibling doing the travelling. December 8 is about airport queues, hotel check-ins and registration. December 15 is about getting everyone and their chess sets home. The playing days sit inside that frame.

FIDE, the world chess federation, organises the championship. The Egyptian Chess Federation hosts it. For each young player, a world title in their own age group is on the line, played at speeds where one loose move can cost a whole game.

## Fast chess, six age brackets

Nothing about this event is slow. Under FIDE rules, a rapid game gives each player more than 10 and under 60 minutes on the clock. In blitz, each player has 10 minutes or less. That makes for several rounds a day, not one long sitting.

The field is split into six age categories, from the under-7s up to the under-17s. Picture rows of tables, each with a board, a set and a digital clock between the two players. In rapid and blitz, FIDE rules don't require players to write down their moves. So the main sound in the hall is the steady click of clock buttons.

For younger children this is a very different experience from a classical tournament. Games finish quickly and results come in waves. A child can have a bad morning and a good afternoon. Coaches and parents tend to treat each round on its own rather than tracking the standings minute by minute.

## The venue, entries and tickets

The organiser publishes the dates, venue and any spectator or ticket arrangements on its official site. FIDE's own events pages are the other main source, and they also carry pairings and results while play is under way.

Players don't enter as walk-ins. At FIDE world youth championships, players are registered through their national chess federations. Families travelling with a player usually deal with their own federation for accommodation and accreditation, not with the host directly.

Spectating at a youth championship is shaped mostly by hall-access rules. The physical layout of the playing area, the space set aside for parents and how close visitors can get to the boards all depend on the venue. That information sits with the organiser.

## Behind the rope line: how parents and coaches behave

The etiquette at a FIDE youth event is strict, and it is the same in Cairo as anywhere else. Parents who have done a few of these tend to follow a familiar routine:

- **Phones stay out of the playing area.** FIDE's anti-cheating rules ban electronic devices near the boards, and players can be checked. Parents usually leave phones with someone outside the hall, or leave them behind.
- **No signals during a round.** Waving, mouthing a word or pointing at a board can get a game forfeited. Most parents wait outside the playing zone until their child comes out.
- **Results come online.** FIDE events are usually broadcast live, so many families follow the boards on a phone in the lobby instead of watching from the doorway.
- **Handshakes bookend every game.** Children are expected to shake hands before the first move and after the result, whatever happened.
- **Coaches debrief away from the hall.** Post-mortems happen in corridors and hotel rooms, never beside a board that is still in play.

Outside the venue, the courtesies are Cairo's own. A *salam alaykum* on arrival and a *shukran* (thank you) go a long way. Small tips, known as *baksheesh*, are customary for porters, drivers and anyone who helps with bags. Visitors usually carry some Egyptian pounds in cash for kiosks, cafés and tips. Uber and Careem both operate in the city.

## Cairo in mid-December

December is one of Cairo's most comfortable months. Days are mild, mostly in the low twenties Celsius, and the sky is usually clear and dry. Evenings get noticeably cooler, so families tend to pack a jumper for the walk back from dinner.

Most international delegations fly into Cairo International Airport, northeast of the centre. Many nationalities are eligible for an Egyptian e-visa issued online, and some can get a visa on arrival. Each country's rules differ, and the official Egyptian visa portal sets them out.

Cairo traffic is famous for good reason. A crossing that looks short on a map can take an hour at rush hour. That's why families at events like this usually stay near the venue. They don't want a long drive eating into a child's rest between rounds.

## Tawla, tea and the coffee houses

Board games are already part of street life here. In Cairo's traditional coffee houses, the *ahwa*, men sit at metal tables on the pavement over glasses of tea. They play *tawla* (backgammon) and dominoes, and the slap of the pieces carries down the street. Chess players notice it straight away.

After a day of fast chess, an *ahwa* is a low-key place to sit. Order *shai* (tea) or a small Turkish-style coffee. Watching an older generation play its own fast games is part of the visit. Many *ahwa* are smoky with shisha, so families with small children often pick an outdoor table.

## Between rounds and after the last game

Most families add sightseeing before or after the playing days. These are the places they usually head for:

- **The Pyramids of Giza and the Great Sphinx**, on the plateau at the western edge of greater Cairo. They're best seen in the morning, before the midday light flattens everything.
- **The Grand Egyptian Museum**, beside the Giza plateau, home to the Tutankhamun collection on a huge scale.
- **The Egyptian Museum on Tahrir Square**, the older pink-walled museum in downtown Cairo.
- **Khan el-Khalili and Al-Muizz Street** in Islamic Cairo: a covered bazaar and a street lined with mosques and medieval gateways.
- **Al-Azhar Park**, a hilltop garden with views across the minarets of the old city to the Citadel.
- **A felucca on the Nile**, the traditional sailboat. An hour on the river is a quiet way to end a tournament week.

Because December 15 is a departure day, families who want the pyramids without pressure usually fit them in around the first round or add a night at the end.
