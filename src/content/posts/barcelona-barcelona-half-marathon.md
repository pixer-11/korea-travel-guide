## A Valentine's Sunday on the seafront

The date is Sunday, February 14, 2027. For one morning the city's eastern streets belong to runners rather than traffic, and the 35th anniversary makes this edition a milestone.

More than 23,000 participants line up, and they come from all over the world. Many of them travel to Barcelona for exactly this weekend. You hear Spanish, Catalan, English, French and Italian in the start pens. Plenty of couples run it together, and on Valentine's Day that's hard to miss.

## The course and why runners chase times here

The route is known as one of Europe's fast half marathons. It's flat, it's at sea level, and it uses wide avenues rather than tight old-town lanes. In recent editions it has started and finished near Parc de la Ciutadella and the red-brick Arc de Triomf, then headed east.

From there the course usually takes in:

- **Poblenou**, the old industrial grid of former factories and long straight streets
- **Avinguda Diagonal**, the broad avenue cutting across the city
- **Diagonal Mar and the Fòrum**, with open esplanades by the Mediterranean
- **The seafront stretches** near the Vila Olímpica and the beaches

There is very little climbing to break your rhythm. Spectators get long sight lines too, so you can spot your runner well before they reach you.

## February weather and what runners usually wear

Barcelona in mid-February is mild by northern European standards. It's still winter, though. Early mornings are cool, and the open seafront sections can feel breezy even on a clear day.

Most runners wear a disposable layer, such as an old sweatshirt or a bin bag, in the start pen and drop it once they're moving. Spectators usually dress for standing still: a jacket, a scarf and something warm to hold from a café. By late morning the sun on the promenade makes the finish area a comfortable place to stay.

## Registration, bibs and race weekend

Entry works through online registration rather than spectator tickets. The organiser publishes registration details, entry categories and the official course map on the race's website. Watching from the roadside is free and open to anyone.

On race weekends like this, runners collect their bibs at a race expo in the days before the start. International entrants usually arrive in Barcelona on the Friday or Saturday for that reason. It also lets you walk part of the course and sort out your route to the start before Sunday.

## Getting to the start line

The metro is how most people reach the start area, since roads around the course are closed on race morning. Two stations sit close to the Ciutadella end:

- **Arc de Triomf** (Line 1, red), beside the triumphal arch at the top of Passeig de Lluís Companys
- **Ciutadella | Vila Olímpica** (Line 4, yellow), by the park's seaward side and the Olympic port

Visitors running the race tend to stay in El Born, the Eixample or Poblenou, all within walking distance or a few metro stops of the start. Taxis and ride-hails get stuck behind closures on Sunday morning, so most people leave them for the afternoon.

## Cheering and refuelling the Barcelona way

The race goes by its Catalan name, **Mitja Marató de Barcelona**, and you'll see that on signs and in the local press. Crowds shout "Ànims!" (Catalan for "courage", or "keep going") and "Vinga!" ("come on"). Shouting either one at a flagging stranger gets a smile, and sometimes a second wind.

Some practical things to know about race day:

- **Sunday trading:** many shops close on Sundays, but cafés and bakeries near the course open for the crowds
- **Ordering coffee:** a *tallat* (Catalan) or *cortado* (Spanish) is the espresso with a splash of milk that locals drink standing at the bar
- **Paying:** cards and contactless phones work almost everywhere, including small bars
- **Tipping:** rounding up or leaving a few coins is normal; nobody expects a percentage
- **Post-race lunch:** Spaniards eat late, so restaurants fill from around 2pm, well after the race is over

Supporters usually choose one point near the start and a second on the seafront, then take the metro to the finish. The flat, mostly straight course makes that easy. After the finish, a lot of runners walk into Parc de la Ciutadella, past the Cascada fountain, to stretch on the grass before heading to El Born for lunch.
