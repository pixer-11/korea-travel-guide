Seoul has five palaces left from the Joseon dynasty, plus the shrine where the kings' spirit tablets are kept, and for five days in October 2026 all six were set to operate as one coordinated festival instead of six separate ticket lines. That's the whole premise of the K-Royal Culture Festival: it doesn't build a stage in a park, it borrows stages that are five hundred years old.

## What's actually happening October 7-11

The 2026 Autumn Edition was planned to turn Gyeongbokgung, Changdeokgung, Changgyeonggung, Deoksugung, Gyeonghuigung and Jongmyo Shrine into what the organizers call living stages of royal heritage. In practice that meant the throne halls, courtyards and stone-paved plazas that are normally roped off or seen from a walkway would become the backdrop for performances, ceremonial reenactments and after-dark openings.

Because the program spanned six separate sites, no two of them had identical schedules. A costume parade at Gwanghwamun gate reads nothing like a court music recital inside Injeongjeon Hall at Changdeokgung, and Jongmyo's ancestral rites are a different register entirely from anything staged at Deoksugung.

## Picking which palace to see it at

Each site sits in a different part of central Seoul, so the choice of venue shapes a whole day, not just an evening.

- **Gyeongbokgung**: the largest and most photographed palace, near Gyeongbokgung Station (Line 3)
- **Changdeokgung**: known for its Secret Garden, Anguk Station (Line 3) exit 3
- **Changgyeonggung**: quieter grounds next to Changdeokgung, walkable from the same Anguk exit
- **Deoksugung**: downtown, beside City Hall Station (Lines 1 and 2), famous for its stone wall road
- **Gyeonghuigung**: the smallest and least crowded of the five, near Seodaemun Station
- **Jongmyo Shrine**: a short walk from Jongno 3(sam)-ga Station (Lines 1, 3 and 5)

Gyeongbokgung and Changdeokgung typically carry the biggest festival presence simply because they're the biggest palaces. Gyeonghuigung and Jongmyo represent the calmer, more ceremonial end of the program.

## Jongmyo after dark, and why it's different

Jongmyo Shrine isn't a palace, it's the Confucian shrine where Joseon kings and queens were memorialized, and it's usually one of the more subdued stops on a Seoul itinerary. During the festival it tends to host the more solemn, ritual-based programming rather than music-and-parade spectacle, nearer in tone to the ancestral rites the shrine is built for than to a palace gala.

For anyone who has already done the standard palace circuit, this is the site that feels genuinely new rather than a rerun with lanterns.

## Getting between six sites in five days

Six sites are more than a single visit comfortably covers. Four of them (Gyeongbokgung, Changdeokgung, Changgyeonggung, Gyeonghuigung) sit within a compact cluster in Jongno-gu, while Deoksugung is a subway ride south near City Hall and Jongmyo is its own stop east of Anguk.

- Gyeongbokgung and Gyeonghuigung pair naturally, as do Changdeokgung and Changgyeonggung, since each pair is a short walk apart
- Deoksugung and Jongmyo work better as separate half-days than as add-ons
- Palace grounds are large, and October evenings cool off fast once the sun drops

## Reading a festival schedule that spans six locations

Because this wasn't one event with one printed program, the schedule was broken down by venue and date. Individual performances and ceremonies at each palace had set times within the October 7-11 window rather than continuing all day.

Some palace programming, especially night openings, has historically required separate reservation from daytime palace admission in past festival editions, rather than being included in a regular palace ticket.

## Dressing the part at the gate

Several of these palaces run hanbok-related perks the rest of the year, where visitors wearing the traditional dress get free or discounted daytime entry, and rental shops cluster right outside Gyeongbokgung and Changdeokgung's main gates. Hanbok fit the visual theme of the whole festival, and it photographs well against lit-up gates and courtyards. Palace policies vary by site and by season, including whether the admission perk or any festival-specific dress code applies.

## Locals' evening routine at Gwanghwamun

Seoul residents who aren't tourists still treat Gwanghwamun Square and the palace walls as an evening walking route, especially once the October air turns crisp. On festival evenings, that ordinary foot traffic typically merges with festival crowds heading to the same gate, so the plaza in front of Gyeongbokgung is usually busier than on a normal autumn night, even outside official performance times.

The gate itself is easiest to photograph without a wall of people before an evening's program starts. Once a parade or ceremony is underway, the courtyard and gate area typically fill in fast and stay full until it ends.
