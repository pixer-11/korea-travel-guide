Seoul has five palaces left from the Joseon dynasty, plus the shrine where the kings' spirit tablets are kept, and for five days in October all six of them run as one coordinated festival instead of six separate ticket lines. That's the whole premise of the K-Royal Culture Festival: it doesn't build a stage in a park, it borrows stages that are five hundred years old.

## What's actually happening October 7-11

The 2026 Autumn Edition turns Gyeongbokgung, Changdeokgung, Changgyeonggung, Deoksugung, Gyeonghuigung and Jongmyo Shrine into what the organizers call living stages of royal heritage. In practice that means the throne halls, courtyards and stone-paved plazas that are normally roped off or seen from a walkway become the backdrop for performances, ceremonial reenactments and after-dark openings.

Because the program spans six separate sites, no two of them run identical schedules. A costume parade at Gwanghwamun gate reads nothing like a court music recital inside Injeongjeon Hall at Changdeokgung, and Jongmyo's ancestral rites are a different register entirely from anything staged at Deoksugung.

## Picking which palace to see it at

Each site sits in a different part of central Seoul, so your choice of venue decides your whole day, not just your evening.

- **Gyeongbokgung**: the largest and most photographed palace, Gwanghwamun gate faces Gyeongbokgung Station (Line 3)
- **Changdeokgung**: known for its Secret Garden, Anguk Station (Line 3) exit 3
- **Changgyeonggung**: quieter grounds next to Changdeokgung, walkable from the same Anguk exit
- **Deoksugung**: downtown, beside City Hall Station (Lines 1 and 2), famous for its stone wall road
- **Gyeonghuigung**: the smallest and least crowded of the five, near Seodaemun Station
- **Jongmyo Shrine**: a short walk from Jongno 3(sam)-ga Station (Lines 1, 3 and 5)

If you only have one evening, Gyeongbokgung or Changdeokgung will carry the biggest festival presence simply because they're the biggest palaces; Gyeonghuigung and Jongmyo reward a visit if you want to see the calmer, more ceremonial end of the program.

## Jongmyo after dark, and why it's different

Jongmyo Shrine isn't a palace, it's the Confucian shrine where Joseon kings and queens were memorialized, and it's usually one of the more subdued stops on a Seoul itinerary. During the festival it tends to host the more solemn, ritual-based programming rather than music-and-parade spectacle, closer in tone to the ancestral rites the shrine is built for than to a palace gala.

If you've already done the standard palace circuit on a past trip, this is the site that will feel genuinely new rather than a rerun with lanterns.

## Getting between six sites in five days

You won't manage all six in one visit, and you shouldn't try. Four of the sites (Gyeongbokgung, Changdeokgung, Changgyeonggung, Gyeonghuigung) sit within a compact cluster in Jongno-gu, while Deoksugung is a subway ride south near City Hall and Jongmyo is its own stop east of Anguk.

- Cluster Gyeongbokgung + Gyeonghuigung together, or Changdeokgung + Changgyeonggung together, since each pair is a short walk apart
- Treat Deoksugung and Jongmyo as separate half-days rather than add-ons
- Build in downtime between sites; palace grounds are large and October evenings cool off fast once the sun drops

## Reading a festival schedule that spans six locations

Because this isn't one event with one printed program, the schedule lives on the official festival site broken down by venue and date. Confirm timing and tickets on the official site before committing to a specific evening, since individual performances or ceremonies at each palace run at set times within the October 7-11 window rather than continuously all day.

Some palace programming, especially night openings, has historically required separate reservation from daytime palace admission in past festival editions. Treat that as the pattern to check for on the current site rather than something to assume is included in a regular palace ticket.

## Dressing the part at the gate

Several of these palaces run hanbok-related perks the rest of the year, where visitors wearing the traditional dress get free or discounted daytime entry, and rental shops cluster right outside Gyeongbokgung and Changdeokgung's main gates. During the festival, arriving in hanbok fits the visual theme of the whole event and photographs well against the lit-up gates and courtyards. It's worth checking whether that same admission perk and any festival-specific dress code apply on the official site, since palace policies vary by site and by season.

## Locals' evening routine at Gwanghwamun

Seoul residents who aren't tourists still treat Gwanghwamun Square and the palace walls as an evening walking route, especially once the October air turns crisp. During the festival, expect that ordinary evening foot traffic to merge with festival crowds heading to the same gate, so the plaza in front of Gyeongbokgung will be busier than a normal autumn night even outside official performance times.

If you want photos of the gate itself without a wall of people in frame, arrive before whatever start time is listed for that evening's program rather than at the advertised performance time. Once a parade or ceremony is underway, the courtyard and gate area fill in fast and stay full until it ends.
