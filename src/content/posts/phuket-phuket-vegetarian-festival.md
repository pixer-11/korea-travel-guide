The first sign of the festival isn't a sound. It's a color. Strings of yellow flags snap over shophouse doorways throughout Phuket Town, and vendors selling all-white ritual clothing set up folding tables on street corners days before anything official begins.

## What the festival actually is

The Phuket Vegetarian Festival, or Nine Emperor Gods Festival, is a nine-day Taoist purification rite brought by Hokkien Chinese immigrants generations ago. Participants abstain from meat, alcohol, and other indulgences to cleanse body and spirit. The most visible part is the street processions, which draw crowds with cameras. In them, entranced devotees called ma song pierce their cheeks with skewers, blades, and even branches. They are believed to channel deities and absorb the community's misfortune.

## Getting there and the venues

Most of the action centers on Phuket Town, not the beach resorts. Key sites include:

- **Jui Tui Shrine** on Ranong Road, the main shrine and the epicenter of the loudest processions
- **Bang Niew Shrine**, known for particularly intense piercing rituals
- **Sui Boon Tong Shrine**, smaller and more local in feel

From Patong or Kata, taxis and songthaews run into Phuket Town in 30–45 minutes. A rented scooter gives more flexibility, but roads near shrines usually close during processions. The last stretch is typically covered on foot.

## When to go each day

The 2026 festival was set for October 10–18. Processions typically move at different times on different days. They often start mid-morning and continue into the evening, and some of the most intense piercing rituals happen in the afternoon. There's no single fixed daily schedule across the island. Each shrine sets its own procession times and route, and these can shift from day to day.

## What to eat (and how to order it)

Even apart from the processions, the food is worth the trip. Street stalls across Phuket Town switch entirely to vegetarian and vegan menus for the nine days. They are marked by yellow flags and signs reading "jay".

- The yellow flag signals a jay-certified stall
- Khanom jeen (rice noodles with curry-style sauces made without meat) is a staple
- Mock-meat dishes made from soy, mushroom, and taro are everywhere and genuinely good
- Prices are budget-friendly, often just a bit more than regular street food

## How to visit like a local

Locals dress modestly. White clothing is traditional for those observing the fast, and spectators usually wear white or light, plain colors out of respect. Black is avoided.

Eating meat, drinking alcohol, or bringing non-jay food near shrine areas during the festival is considered deeply disrespectful, given the purification theme.

For the piercing processions, locals claim curb space on plastic stools long before the drums and firecrackers signal an approaching procession, often 30–45 minutes ahead. Firecrackers are thrown directly at the ma song as they pass. Onlookers keep a few meters back, and smoke, noise, and debris underfoot are part of the scene.

Payment at food stalls and shrines is overwhelmingly cash-based. Vendors rarely have change for large bills, so small Thai baht notes are the norm. Entry to shrines and processions is free. Donation boxes are common and appreciated but never mandatory.

## Beyond the processions

For anyone not drawn to the crowds and skewers, the shrines themselves are worth a quieter daytime visit, with incense smoke curling through carved wooden beams, drummers rehearsing, and families lighting joss sticks. Phuket Old Town's Sino-Portuguese shophouses on Thalang and Dibuk roads make a good walking detour between shrine visits. They are especially pleasant in the cooler early morning hours before the day's events start.
