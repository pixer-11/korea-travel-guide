## What you're looking at

Kalan Mosque was built in the 1510s under the Shaybanids, replacing an earlier mosque destroyed by Genghis Khan's army. It's one of the largest in Central Asia.

The courtyard is ringed by a colonnade carrying 288 small domes, each one lighting a bay below. At the far end, a tiled iwan portal frames the entrance, its turquoise and cobalt majolica largely original 16th-century work. The blue-ribbed dome over the mihrab chamber is visible from across the old city.

## Po-i-Kalyan, not a standalone stop

The mosque is one piece of the Po-i-Kalyan ensemble, on Khodja Nurobobod Street in central Bukhara. The 47-metre Kalyan Minaret stands right next to it, the structure Genghis Khan reportedly spared for its beauty. Directly opposite is the Mir-i-Arab Madrasa, still a working Islamic school.

- Kalyan Minaret: climb-adjacent landmark, same courtyard
- Mir-i-Arab Madrasa: facing entrance, active seminary, exterior viewing only
- Ark of Bukhara fortress: roughly 10-minute walk northwest
- Lyab-i Hauz plaza: about 15 minutes on foot, good spot to end the walk

Most visitors combine all three Po-i-Kalyan buildings with the Ark fortress in a single half-day loop through the old city.

## Timing your visit

Hours run 9am to 6pm every day of the week, no weekly closure. That's unusual for a working mosque cluster and worth building a route around.

Midday brings the bulk of tour groups, since Bukhara's old city walking tours cluster around lunchtime. Early morning light also suits the tilework better, catching the turquoise glaze at a low angle rather than the flat glare of noon. The mosque is described as well-known rather than obscure, so expect company at any hour; there's no quiet secret entrance or back route around the crowds here.

## Dress and courtyard etiquette

This is an active mosque, not a museum, even though it draws heavy tourist footfall.

- Cover shoulders and knees; women may want a scarf on hand for the prayer hall
- Remove shoes before stepping onto carpeted areas inside
- The courtyard itself is open and photographable; the interior prayer hall is smaller and quieter, so keep voices down
- Friday mornings can see local worshippers using the space for prayer, so tourist access to the hall may be more limited than other days

Entry to the courtyard is typically ticketed alongside the wider Po-i-Kalyan complex rather than the mosque alone; confirm current pricing at the gate since fees and combined-ticket arrangements shift year to year. Cash in Uzbek som is the safe default for any ticket booth in the old city, as card readers are inconsistent.
