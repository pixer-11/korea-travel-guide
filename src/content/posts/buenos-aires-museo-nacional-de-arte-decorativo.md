## A Paris mansion on Libertador

From the avenue it looks like a palace lifted out of the 8th arrondissement. That is close to the truth. French architect René Sergent designed the Palacio Errázuriz for the Chilean diplomat Matías Errázuriz and his wife Josefina de Alvear. It was finished in the 1910s, and Sergent never visited Buenos Aires to see it built.

The family's furniture, art and objects stayed with the house when the state acquired it. The museum opened here in 1937. You are walking through a private home kept largely as it was furnished, so this is no white-walled gallery.

## Room by room

Each salon was dressed in a different historical style. That makes the visit feel like a sequence of sets:

- **The Great Hall**: a double-height space in a Renaissance-revival style, with dark carved wood and a grand staircase.
- **The ballroom**: French Rococo, with gilded panelling, mirrors and chandeliers.
- **The smaller salons**: Louis XV and Louis XVI rooms with 18th-century French furniture, porcelain and tapestries.
- **The garden**: a formal French terrace facing Libertador, a good place to look back at the stone facade.

Allow 60–90 minutes for the interior. A guided tour adds context that the wall labels skip.

## Getting in without the crowd

Weekends are busy across the whole 1pm–7pm opening. If you have a choice, plan on Wednesday, Thursday or Friday instead. Saturday and Sunday afternoons fill the salons, and those rooms are not large.

- **Hours:** at the moment it opens 1–7pm, Wednesday to Sunday. Museum schedules change through the year and on holidays, so check the official site for this month's hours.
- **Monday and Tuesday:** currently closed. This catches many visitors planning a start-of-week museum day.
- **Tickets:** bought at the entrance. The current price is posted there and on the museum's website.
- **Tours:** guided visits run mainly in Spanish. Ask at the desk about English-language times before you go.
- **Etiquette:** keep bags close and hands off the furniture. Many pieces sit unroped in rooms that were built as living spaces.

## Getting there and what's nearby

The palace stands at Av. del Libertador 1902, near the corner of Pereyra Lucena. Many city bus lines run along Libertador and stop close by. The nearest Subte station is Las Heras on Line H, roughly 15 minutes away on foot through Recoleta.

The museum fits neatly into a half-day along the avenue:

- **Museo Nacional de Bellas Artes**: a short walk toward Recoleta, with Argentina's main national fine-art collection.
- **Floralis Genérica**: the giant steel flower in Plaza de las Naciones Unidas, about 10 minutes away.
- **Recoleta Cemetery**: a longer walk south, best seen before the museum opens at 1pm.
- **MALBA**: Latin American modern art on Av. Figueroa Alcorta, deeper into Palermo Chico.

On a weekday, the most efficient order is the cemetery in the morning, then this palace after 1pm, then Bellas Artes on the walk back.
