## A frontier town at the edge of Nopporo Forest

The village sits on the edge of Nopporo Forest Park, in Sapporo's eastern Atsubetsu Ward. Here the city gives way to a large stretch of mixed woodland. The buildings were moved here or reconstructed from all over Hokkaido. Each one stands for the decades after the 1860s, when settlers, farmers and fishermen poured north to open up the island.

You come in through a reproduction of the old Sapporo Station. It's a white-painted wooden building in the Western style the Meiji government favoured. Past it, a gravel street runs between clapboard shopfronts, sliding-door farmhouses and stone storehouses. The layout reads like a walk from town to countryside.

## Four villages in one afternoon

The site is split into districts, and each has its own trade and building style. The editorial summary counts over 60 homes and structures. They fall roughly into these groups:

- **Town area:** merchant shops, a newspaper office, a post office and a school on a central street
- **Fishing village:** herring-fishing quarters, where crews ate and slept on raised wooden floors around a central hearth
- **Farm village:** settler homes and barns, including the thatch and timber style brought from Honshu
- **Mountain village:** simpler dwellings and work sheds set among the trees

The herring quarters are worth slowing down for. Herring fishing once made fortunes on Hokkaido's west coast. These buildings show how big the crews were: long sleeping platforms and one huge shared room. Most interiors can be entered with shoes removed, and the dim, smoke-darkened timber is part of the effect.

## The horse on the main street

A horse-drawn trolley runs along the town street in the warmer months. Once snow settles, a horse-drawn sleigh takes over. Neither covers the whole site, but it's the easiest way to picture the street as it once worked.

Winter changes the village completely. Snow piles against the eaves, the paths are packed down, and the unheated interiors make it obvious how hard settler life was. Summer and early autumn give you the forest in full leaf, with longer, easier walking between districts.

## Getting there from central Sapporo

Take the Tozai Line subway, or a JR train, to Shin-Sapporo Station. From there, a bus heading toward the Nopporo Forest Park area stops near the village entrance. Check the bus stand at Shin-Sapporo for the current route and timetable before you set off.

By car, the address is Konopporo-50-1, Atsubetsu-cho, and there's parking near the park. The Hokkaido Museum is in the same forest park, a short walk away. Pairing the two makes a full day: the museum covers the island's natural history and Ainu culture, while the village shows the settler era up close.

## Timing your walk around the village

The gates open at 9:00am and close at 4:30pm every day of the week. Weekends are busy from 10am to 4pm, which is practically the whole day. On a Saturday or Sunday, be at the entrance at 9am so you have the farm and fishing districts to yourself for the first hour.

Three hours is a comfortable visit. Two hours works if you stick to the main street and the herring quarters. Last admission is usually earlier than closing time, so confirm it on the official site if you plan a late start. In winter, remember the light goes early in Hokkaido.

## Shoes off, eyes up: moving through the houses

Many buildings have a genkan, the sunken entry area just inside the door. Leave your shoes there, pointed toward the exit, before you step up onto wooden or tatami floors. Wear shoes you can slip off and on quickly, because you'll do it dozens of times.

A few other habits help:

- Don't sit or lean on displayed furniture, hearths or tatami set up as exhibits
- Photography is generally fine, but keep flash off inside the darker interiors
- Pick up an English map at the entrance; the site is spread out and the districts aren't obvious from the street
- Admission prices are posted at the gate and on the official website

Volunteer guides sometimes work in the buildings, often in Japanese. A simple "arigatou gozaimasu" when you leave goes a long way. Prices for the trolley or sleigh are posted at the boarding point, and cash is the safest way to pay for it.
