Picture a grassy bowl of land with rows of grapevines running up the slopes. Stages and sound towers stand at the bottom. That is Waiohika Estate, a working vineyard on the edge of Gisborne, and for four days at the end of December it hosts one of New Zealand's biggest New Year's Eve music festivals.

Rhythm and Vines runs from **December 28 to 31, 2026**. The final night is the one everyone builds towards: you count down to midnight in a city that sits close to the International Date Line.

## Four days on a vineyard

The setting is what separates this festival from a stadium gig. Waiohika Estate is farmland and vines, not concrete. You spend the days on grass under an open sky, and you move between stages by walking across the property.

It is midsummer in New Zealand. Days are long and the sun is strong; the country's UV levels are among the highest in the world. Regular festival-goers treat these things as standard kit:

- a hat and high-SPF sunscreen
- a refillable water bottle
- layers for after dark, when evenings on the East Coast cool off
- footwear that copes with grass, dust or mud

Four days is a long run, and veterans pace themselves. Most treat the early days as the warm-up and save their energy for the 31st.

## Counting down at the edge of the date line

This is the festival's real timing quirk. Gisborne is among the first cities on Earth to see each new day, so the R&V countdown on December 31 comes before almost all of the world's other celebrations. Sydney, London and New York see in the new year hours later.

The city's location also explains its Māori name, Tūranganui-a-Kiwa, and the region's name, Tairāwhiti, often translated as "the coast upon which the sun shines across the water". For anyone staying past midnight, the first sunrise of the year comes up over the Pacific a few hours later. Some attendees make their way to the coast for it on New Year's morning.

## Reaching Gisborne

Gisborne is remote by New Zealand standards, and the trip there is part of the plan. There are three main approaches:

- **By air:** Gisborne Airport sits just west of the city centre, with domestic flights linking it to larger hubs such as Auckland and Wellington.
- **From the north:** State Highway 2 from Ōpōtiki runs through the Waioeka Gorge. From Auckland it is a long day's drive of roughly six to seven hours.
- **From the south:** State Highway 2 from Napier and Hawke's Bay takes around three hours.

The gorge road is narrow and winding in places. With festival traffic heading the same way, drivers usually allow extra time and split the trip with rest stops. Waiohika Estate itself is a short drive out of the city.

## Tickets, camping and where people sleep

The organiser publishes ticket types, camping packages and the lineup on the official Rhythm and Vines site. That is the source to trust, rather than resale posts on social media.

Camping is a large part of the culture. Many attendees stay near the festival for the whole run, so the campsite becomes a second social scene between sets. Others base themselves in Gisborne's motels, holiday parks or rentals. That choice trades a tent for a bed and a daily trip out to the estate.

Gisborne is a small city, and the festival fills it in late December. Visitors who want a bed in town usually sort it out months in advance.

## Festival-week manners on the East Coast

A few local norms make the four days easier.

- **Paying:** New Zealand is a card-and-contactless country, and tapping a card or phone is normal almost everywhere. Some festival bars and food stalls use their own payment systems, which the organiser describes on its site.
- **Tipping:** It is not customary in New Zealand. Nobody expects a tip at a food truck or bar, though no one minds one either.
- **Leaving no trace:** This is someone's vineyard and farm, so regulars take their rubbish with them and leave the campsite as they found it.
- **Respecting the region:** Tairāwhiti has a large Māori population and deep iwi connections to the land. Treat local people and places with care, on site and in town.

Returning attendees also tend to pick a meeting spot on day one, such as a sign, a tree or a particular stage. Phone signal can get patchy when thousands of people are in one paddock.

## Days around the festival

Gisborne is worth staying in for a day or two either side.

- **Wainui Beach** is a long surf beach just north of the city and the obvious place to recover in the sea.
- **Midway Beach** is closer to town, with gentler water for swimming.
- **Titirangi (Kaiti Hill)** has a lookout over the port, Poverty Bay and the city.
- **Tairāwhiti Museum** covers the region's Māori and settler history.
- **Eastwood Hill Arboretum**, inland from the city, is a huge collection of trees and offers a quiet, shady walk after four days of bass.

The wider region is wine country too. The vines you dance beside at Waiohika are part of a district best known for its chardonnay.

## The morning after

New Year's Day in Gisborne is quiet. Roads out of town are busy as campers pack up and head home along SH2, and flights out are popular. Many visitors stay an extra night so they are not driving the gorge on little sleep. For a festival that ends with one of the planet's earliest midnights, it is a gentle way to start the year.
