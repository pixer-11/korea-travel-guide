## Where the town got its name

The actual "spring" is a waterhole. It lies in the sandy bed of the Todd River, below a low granite bar, with river red gums leaning over it. Surveyors in the 1870s took it for a permanent spring and named it after Alice Todd, wife of Charles Todd, the man behind the telegraph line.

Walk a few steps uphill and you reach the reason anyone stopped here. Low stone buildings with iron roofs and deep verandahs sit on the rise. These are the remains of a repeater station that relayed messages across the continent.

This is Arrernte country, and it was long before any wire arrived. The waterhole mattered to Arrernte people for thousands of years. Keep that in mind as you read the station's own story.

## Inside the 1872 station

The Overland Telegraph Line ran from Adelaide to Darwin. It opened in 1872, and from Darwin an undersea cable linked Australia to the rest of the world. Morse signals weakened over distance, so staffed repeater stations along the route picked them up and sent them on. This was one of them.

The precinct's buildings are laid out around a central yard. Look for:

- the telegraph office, where operators keyed messages through
- the stationmaster's residence, with period furnishings
- the barracks and kitchen, built of thick stone walls against the heat
- the blacksmith's shop, which kept equipment and horses working

The museum of communication tools gives the telegraph some context. You see how a message actually crossed the desert before radio. Guided tours run on site. They are the quickest way to understand what each room did, so check the day's tour schedule when you arrive.

## The Bungalow years

The site's later history is harder. After the telegraph work moved into town, the buildings became The Bungalow. From 1932 this was an institution for Aboriginal children of mixed descent, many taken from their families.

Interpretive material covers this period, and it belongs alongside the Morse keys and stationmaster's china. Take the time to read it. For many Central Australian families, this is the chapter that matters most.

## Getting out there from town

Herbert Heritage Drive runs north from the town centre, and the drive takes a few minutes. There is a car park at the reserve entrance.

A walking and cycling path also follows the Todd River from town up to the reserve. It is flat and easy, and a bike makes it a short, pleasant ride in the morning. On foot, allow about an hour each way. In the hotter months, do not try it after mid-morning.

## Pacing a visit in the Centre

Your day here depends on the heat. Plan around it.

- **Pay at the entry.** The ticketed part is the historic precinct. The current price is posted at the entry and on the NT Parks website.
- **Start early.** Summer days in Alice Springs are fierce, and the precinct has plenty of open ground between buildings. From April to September, mornings are cool, even crisp.
- **Carry water and a fly net.** Bush flies in the Centre are relentless in warm weather, and locals don't feel silly wearing a net.
- **Stay on the marked paths near the waterhole.** It is a place of Arrernte significance, not just a photo backdrop.
- **Use the cafe as a pause.** It is the natural break between the tour and a walk. The shaded lawns nearby suit a picnic.

Allow about 90 minutes for the buildings and museum. Add an hour or more if you plan to walk beyond them.

## Past the fence: trails and lookouts

The reserve is also where the Larapinta Trail begins. It runs 223 km west along the West MacDonnell Ranges to Mount Sonder. You can walk the opening stretch for an hour and turn back, and still feel what the full route is like.

Shorter marked walks climb the rocky hills around the station. From the high points you see red ridges, spinifex, and the line of gums tracing the dry Todd. On your way back to town, Anzac Hill gives the same country from the other end: the town grid set against the ranges.
