## Open sand, and a name from Tulum

Zamna Egypt puts a dance floor on the desert edge. The festival takes place in late November 2026 at an outdoor venue just beyond Cairo, where the city's concrete gives way to sand and a wide, unlit sky.

The Zamna name started in Tulum, Mexico. The brand made its reputation there with DJ sets staged in the jungle, and it is best known for house and techno. In Egypt the trees give way to dunes, but the idea stays the same: international DJs playing long sets in a setting that is part of the show.

## Getting out to the desert edge

Most international visitors fly into Cairo International Airport, east of the centre. Sphinx International Airport, out west near the Giza side, handles a smaller number of flights.

Getting around Cairo takes patience. A crossing from one side of the metro area to the other can take well over an hour when traffic is heavy. Uber and Careem both operate across the city, and most festival-goers use one of them or a car with a driver.

The ride home is the hard part, since desert-edge venues sit far from taxi ranks. Groups often hire a driver for the whole night or agree a pickup in advance. Where to stay depends on where the venue is:

- **West of Cairo:** Giza or Sheikh Zayed
- **East of Cairo:** New Cairo
- **Central base for sightseeing:** Zamalek or Downtown

## Warm afternoons, cold sand at 2am

Late November is one of Cairo's most comfortable months. Afternoons are warm and dry. Once the sun goes down, the desert loses its heat quickly, and an outdoor dance floor at night is noticeably colder than the city streets.

What people usually bring for a night on the sand:

- A jacket or warm layer for after midnight
- Closed shoes, since sand fills sandals within minutes
- A scarf or bandana against dust kicked up by the crowd
- A charged phone, needed for the ride home

## Moving through Cairo on a festival night

Cairo has its own rhythm, and a few habits make the night smoother.

Cash in Egyptian pounds still runs daily life here. Hotels, malls and bigger restaurants take cards, but drivers, porters and small shops often prefer cash. Small notes come in handy for tipping, known locally as *baksheesh*, which Egyptians expect for small services.

With ride-hailing apps, drivers often phone to confirm the pickup point, sometimes in Arabic. Dropping the pin at a named hotel or landmark makes this much easier. Police checkpoints are common on Egyptian highways, so attendees usually carry a passport or a copy of it on the drive out.

Dress follows two codes. Inside the venue, normal festival clothes are the norm. In the street, the hotel lobby and the car, Cairo dresses more modestly, so many attendees wear a cover-up layer on the way there and back.

## Days around the nights

Most people who fly in for a festival this far from home add a few days in the city, and Cairo gives them plenty to do:

- **Giza pyramids and the Sphinx**, on the plateau at the city's western edge
- **The Grand Egyptian Museum**, beside the Giza plateau and home to the Tutankhamun collection
- **Khan el-Khalili**, the old bazaar in Islamic Cairo, busiest in the evening
- **A felucca on the Nile**, the traditional sailing boats moored along the corniche near Zamalek and Garden City

A morning at the pyramids after a late night is a tough sell. Many visitors put the sightseeing before the festival and keep the day after for slow coffee in Zamalek.
