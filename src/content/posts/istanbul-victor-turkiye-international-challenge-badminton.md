## Shuttles at full speed, a few rows away

This is badminton you can actually see up close. The Türkiye International Challenge is an annual tournament sanctioned by the Badminton World Federation (BWF). It sits on the International Challenge tier, a few levels below the World Tour events you see on television. For the players, that tier is where world ranking points get built.

For you, it means a hall where several courts run at the same time. Players come from across Europe and from Asia, including badminton's strongest regions. The Victor in the title is the Taiwanese racket and shuttle brand that sponsors the tournament. Its logos usually cover the court surrounds, and the players' kit too.

## A December fixture in Istanbul

The 2026 edition is scheduled for December. The organiser publishes the exact dates, the venue and ticket information on its official site. Results and draws for BWF-sanctioned events appear on the BWF's tournament software platform, which is also where many fans follow live scores from home.

December is Istanbul's off-season. The days are short, the city is cool and often wet, and the sightseeing queues are far shorter than in summer. A tournament week fits well with a winter city break. You get the matches during the day and the city's restaurants and ferries at night.

## From qualifiers to finals day

The week has a set shape, and knowing it shapes your visit. International Challenge events usually open with qualifying rounds. Main-draw matches follow, and the finals come on the last day. There are five disciplines:

- Men's singles
- Women's singles
- Men's doubles
- Women's doubles
- Mixed doubles

Early in the week, several courts run side by side, so one ticket gets you a lot of badminton. Fans typically wander between courts, picking up a tight third game here and a doubles exchange there. By the final day there are fewer matches, usually one court in use, and every point is a title point.

Matches use the rally-point format: best of three games, each played to 21. A singles match can be over in half an hour or go well past an hour. Doubles points are short and very fast. Mixed doubles has its own tactics, with the woman usually at the net and the man smashing from the back.

## Courtside manners between rallies

Badminton crowds follow a quiet etiquette, and it matters here. Spectators stay in their seats while a rally is being played. They move, sit down or leave only once the shuttle hits the floor. At ends and between games there are short breaks, and that is when people usually move between courts.

- **No flash.** Shuttles travel very fast, and a flash at the wrong moment can put a player off. Most halls ban it.
- **Cheer after the point.** Applause and shouting come after the rally finishes, never during a serve.
- **Turkish phrases help.** "Merhaba" (hello) and "teşekkürler" (thank you) are useful at the entrance and the concession stand.
- **Layers.** Halls keep air movement low so the feather shuttles fly true. With the doors opening into the December weather, a hall can feel colder or warmer than you expect.

Players at this level are often easy to approach after their match. Plenty of fans ask for a photo or an autograph near the warm-up areas. They wait until the player has cooled down and is no longer heading straight for the physio.

## Getting around the city in December

Istanbul's public transport runs on one card. The Istanbulkart, which you top up at machines in stations, works on the metro, trams, Marmaray rail, buses and the city ferries. Visitors usually buy one on arrival rather than paying for single fares.

The city spreads across two continents, and traffic is heavy at rush hour. Rail is usually the more reliable choice. The Marmaray line runs under the Bosphorus and links the European and Asian sides in minutes. The T1 tram runs through the old city, past Sultanahmet, Eminönü and Karaköy. Both airports, Istanbul Airport and Sabiha Gökçen, have metro links into the city.

Visitors usually pick a base close to a rail line that serves the venue. In the old city that means the T1 corridor. On the European side, it means the M2 metro through Taksim and Şişli. Kadıköy, on the Asian side, is good for food and well served by ferries.

## Fitting the city around the draw

A tournament day leaves mornings or evenings free, depending on your order of play. Some favourites suit winter well:

- **Hagia Sophia and the Blue Mosque** in Sultanahmet, a short walk apart. Mosques close to tourists during prayer times.
- **The Basilica Cistern**, underground and out of the rain.
- **The Grand Bazaar**, a covered market with roughly sixty streets.
- **A Bosphorus ferry** from Eminönü or Karaköy. Fans usually ride it at dusk, with a glass of tea from the onboard vendor.
- **Kadıköy's market streets**, for fish, pickles and meyhane dinners.

A single evening session works for a casual fan. Badminton followers often come for two or three days and save the last for finals. Many spend the morning of the final at Topkapı Palace before heading to the hall.
