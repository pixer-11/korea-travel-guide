## A red-brick maze from 1874

The clock tower is how you find it. Above the traffic on Lindsay Street you'll see the red-brick Gothic front of the Sir Stuart Hogg Market. It opened in 1874, and hardly anyone in Kolkata calls it anything but New Market.

It was built as a covered market for the British residents of Chowringhee. Well over a thousand sellers work there now. The building has been extended and rebuilt over the years, including after a serious fire in 1985. Under the roof, the lanes cross one another and shops sit side by side.

This is one of the most visited and most reviewed markets in eastern India. Don't expect a quiet discovery. Expect crowds, noise and a very old way of shopping that still works.

## What's under the roof

Stalls are loosely grouped by trade. Walk a few turns and the goods change from fabric to spices to steel pots. What you'll find:

- **Textiles and ready-made clothing:** saris, kurtas, shawls and lengths of cloth, much of it in the outer lanes
- **Leather:** bags, belts, wallets and sandals
- **Dry fruits, spices and tea:** Darjeeling and Assam leaf, sold loose by weight
- **Cheese and cold stores:** look for Bandel cheese, the small smoked cheese from Hooghly, beside imported goods
- **Nahoum and Sons:** the Jewish bakery that has traded here since the early 1900s, known for its fruitcake and macaroons

Nahoum's is busiest around Christmas, when Kolkata gets serious about plum cake. It's worth a stop even if you buy nothing else.

## Reaching Lindsay Street

The closest metro stop is **Esplanade** on the Blue Line (Line 1). From there, walk south along Jawaharlal Nehru Road (Chowringhee) and turn onto Lindsay Street. The walk takes about ten minutes and passes the Maidan.

A yellow taxi or app cab can drop you at the Lindsay Street entrance. Parking nearby is very limited, so don't drive. Allow two to three hours for the market itself. Add more time if you plan to shop for clothes or eat in the lanes outside.

## Timing a visit to New Market

The market opens at 10:30am and closes at 8:30pm, seven days a week. Individual stalls set their own hours inside that window, so a few shutters may still be down at the start of the day.

**Weekdays between 11am and 12pm** are the quietest time. The lanes are open enough to stop and look without being pushed along, and shopkeepers have time to show you stock.

**Try not to come on a weekend between 2pm and 8pm.** That's when it's at its most crowded, and walking the narrow lanes becomes slow. It gets even busier before Durga Puja in autumn and in the weeks before Christmas and Eid.

## Bargaining, porters and paying at the stalls

How you shop here matters as much as when you come. A few habits make it easier:

- **Porters will find you.** Men with baskets often meet visitors at the entrances and offer to carry bags or guide you. Many are paid commission by particular shops. If you don't want one, say no politely and keep walking. If you do take one, agree his fee before you start.
- **Haggle at open stalls, not at fixed-price shops.** Clothing, leather and souvenir sellers expect a counter-offer. Bakeries, cold stores and branded shops usually don't.
- **Pay with cash or UPI.** Small stalls mostly take cash or UPI payments by QR code. Card machines are more common in the larger shops.
- **Ask for prices by weight.** For tea, spices and dry fruit, ask the price per 100 or 250 grams and taste before you buy.

Keep your phone and wallet in a front pocket in the tight inner lanes. That's standard practice in any crowded bazaar.

## Around the block afterwards

New Market is surrounded by other places worth seeing. **Free School Street**, just to the south, has second-hand bookshops. **Park Street**, a short walk further, has old-fashioned restaurants and tea rooms from the city's colonial and jazz-era past.

To the north, the **Indian Museum** on Chowringhee is the oldest museum in India. If you still have daylight, walk across the Maidan to the **Victoria Memorial** and see its white marble at the end of the afternoon.
