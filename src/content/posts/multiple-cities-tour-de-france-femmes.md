## Why go

The Tour de France Femmes is the biggest annual event in women's professional cycling, run by the same organization (ASO) that stages the men's Tour. The 2026 edition, its fifth, spans nine stages and 1,175 km, gathering the world's top teams — think riders from SD Worx-Protime, Lidl-Trek, and FDJ-Suez-Futuroscope in recent years — for roughly nine days of racing. Unlike a stadium event, it's entirely free to watch from the roadside, and the race passes through dozens of towns, so most spectators build a trip around one or two stages rather than the whole race. The headline stage in 2026 is a queen stage summit finish atop Mont Ventoux, the "Giant of Provence," a climb with a brutal, treeless upper section that has decided general-classification battles for decades.

## Getting there

Because the race moves city to city, "getting there" depends entirely on which stage you choose. Stage-start and finish towns are typically well served by SNCF regional or high-speed trains, and race organizers usually publish park-and-ride or shuttle information for mountain finishes a few weeks beforehand. For the Mont Ventoux stage, expect road closures on the mountain itself well ahead of the peloton — cars are usually barred from the summit road on race day, so plan to park in a valley town (Bédoin, Malaucène, or Sault are the traditional approach villages) and either walk, cycle, or catch a shuttle up. If you're targeting a flatter stage in a city center, arriving by train and walking to the barriers near the finish line is usually simplest.

## What to see (and how the race works)

Each stage has a start ("départ") and finish ("arrivée"), with a caravan of team cars, sponsor vehicles, and media preceding the riders — this rolling publicity parade is part of the spectacle and often more visible up close than the race itself, which can pass a fixed point in under a minute. For genuine drama, mountain stages are the move: on a climb like Ventoux, riders are spread out and moving slowly enough to see faces, team tactics, and gaps in real time, and spectators can walk sections of the closed road for hours beforehand. Flat and rolling stages suit those who want a shorter, festival-like day — a big screen at the finish town's fan zone, food stalls, and a sprint finish.

## When to go

The race runs August 1–9, 2026, squarely in the middle of the French summer holiday season, so expect warm weather, busy trains, and higher accommodation demand in host towns — booking lodging near a stage town months ahead is worth it, especially near Ventoux. Mountain-stage summits draw crowds that camp out from morning for an afternoon finish, so arriving early is less about missing the race and more about securing shade, a fence spot, or a view of a switchback. Dates, host towns, and stage order are set by ASO and can still shift slightly before race day, so always check the official Tour de France Femmes website or app for the confirmed itinerary before booking travel.

## How to visit like a local

Seasoned roadside fans don't try to see the whole race — they pick one climb or one town and settle in. On a mountain stage, arrive at least three to four hours before the expected passage time (organizers post estimated caravan and rider times per kilometer marker); bring your own food and water, since stalls near the summit sell out and prices climb. Payment on-site is mostly card or contactless now, but small roadside vendors in rural climb towns still favor cash. There's no ticket and no gate — the etiquette is simply to stay behind the barriers or off the tarmac once marshals wave flags, and to keep dogs leashed and children back from the white line, since motorbikes and team cars pass at speed right before the riders. The rookie mistake is treating it like a spectator sport with reserved sightlines: locals claim a spot with a chair, umbrella, and cooler well before the road closes, then wait out the caravan, which is half the fun, not a delay. For the closest thing to insider knowledge, follow local town Facebook pages or tourism offices in host communes — they post road-closure times and the best free viewing points days ahead of the officials.

## Nearby detours

If you're based near Ventoux, pair race day with a visit to the Provençal villages of Bédoin or Séguret, both known for markets and rosé, or the Roman theater at Orange. In flatter stage towns, town-center cathedrals, weekly markets, and regional food halls are usually within walking distance of the finish-line fan zone.
