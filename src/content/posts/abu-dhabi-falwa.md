## A small horse on the sign

The name comes first. *Falwa* is Arabic for "little horse," and the restaurant chose it as a symbol of grace and cultural pride. In the Gulf, horses carry real weight. Arabian bloodlines, endurance racing and the old desert relationship between rider and animal all sit behind that one word.

That choice tells you what to expect. This is a restaurant that wants to be read as Emirati first. It is neither a generic Middle Eastern grill nor a hotel buffet with a few local dishes added to make up the numbers.

## Why a contemporary Emirati kitchen stands out here

Abu Dhabi has no shortage of places to eat. What it has far fewer of is restaurants built around the cooking of the Emirates themselves. Many visitors leave the country without tasting it at all, having eaten Lebanese, Indian and international food all week.

Falwa's angle is contemporary takes on Emirati cuisine. To appreciate the reworking, it helps to know the tradition behind it. Classic Emirati home cooking draws on:

- **Machboos**: spiced rice cooked with meat or fish, the backbone of family meals
- **Harees**: wheat and meat slow-cooked to a smooth porridge, closely tied to Ramadan and weddings
- **Balaleet**: sweet saffron vermicelli, often topped with an omelette, eaten at breakfast
- **Luqaimat**: small fried dumplings finished with date syrup
- **Loomi**: dried black lime, giving many dishes their sour, smoky edge

That list is context, not Falwa's menu. The restaurant hasn't published a menu we can confirm. Look it up on its own channels before you go, and ask your server which traditional dish sits behind each plate.

## The chef and the room

Chef Faisal Alharmoodi leads the kitchen. Having an Emirati chef interpret Emirati food is part of the point. The dishes come from inside the culture rather than from a consultant's mood board.

The interiors are described as design-led, and the restaurant clearly treats the room as part of the experience. We can't vouch for the specific materials or layout, so we won't describe them. Expect a dining room that has been thought about as carefully as the plates, and dress to match. Smart casual is the safe choice for a newer Abu Dhabi restaurant with this kind of ambition.

## Booking a table at Falwa

Recently opened restaurants in Abu Dhabi fill fast, especially at weekends. Reserve ahead through the restaurant's official channels, and confirm your booking the day before. A few habits will make the evening go smoothly:

- **Coffee and dates.** If Arabic coffee (*gahwa*) comes out, it arrives in a small handleless cup. Hold it in your right hand. A gentle shake of the cup from side to side signals you've had enough.
- **Right hand for sharing.** When dishes are shared, pass and take food with your right hand.
- **Paying.** Cards and contactless payments are standard across Abu Dhabi. Read the bill before tipping, because many restaurants already add a service charge.
- **Dress.** Covered shoulders and knees sit comfortably in any UAE dining room.
- **Ramadan.** Restaurant hours change during the holy month, with many places centring service on iftar after sunset. Recheck timings if your trip falls in Ramadan.

## Getting there and planning the night

The address and opening hours aren't in our verified details, so take both from the restaurant's own listing or website. Abu Dhabi is a city built around cars. Taxis are everywhere, and ride-hailing through Careem or Uber is the easy option for most visitors.

From the main island or Saadiyat, journey times can stretch at evening rush hour. Leave yourself a buffer before your booking. If you're coming from Dubai, the drive takes roughly 90 minutes. Make Falwa the main event of the evening rather than squeezing it in on the way back.

Allow a couple of hours for dinner. A contemporary tasting-style meal with coffee at the end works best at an unhurried pace.

## Who will get the most from it

Falwa suits travellers who want to know what Emirati food tastes like, served with the polish of a modern restaurant. It works well as a first introduction, and better still after you've tried a traditional plate of machboos or luqaimat somewhere simpler. Having that comparison makes the reinterpretation easier to read.

It also suits anyone who cares about design and wants a dinner that feels rooted in the country they're visiting. A little horse is a modest name for an ambitious idea, and that modesty fits the place.
