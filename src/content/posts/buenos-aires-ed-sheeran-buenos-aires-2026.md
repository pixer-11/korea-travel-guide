## One man, one loop pedal, one old palace

The date is Tuesday, December 1, 2026. Ed Sheeran brings a stadium show to Estadio Huracán in Buenos Aires, and FINNEAS opens as the guest act. That is everything known about the night, and it's enough to plan around.

If you have never seen Sheeran live, the format is the surprise. For most of his career he has played stadiums more or less alone, building songs layer by layer on a loop pedal: a guitar slap for the beat, a hummed harmony, then the chorus over the top. Seventy thousand people waiting on one man to stamp a pedal is a strange and very good kind of tension.

FINNEAS comes at it from the other side. He is best known as the producer and co-writer behind his sister Billie Eilish's records, and on his own he plays piano-led songs with a quieter, bedroom-studio feel. The pairing works because both are songwriters first.

## Estadio Huracán, the building fans call El Palacio

You don't need to follow Argentine football to see why locals call this ground "El Palacio". The Estadio Tomás Adolfo Ducó went up in the late 1940s for Club Atlético Huracán, and it still looks like a building from that era. It has long horizontal stands in pale concrete, clean lines and a tall tower that stands over the main entrance like a lighthouse.

Film fans may know it already. The long stadium sequence in *El secreto de sus ojos*, the Argentine film that won the Oscar for Best Foreign Language Film, was shot here. Huracán's emblem is a hot-air balloon, which is why the club is nicknamed "El Globo". You'll see the balloon painted on walls for blocks around.

## Parque Patricios on a Tuesday in early summer

December 1 falls at the start of the southern summer. Evenings in Buenos Aires are long and warm at this point in the year, so the walk to the gates usually happens in daylight. People dress lightly and carry a layer for after midnight.

Parque Patricios is a working barrio in the south of the city, not a tourist district. Its centre is the park of the same name, Parque de los Patricios, a broad square of trees and paths along Avenida Caseros. In recent years the area has been redeveloped as the city's Distrito Tecnológico, so glass office blocks now stand among low houses and corner bars.

On a concert night, that mix becomes the pre-show. Fans gather on the grass in the park, street vendors set up grills along the avenues, and the smell of choripán (chorizo in bread, with chimichurri) follows the crowd to the stadium. Because it's a weeknight, plenty of people come straight from work, so the streets fill up gradually through the evening rather than all at once.

## Getting to Parque Patricios

The Subte (metro) is the simplest way in. Line H runs through the neighbourhood, and its Parque Patricios and Hospitales stations are the closest stops to the stadium. From either one it's a walk of several blocks, and on show nights the crowd shows you the way.

- **Subte Line H:** connects with Line E, Line A and Line B on its way north, so you can reach it from most central hotels with one change.
- **SUBE card:** the rechargeable card used for the Subte and city buses. You buy and top it up at stations and kiosks.
- **Colectivos (buses):** several lines cross the area, and journey planners show the right one from your address.
- **Taxis and ride apps:** easy on the way in. Afterwards, drivers usually wait several blocks from the ground, beyond the road closures.

Many visitors stay in San Telmo, Palermo or the Microcentro. San Telmo is the closest of the three.

Argentine stadium concerts usually sell several kinds of ticket. The main split is between *campo* (the standing field in front of the stage) and *platea* (numbered seats in the stands). Resale through unofficial sellers and social media is common for big international acts. ## How the night works in an Argentine stadium

Concerts here borrow their rituals from football. Learning a few of them makes the night easier.

- **Queues form by sector.** Each gate serves a specific part of the ground, and your ticket names it. People who join the wrong line lose a lot of time walking around the outside.
- **Police cordons:** there are usually two or three checks before the turnstiles, and IDs and tickets get shown more than once.
- **Paying for food:** street vendors outside mostly take cash in pesos. Mercado Pago QR codes are also everywhere, and Argentines use them for almost everything.
- **Singing:** Argentine crowds sing whole verses back at performers, often louder than the PA. Joining in is expected.
- **Leaving:** after the encore, most fans walk to Avenida Caseros or towards the Subte in large groups instead of waiting for a car at the gate.

Tipping is not part of the street-food or kiosk routine. In sit-down restaurants, leaving around ten percent (*la propina*) in cash is the norm.

## Before and after: Boedo and San Telmo

Boedo starts just north-west of Parque Patricios. It's one of the city's old tango barrios, and the corner of Avenida San Juan and Boedo is named in the tango "Sur". Esquina Homero Manzi, the café and tango house on that corner, is a classic spot for a drink before the show.

North-east of the stadium, San Telmo has cobbled streets, colonial-era houses and the covered Mercado de San Telmo. It's also a natural place to stay. Its parrillas (steakhouses) and bars stay open well after midnight, which suits a crowd coming back from a stadium.

If you have a day to spare, the river-port neighbourhood of La Boca is close by too. You'll find the painted houses of Caminito there, and Boca Juniors' stadium, La Bombonera.
