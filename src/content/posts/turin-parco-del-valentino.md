You enter from Corso Massimo d'Azeglio and the traffic noise drops away almost immediately. Plane trees and horse chestnuts, some over a century old, arch over gravel paths that trace the curve of the Po river. Rowing crews slice past below the embankment; joggers and cyclists share the same wide lanes without much friction.

## Castello del Valentino and the Borgo Medievale

The castle anchors the park's northern end, a French-inspired brick and stone residence built for Turin's royal family in the 1600s. It now houses the university's architecture faculty, so the interior is off-limits to casual visitors, but the riverfront facade and formal gardens are worth the walk. Further south sits the Borgo Medievale, a full reconstruction of a 15th-century village built for the 1884 General Italian Exhibition. Its fortified walls, working courtyard, and small church make it feel older than it is, though it's barely 140 years old.

## The rock garden and river paths

Near the castle, a small rock garden (giardino roccioso) packs alpine plants and a pond into a tight, shaded pocket, a good five-minute detour off the main paths. The riverside promenade runs the length of the park and connects to the Murazzi embankment further north, popular at dusk. Benches face the water in stretches, and in warmer months you'll see paddle-boarders and rowing club members using the same stretch of river.

- Enter near Piazza Vittorio Veneto or from Corso Massimo d'Azeglio for the castle and Borgo Medievale
- Allow 1.5 to 2 hours to cover castle grounds, rock garden, and river path at an easy pace
- Nearest transit: Porta Nuova train station, then a 15-20 minute walk, or tram lines running along Corso Vittorio Emanuele II
- Free to enter; the Borgo Medievale's interior sections may charge separately, so confirm current admission before going

## How to visit like a local

The park is open around the clock, but that doesn't mean every hour suits every visitor. Mornings between 7am and 9am, on both weekdays and weekends, are consistently the quietest stretch, before joggers give way to dog walkers and student crowds from the nearby Politecnico. Weekend afternoons, roughly noon through 11pm, draw the heaviest foot traffic, especially near the castle and riverside cafes, so if you want empty paths and good light for photos, come early.

This is one of Turin's most visited green spaces, not a quiet secret, so don't expect solitude on a sunny Sunday. Locals treat it as a through-route as much as a destination: commuting cyclists, students cutting between the Politecnico and city center, and rowing club members heading to the river all use it daily. There's no ticket booth at the main gates and no tipping culture here; bring cash only if you plan to buy something from a kiosk or gelato cart inside.

The biggest mistake visitors make is rushing straight to the castle and skipping the rock garden, tucked just off the main path and easy to miss if you're not looking for it.
