Locals call it La Manquita, "the one-armed woman," because the south tower stops abruptly at the roofline while the north tower rises to over 80 metres. Money meant for finishing it reportedly went to help American colonists fight the British in the 1780s, or so the popular story goes. Either way, the lopsided silhouette is now the thing people photograph from Calle Molina Lario before they've even bought a ticket.

Construction dragged on for over 250 years, starting in 1528 on the site of a former mosque. That's why the building reads as a layered timeline rather than one clean style: a Renaissance floor plan, a baroque main facade, and Gothic-influenced side chapels all coexisting under one roof.

## Inside the nave

Once you're through the entrance on Molina Lario, the scale is what registers first. The vaulted ceilings run nearly 40 metres high, and the central nave stretches back toward a gilded main altar flanked by columns.

The real showpiece is the choir stalls, carved in mahogany and cedar by Pedro de Mena and other 17th-century sculptors. Look for the 42 individual figures of saints, each face distinct, worked into the seat backs and armrests over decades.

Side chapels branch off the ambulatory, each with its own retablo, painting, or tomb. The Capilla del Rosario and the Capilla de la Encarnación are worth slowing down for; both hold significant devotional art tied to the cathedral's original 16th-century commissions.

## The rooftop terraces

A separate ticket (usually bookable at the entrance or online) gets you onto the roof, where you walk between the buttresses and domes at close range. This is the best way to understand how the unfinished tower actually looks up close, stone scaffolding included.

The terrace route is guided and takes about 45 minutes on top of your nave visit. Wear flat shoes; the walkway is uneven stone and there are stairs with no lift access.

## The bell tower and museum

The finished tower holds a working bell dating to the 18th century, and the small cathedral museum displays vestments, silverware, and liturgical objects collected over centuries. It's a quieter stop after the nave, usually with far fewer people lingering.

Give the museum ten to fifteen minutes; it's compact rather than exhaustive, more a coda to the main visit than a destination on its own.

## Getting there and timing your visit

The cathedral sits in the pedestrian core of Distrito Centro, a short walk from Plaza de la Constitución and the Alcazaba fortress. Most visitors combine all three in a single morning.

- Nearest landmark: Plaza del Obispo, right outside the main facade
- On foot: 10-12 minutes from Malaga's Muelle Uno waterfront
- By bus: several city lines stop near Calle Molina Lario and Calle Cister

Because this is one of the most-reviewed religious sites in Spain, midday and early afternoon get dense with cruise excursion groups and coach tours. Mornings right after opening tend to be calmer, before the tour buses unload.

Always check the official website for current opening hours before you go, since they shift seasonally and for religious holidays like Holy Week, when the cathedral's schedule changes significantly.

## Visiting respectfully

This is an active place of worship as well as a monument, and that shapes how you should move through it. Shoulders and knees covered is the general expectation, and photography without flash is normally fine in the nave but restricted during Mass.

- Keep voices low near the altar and chapels where people may be praying
- Avoid visiting during scheduled Mass times if you only want to sightsee
- Buy tickets at the counter or online in advance; queues form fast once tour groups arrive
- Cash and card are both accepted at the ticket desk

If you want the building almost to yourself, aim for the first hour after opening on a weekday rather than a weekend morning, when local families and tourists both tend to arrive later in the day.
