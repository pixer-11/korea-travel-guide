## Two days of herding on frozen ground

This festival is a working contest. The 2026 Winter Horse Festival was set for two days in November in Batnorov, a rural sum (district) in Khentii Province. The programme announced horse-riding and herding competitions, which means herding culture judged by the people who live it.

The setting is open steppe in early winter. The grass has long since cured to tan, and snow often lies in patches. Mongolian horses are short, stocky and thick-coated, and they live outdoors all year. Riders usually compete in the deel, the long belted robe worn across the countryside. In November it is lined and padded.

Village festivals like this one have no grandstand or ticket barrier. Spectators stand along the course, and vehicles are parked in a loose ring on the grass.

## Reaching Batnorov from Ulaanbaatar

Khentii lies east of the capital. The route follows the paved eastern highway to Chinggis City, the provincial centre once called Öndörkhaan, about 330 km from Ulaanbaatar. Beyond it the roads get rougher. The last stretch to Batnorov is the kind 4x4 drivers handle every day.

- **Usual approach:** a hired vehicle with a driver, often a Russian UAZ van or a Land Cruiser, arranged through an Ulaanbaatar tour operator.
- **Overnight:** visitors usually sleep in Chinggis City on the way out, or in a family ger close to the village.
- **Time:** two days of driving there and back, plus the two festival days. That makes four days at minimum.

Bus services stop at Chinggis City. There is no public transport to the festival ground itself.

## Dressing for a November steppe

November in Khentii is properly cold. Daytime temperatures sit well below freezing, and the wind sweeps across the flat ground with nothing to stop it. Spectators spend hours standing still at a start or finish line, and that drains warmth faster than walking does.

- **Layers:** a thermal base, a fleece and a down jacket rated for deep cold are the norm.
- **Feet:** insulated winter boots matter more than anything else here.
- **Face and hands:** a balaclava or scarf, plus mittens worn over thin gloves for working a camera.
- **Power:** phone and camera batteries die quickly in the cold. Spares kept in an inside pocket last longer.

Inside a ger the stove keeps the air hot. Hosts usually serve suutei tsai, salty milk tea, as soon as guests sit down.

## Standing at the finish line in Batnorov

Herder etiquette applies on the festival ground too. No one walks behind a horse or steps across the line a herding event is using. Spectators leave the course open, and mounted riders always have right of way.

- **Photos:** herders are generally happy to be photographed. Asking first with a gesture is customary, especially for close portraits.
- **Horses:** a horse's head and a rider's tack are left alone unless the owner invites otherwise.
- **Offerings:** tea, food or snuff is accepted with the right hand, with the left hand supporting the right elbow.
- **Ger manners:** people move clockwise inside, and no one leans on the two central support posts.
- **Payment:** there are no card terminals out here. The driver or guide handles anything that needs paying.

A guide who speaks Mongolian turns watching into understanding. Herders will explain which horse belongs to which family and why a rider won, but only if someone can translate.
