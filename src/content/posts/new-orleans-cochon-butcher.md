## Why Cochon Butcher

You smell the smoked meat before you see the door. Cochon Butcher sits half-hidden down a narrow alley off Tchoupitoulas Street in the Warehouse District, part butcher shop, part sandwich counter, part bar.

It's the casual offshoot of Cochon next door, run by chefs Donald Link and Stephen Stryjewski, both deeply tied to Louisiana's Cajun and cochon-de-lait traditions. The name means "pig" in French, and the glass case up front backs that up — coils of house-made boudin, hunks of tasso, jars of pickled everything.

This isn't fine dining. It's a butcher counter that happens to make some of the best sandwiches in New Orleans.

## Getting there

The address is 930 Tchoupitoulas St, Ste B — look for the alley entrance, not a storefront on the main sidewalk. It's a short walk from the Ernest N. Morial Convention Center and an easy ten-minute stroll from the streetcar stops along the St. Charles line.

If you're coming from the French Quarter, it's roughly a 20-minute walk or a quick rideshare. Street parking exists but is tight; several pay lots dot the Warehouse District nearby.

## What to order

Everything on the menu leans toward pork, but not exclusively. The counter menu changes slightly over time, so treat these as reliable bets rather than gospel:

- **Muffuletta** — the New Orleans classic, olive salad and cured meats on house bread
- **Pork belly cubano** — pressed, salty, a little sweet from pickles
- **Wood-fired oyster po'boy** (when available) — smoky and briny in equal measure
- **Boudin balls** — crisp outside, rice-and-pork soft inside
- **Cochon Butcher plate** — a sampler of house charcuterie if you want to graze rather than commit to one sandwich

Order at the register, grab a number, and find a seat at the communal tables or the bar. Drinks include local beer and a short wine list, plus a bloody mary that regulars order with lunch.

## What the room feels like

Inside, it's tight and loud in the best way. Butcher paper covers small tables, the open kitchen clatters behind the counter, and the smell of cured pork and fresh bread fills the room.

At peak lunch — noon to 1:30pm on weekdays — every seat fills and the line snakes toward the door. It's a well-known stop for both locals grabbing lunch and visitors detouring from convention business next door, so don't expect a quiet hideaway.

## When to go

Cochon Butcher is open daily with no midday closure, which gives you flexibility. Lunch rush hits hard between noon and 1:30pm, especially on weekdays when convention center crowds spill over.

Dinner tends to be calmer, and a mid-afternoon visit — say 2pm to 4pm — is usually your quietest window for grabbing a table without waiting. Weekends bring a steady crowd all day, but rarely the same crunch as a Tuesday lunch hour.

## How to visit like a local

Order at the counter and pay there — tipping a dollar or two in the jar by the register is customary, since there's no table service. Nobody splits hairs over reservations here; there aren't any, and there's no need for one.

- Skip the noon rush if you can — 2pm to 4pm is far calmer
- Order the muffuletta to-go if you just want a fast bite; the packaging travels well
- Grab a few things from the butcher case (tasso, boudin, pickles) if you're cooking that night — locals treat it as a specialty grocery stop, not just a lunch counter
- Cash or card both work fine, but expect a short line even for one register at peak hours

The biggest mistake newcomers make is showing up right at noon expecting a quick in-and-out. Shift even 90 minutes later and the whole experience gets easier — shorter line, calmer room, same sandwich.
