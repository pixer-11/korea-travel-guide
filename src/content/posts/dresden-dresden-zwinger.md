Pale sandstone galleries close around a sunken lawn on all four sides. Stone satyrs and nymphs hold up the cornices, and fountains run down the middle. The Zwinger was built between 1710 and 1728 for Augustus the Strong, Elector of Saxony, as a stage for court festivals. Today it is the most-visited building in Dresden.

You can walk through the courtyard without a ticket. The museums inside the pavilions are paid and ticketed separately.

## A courtyard built for parties

The architect was Matthäus Daniel Pöppelmann. Most of the carved figures are by the sculptor Balthasar Permoser. Together they made the Zwinger read as one long piece of theatre: low single-storey galleries joined by taller pavilions, with sculpture covering nearly every surface.

The parts to find:

- **Kronentor (Crown Gate):** an onion-domed gateway topped by a gilded Polish crown. It stands over the old moat on the west side.
- **Wallpavillon:** the most heavily carved pavilion, with Hercules carrying the globe on top.
- **Nymphenbad (Nymphs' Bath):** a grotto-like fountain court hidden behind the Wallpavillon. Many visitors walk straight past it.
- **Glockenspielpavillon:** its carillon is made of Meissen porcelain bells, which play at set times through the day.

The Allied bombing of February 1945 left most of the Zwinger in ruins. What you see now was rebuilt over the following decades. Look for patches of lighter, newer stone set among the darker blocks.

## What's inside the pavilions

Three collections of the Staatliche Kunstsammlungen Dresden are housed here.

The **Gemäldegalerie Alte Meister** fills the Semperbau, the wing Gottfried Semper added to close the courtyard's northern side. Its best-known painting is Raphael's Sistine Madonna, and the two bored cherubs at the bottom of it may already be familiar to you. The gallery also holds Old Masters by Giorgione, Vermeer and Rembrandt.

The **Porzellansammlung** grew from Augustus's obsession with porcelain. It holds East Asian pieces and early Meissen ware, including life-sized porcelain animals.

The **Mathematisch-Physikalischer Salon** shows historic clocks, globes and scientific instruments, all housed in a pavilion of the Zwinger.

Each collection keeps its own hours, separate from the courtyard's. Check the SKD website before you plan around one.

## Timing your walk through

The courtyard gates are open every day from 6am to 10pm. That gives you a long window.

On weekdays, foot traffic stays steady and manageable from 7am to 10pm. Weekends are different. The courtyard is at its busiest from 11am to 8pm, when tour groups arrive from the Frauenkirche and the Elbe riverfront. If your only free day is a Saturday or Sunday, come before 11am or after 8pm.

An evening visit is the best reason to come late. The galleries are floodlit and the courtyard empties out well before the gates close.

For time, allow:

- 30 to 45 minutes for the courtyard and the terrace walk
- about two hours for the Old Masters gallery alone
- most of a day if you add the porcelain and instruments

## Getting in and around

The Zwinger sits at the western edge of the Altstadt. The Theaterplatz and Postplatz tram stops are both a few minutes' walk away, and many city lines pass through Postplatz.

From Dresden Hauptbahnhof it is roughly a 20-minute walk north. Head up Prager Straße, past the Altmarkt.

Arriving from Theaterplatz is the best approach. You walk through the arch under the Semperbau and the courtyard opens up in front of you all at once.

Everything nearby is within ten minutes on foot:

- the Semperoper, directly opposite on Theaterplatz
- the Residenzschloss, home of the Green Vault (Grünes Gewölbe)
- the Hofkirche (Cathedral)
- Brühlsche Terrasse above the Elbe
- the Frauenkirche, a few streets further east

## Walking the walls like a Dresdner

The upper terrace runs along the top of the galleries, and it is the best free viewpoint in the complex. Stairs near the Wallpavillon lead up. From the top you look down over the whole courtyard and its fountains, with the Kronentor across the moat.

Some practical habits help:

- **Buy museum tickets ahead.** SKD sells timed tickets online, and the weekend queue at the gallery desk is the slowest part of a visit.
- **Pay by card.** Museum desks in Dresden take cards. Keep a few coins anyway, because some public toilets charge.
- **Stay off the lawn.** The grass parterre is for looking at. People sit on the fountain edges and benches instead.
- **Be ready for German.** Signs and staff will greet you in German. A "Guten Tag" when you reach the ticket desk is the norm.

Walk the moat side at the end of your visit. The path along the Zwingerteich behind the Kronentor is green and quiet, and it shows you the outer walls that most visitors never see.
