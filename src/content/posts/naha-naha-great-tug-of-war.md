## A Rope as Thick as a Car

On tug-of-war day the rope fills the whole street. Route 58 is Naha's main artery, and it closes to traffic for the Great Tug-of-War. The rope is woven rice straw roughly 200 metres long, its main body taller than many of the people who stand beside it. In 1995 Guinness recognised it as the world's largest rope made of straw.

Hundreds of thinner side ropes branch off the main rope like ribs, and these are what people actually hold. The two halves face each other across the Kumoji intersection, one team for the East and one for the West, and tens of thousands of people usually pack in on both sides.

## Date, Tickets and the Kumoji Intersection

The 2026 pull was set for **October 11, 2026**, a Sunday. It usually falls on the long weekend around Japan's Sports Day holiday, as part of the wider Naha Festival.

- **Tickets:** none. Entry was free, and spectators could watch from the street and join the pull.
- **Venue:** Route 58 at the Kumoji intersection (久茂地交差点) in central Naha, a short walk from the western end of Kokusai-dori.
- **Timing:** Route 58 closes to traffic for the pull, and a typhoon can force a postponement.

October can still bring typhoons to Okinawa, and organisers have moved the pull before because of weather.

## The Kanuchi Pin and the Ceremony Before the Pull

The pulling comes last. First the two halves have to be joined. Traditionally each half ends in a big loop, and joining them means locking the loops together with a heavy wooden bar called the *kanuchi-bō* (カヌチ棒). Getting that bar through both loops usually takes a crowd of men lifting and shoving, and it is a show in itself.

Before that, the *shitaku* customarily step up onto the rope. These are performers in costume who play heroes from Ryukyu history and legend. Banners called *hata-gashira* go up on tall poles, carried by men who balance them against the wind. Gongs and drums keep a beat throughout. The tradition dates back to the Ryukyu Kingdom era, and the modern street version was revived in 1971.

## Getting a Grip: Joining the Pull

Anyone can pull, and almost everyone does. This is how it usually works on the ground:

- **Stand by a side rope.** Pullers push forward towards one of the thin branch ropes along the main body. Whichever side of the intersection you're on decides your team.
- **Wait for the signal.** Nobody pulls until the official start. When the gongs and the crowd's shout begin, everyone leans back and digs in.
- **The call.** The chant is *"Haiya! Haiya!"*, shouted in rhythm with each heave.
- **The crowd.** It is tightly packed and hot. Families with children and anyone who dislikes crushes tend to watch from the edges rather than the centre of the intersection.

The contest has a time limit, so it doesn't drag on until one side drops. If neither team moves the rope far enough, it can end in a draw. Closed shoes make sense, since the street is crowded and feet get trodden on. Gloves help with the straw, which is rough on bare hands.

## Riding the Yui Rail to Kumoji

The Yui Rail monorail is the easiest way in, because Route 58 closes to cars for the event. Two stations are within walking distance:

- **Kencho-mae Station (県庁前):** closest to the western end of Kokusai-dori and Kumoji. Route 58 is a short walk away.
- **Asahibashi Station (旭橋):** right by the Naha Bus Terminal, a short walk north up Route 58 to the intersection.

Trains get very full on tug-of-war day, and the ticket machines tend to have queues. An IC card such as OKICA or Suica avoids them, since the Yui Rail accepts the major nationwide IC cards. Driving isn't worth it: roads around the venue close and parking in central Naha fills up.

From hotels near Kokusai-dori, walking is simplest. The intersection is at the western end of the street, and the walk from most hotels on the strip is quicker than the train.

## A Piece of Rope to Take Home

The rope doesn't stay in one piece once the pull is over. People cut off lengths of straw to take home, because a piece of the Great Tug-of-War rope is believed to bring good luck and good health for the year. Plenty of locals keep a strand somewhere in the house.

Those who want a piece usually bring a small pair of scissors or a folding knife and stay near the rope after the finish. The main rope gets cut apart in a hurry. Blades can't go through airport security, so on a same-day flight they belong in checked luggage.

## Around the Pull: Kokusai-dori and the Naha Festival

The tug-of-war is the main event of the Naha Festival, held over the same weekend. During the festival, Kokusai-dori often hosts parades and Eisa drum dancing.

Walking east along Kokusai-dori leads into the covered arcades of Heiwa-dori and Ichiba-hondori. They lead to the Makishi Public Market, where the ground floor sells fish and pork and restaurants upstairs will cook what you buy.

For somewhere quieter, the stone-paved Tsuboya pottery street is a short walk further on. Shuri Castle is a few stops up the Yui Rail at Shuri Station, a good morning trip before an afternoon at the rope.
