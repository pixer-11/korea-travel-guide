## Why it's called a "buffet"

The name can trip up English speakers. Don't expect a sneeze guard and trays to help yourself from. In older Québécois usage, a *buffet* is a modest lunch counter or snack bar: a cheap neighbourhood place to eat, not an all-you-can-eat spread.

The first half of the name fits the street. Rue Saint-Paul has long been Quebec City's antiques row. Dealers' windows line the blocks between the Old Port and the Gare du Palais, so "L'Antiquaire" tells you where you are as much as what you're eating.

## What lands on the table

Generous portions are the main thing to plan around. The kitchen's reputation rests on big plates, so if you're heading for a long lunch in Upper Town later, think about sharing or ordering lighter.

## Ordering and paying in Québécois

A few words go a long way here. Staff usually greet you with "Bonjour" or the bilingual "Bonjour-Hi". If you answer in English, most people in the Old Town will switch. Starting in French is still the polite move.

Meal names trip up visitors. In Quebec:

- **Déjeuner** means breakfast, not lunch
- **Dîner** is lunch
- **Souper** is the evening meal

So "déjeuner toute la journée" on a sign means breakfast all day, which is exactly what this place does.

Tipping follows the Canadian norm: around 15–20% on the pre-tax bill is standard for table service. Most restaurants bring a card terminal to your table. It will ask you to choose a tip percentage before you tap, so have a number in mind before it arrives. Ask for the bill with "L'addition, s'il vous plaît". Servers don't usually bring it until you do.

## The streets around 95 Rue Saint-Paul

The diner sits in the Old Port district of Lower Town (Basse-Ville). It's a flat, walkable grid of stone buildings below the cliff that holds Upper Town and the Château Frontenac. Quebec City has no metro. Most visitors arrive on foot, and the walking is what makes this a good base for a morning.

A loop that starts or ends here:

- **Rue Saint-Paul antique shops:** browse the windows west along the street toward the Gare du Palais train station
- **Musée de la civilisation:** a few minutes away on Rue Dalhousie, good for a rainy afternoon
- **Place Royale and Notre-Dame-des-Victoires:** the small square and stone church where the city began
- **Quartier Petit-Champlain:** narrow pedestrian lanes, the Escalier Casse-Cou stairway and the Old Quebec Funicular up to Dufferin Terrace
- **The Old Port waterfront:** the promenade along the St. Lawrence and Louise Basin

Arriving by train or intercity bus at the Gare du Palais puts you a short walk along Rue Saint-Paul from the door. That makes the diner a practical first meal, or a last one before a departure.
