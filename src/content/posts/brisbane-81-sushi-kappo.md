The name points to Japan before you sit down. +81 is Japan's international dialling code. Kappo is a style of Japanese dining where the chef prepares food in front of the guests and talks across the counter, so there is less distance than in a formal kaiseki room.

## How omakase runs here

Omakase means "I leave it to you." You don't order from a list; the chef decides the sequence. At a sushi counter that usually means nigiri arriving one piece at a time, each shaped and seasoned just before it reaches you.

## Counter manners for a 12-seat room

With twelve guests sharing one chef, everyone notices when one person disrupts things. These habits keep the evening running smoothly:

- **Book well ahead.** A room this small fills quickly, and seatings are timed. Confirm hours and the reservation method before you go.
- **Arrive on time.** Late arrivals at a shared-start omakase either miss courses or hold up eleven other people.
- **Declare allergies when booking**, not at the counter. The chef plans the fish before service starts.
- **Eat each piece straight away.** The rice is at its intended temperature for moments. Fingers or chopsticks are both correct for nigiri.
- **Go easy on soy.** If a piece arrives already brushed, it needs nothing more.
- **Skip strong perfume.** At a counter this close, it competes with the fish.

Paying works the way it does across Australia: cards and contactless are standard, and tipping is not expected. Leave something extra only if you want to. Asking the chef about a piece is part of kappo, so talk to him across the counter.
