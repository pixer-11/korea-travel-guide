## Eight concerts over six weeks

This is a slow festival. Most music festivals pack everything into one long weekend. The Tbilisi Baroco Festival spreads eight concerts across six weeks, from October 26 to December 9, 2026. Alongside them runs a scientific conference, which is how you can tell this is a musicians' and scholars' festival as much as an audience one.

The repertoire is baroque, meaning roughly the 17th and early 18th centuries. Think Bach, Handel, Vivaldi, Corelli and their contemporaries. Because the concerts are spaced out, most visitors catch one or two during a trip rather than the whole series. Residents tend to treat it as a season of evenings across the late autumn.

## The Grand Hall on Griboedov Street

Every concert takes place in the Grand Hall of the Tbilisi State Conservatoire. This is Georgia's main music academy, named after Vano Sarajishvili and founded in 1917. Its building sits on Griboedov Street, one block back from Rustaveli Avenue. It has long been the city's home for serious chamber and orchestral music.

The setting suits baroque music. These pieces were written for courts, churches and salons, not stadiums. A conservatoire concert hall gives you that: an audience close to the stage, and acoustics designed for unamplified strings, voices and harpsichord. It also suits the conference, which belongs in an academic building.

## Getting to the Conservatoire

The walk from the metro is short and on flat ground. The nearest station is **Rustaveli**, on Line 1 of the Tbilisi Metro. From there you walk along Rustaveli Avenue and turn onto Griboedov Street. The whole trip takes a few minutes.

Other ways to get there:

- **On foot from Freedom Square:** stroll up Rustaveli Avenue, passing the Georgian National Museum and the Rustaveli Theatre.
- **By taxi:** Bolt and Yandex Go work across the city. Drivers know the Conservatoire by its Georgian name, *Konservatoria*.
- **From the Old Town:** it is a walk of about 20–25 minutes from Meidan, or a single taxi ride.

The metro takes contactless bank cards at the barriers, so you don't need to buy a travel card for one evening.

## Concert-night manners at the Konservatoria

Tbilisi audiences take classical music seriously, and the rituals are old-school. Like most halls in the former Soviet sphere, the Conservatoire has a cloakroom. Concertgoers check their coats before taking their seats. From November onward you will be wearing a heavy coat, so expect to queue at the cloakroom again at the end.

A few habits that help:

- **Dress:** smart-casual is the norm. Nobody expects black tie, but people make an effort, and older concertgoers often dress up.
- **Applause:** clapping waits until the end of a full work, not between movements. Baroque suites have many short movements, so take your cue from the hall.
- **Flowers:** handing a bouquet to a soloist at the curtain call is a common Georgian gesture. Many audience members bring one for performers they admire.
- **Phones:** photography during the performance is frowned upon. The applause at the end is the usual moment for a quick picture.

Card payment is common in central Tbilisi. A little cash in lari still helps if you are buying flowers from a street vendor on the way in.

## Dates, programme and tickets

The festival runs from October 26 to December 9, 2026. Its eight concerts and the scientific conference are all held at the Tbilisi State Conservatoire Grand Hall. The organiser publishes the concert programme, performers and ticket details on its official channels. That is the place for exact evening dates and seating.

Visitors in town for only a few days usually pick the concert that falls during their stay. They don't plan the trip around a particular programme. The conference mostly draws musicologists and performers. Visitors with a specialist interest in historical performance often attend sessions alongside the concerts.

## Before and after the music

Rustaveli Avenue is the best part of the evening's setting. Before a concert you can walk past the Opera and Ballet Theatre with its Moorish-style facade. From there it is a short stroll to Freedom Square. The Georgian National Museum on the avenue is another good stop in the hours before a performance.

After the final bows, the side streets of Sololaki to the south are full of wine bars and restaurants serving khachapuri and khinkali. Late-autumn nights in Tbilisi are cold, especially in December. Most people end the walk somewhere warm with a glass of Saperavi rather than lingering outdoors.
