## Two runs, one day, tight gates

Slalom is the shortest and most technical discipline in alpine racing. Gates are set close together, so skiers turn constantly and clip the poles with shins and forearms as they pass. From the finish you hear a steady rattle of plastic on plastic before each racer comes into view.

The format is fixed by FIS rules:

- **Run one:** the full start list skis the course.
- **Run two:** the fastest 30 return on a reset course, starting in reverse order of their first-run ranking.
- **Result:** the two times combined decide the podium.

That reverse order means the race leader comes down last. The final few minutes usually hold most of the drama.

## Courchevel on December 22

Courchevel is a cluster of villages on one mountainside in the Tarentaise valley, Savoie. It forms part of the Trois Vallées, which links to Méribel and Val Thorens. In descending order of altitude, the villages are:

- Courchevel 1850
- Courchevel Moriond (1650)
- Courchevel Village (1550)
- Le Praz (1300)

The resort's women's World Cup races have traditionally used the Émile Allais slope above Courchevel 1850. FIS publishes the venue, start lists and results for this edition on its website.

December 22 falls at the start of the French Christmas school holidays. Resort lodging and lift queues are typically at their busiest that week.

## Reaching the finish area

Most visitors finish the journey by road up the valley. The nearest mainline rail station is Moûtiers-Salins-Brides-les-Bains, about 25km below the resort. From Moûtiers, buses and transfers climb the switchbacks to the villages.

International visitors usually fly into Geneva, Lyon Saint-Exupéry or Chambéry. Courchevel also has its own short, steeply sloped altiport, which serves light aircraft and helicopters only.

Visitors staying lower down, in Le Praz or La Tania, usually ride the lifts up to 1850 rather than driving. Parking in 1850 is limited and fills during holiday weeks.

## Watching from the snow like a regular

Experienced spectators dress for standing still. You stand on packed snow above 1,800m in late December, for runs spaced hours apart. Fans usually bring:

- insulated boots rather than ski boots, if they're staying in the finish area
- a cowbell or a flag (French tricolours sit alongside Swiss, Austrian, Slovenian and Scandinavian ones)
- sunglasses or goggles, since glare off the snow is strong even on overcast days
- a lift pass, if they plan to ski between runs

Many fans ski in the morning and follow run one from the finish area. They then usually have lunch in 1850 and return for run two. Crowds cheer every racer, not only the home skiers. The loudest noise of the day usually greets whoever crosses the line in the lead after the final starters.

Course-side fences and nets are there for safety. Spectators stay behind them and keep off the race piste, which is closed to public skiing on race day.
