Two storeys of arcaded stone wrap around a courtyard planted with trees. This was the Seminario Conciliar de San José, built in the 18th century to train priests. Since the early 20th century it has housed Jalisco's main regional museum. The building is half the reason to come. You walk out of the downtown traffic and the noise falls away behind the thick walls.

The other half is the collection. It runs from fossils and the deep past of western Mexico through the colonial centuries to the state's modern history. Give it two hours and you will understand the city outside a lot better.

## A seminary turned storehouse of Jalisco

The layout is easy to follow. Galleries open off the cloister walkways on both floors, so you circle the courtyard as you move through time. Ground-floor rooms tend to hold the older material, and the upper floor carries the story forward.

Expect the rooms to feel like a traditional state museum: glass cases, wall texts and room after room of objects. Labels are mainly in Spanish. A translation app with a camera function makes the long texts readable.

## The mammoth on the ground floor

Most visitors make straight for the paleontology hall. Its centrepiece is the mounted skeleton of a mammoth, a reminder that the Valley of Atemajac was grazing land long before anyone built here. Children usually stand underneath it for a while.

It also gives you a sense of scale before the archaeology rooms. Seeing the bones first resets your idea of how old this ground really is.

## Shaft tombs, clay dogs and the colonial galleries

Western Mexico had no pyramids on the scale of Teotihuacan. What it had were shaft tombs: deep vertical burials filled with ceramic offerings. The museum's archaeology rooms are strong on this tradition. Look for:

- Hollow clay figures of warriors, musicians and couples from Jalisco, Nayarit and Colima
- The plump red-clay dogs associated with Colima
- Model scenes of village life and ceremonies

Upstairs, the mood shifts to oil and gilt. The colonial painting galleries hold religious canvases from the viceregal period, hung in long rooms that suit the building's old purpose. Further rooms cover Jalisco's history after independence and the region's Indigenous peoples, including the Wixárika (Huichol).

If time is short, choose. The archaeology rooms are what this museum does that others in the city do not.

## Saturday afternoon is the quiet slot

The crowd pattern is clear. On weekends, the busiest stretch runs from 11am to 2pm, when families and tour groups pile into the downtown sights at the same time. Arrive in that window and the narrow cloister rooms fill up fast.

The quietest weekend hour is 4pm to 5pm. That only works on Saturday, because on Sunday the doors close at 4pm, two hours earlier than the rest of the week. Arriving at 4pm on a Saturday still leaves you two hours before the 6pm close.

Remember that the museum is closed on Mondays. That catches out plenty of people planning a long-weekend city break.

## Getting to Calle Liceo

The museum stands on Calle Liceo, on the east side of the Rotonda de los Jaliscienses Ilustres. That is the plaza of columns honouring the state's notable figures, immediately north of the Cathedral. If you can see the Cathedral's twin yellow-tiled spires, you are about two minutes away.

Line 3 of the light rail stops at Guadalajara Centro station, beside the Cathedral. From there it is a short walk north across the plazas. Line 2 runs under Avenida Juárez a few blocks south if you are coming from the west or east of the city. Downtown parking is tight, so a ride-hailing car or the train is simpler than driving.

## How the visit works at the ticket desk

The ticket desk is just inside the entrance. Admission is posted there and on the INAH website, which runs the museum. Don't rely on old prices online, because these change. A few habits help:

- Carry some cash in pesos. Small purchases go more smoothly with it, even where cards are taken.
- Check the rules on bags. Mexican federal museums often ask you to leave large bags at a cloakroom near the entrance.
- Ask before you film. Photography rules can differ between permanent and temporary rooms, and staff will tell you.
- Keep your voice down in the cloister. Sound carries off the stone arches, and school groups get shushed too.

The gift shop near the exit stocks books on Jalisco history and archaeology, plus reproductions. Spanish-language titles are the norm, but the photo books work in any language.

## Pairing it with the rest of the Centro Histórico

The museum is part of a tight cluster of sights you can walk between in minutes. A half-day loop could take in:

- **Guadalajara Cathedral**, just south across the Rotonda
- **Plaza de Armas** and the **Palacio de Gobierno**, where José Clemente Orozco's mural of Miguel Hidalgo fills the main stairwell
- **Plaza de la Liberación** and the neoclassical **Teatro Degollado** behind the Cathedral
- **Plaza Tapatía**, running east to the **Hospicio Cabañas** and its Orozco murals

A sensible order on a weekday is the museum first, at its 9am opening, while you are fresh enough for wall texts. Then head outside for the plazas and murals. On a Saturday, do it the other way round: the plazas in the morning and the museum from 4pm.
