## A city dressed in blue, red and gold

Phnom Penh puts on its formal clothes for this one. The day marks the coronation of King Norodom Sihamoni, crowned on 29 October 2004 after the abdication of his father, King Norodom Sihanouk. In 2026 it was set for October 29, with flags, portraits and evening celebrations planned in the capital's public squares.

On this holiday the national flag, with its blue bands and white Angkor Wat on red, is traditionally strung along the main boulevards. Framed portraits of the King go up on government buildings, gateways and shopfronts. After dark, the squares host concerts and fireworks.

## Where the celebrations gather

The obvious focus is the Royal Palace on Sothearos Boulevard. Its gold-tipped spires face the riverside park where the Tonle Sap meets the Mekong. Sisowath Quay, the long promenade running north from the palace, is where Phnom Penh walks in the evening on any day of the year. On a national celebration it becomes the city's front row.

Other spots worth walking:

- **Independence Monument**, the lotus-shaped tower at the junction of Norodom and Sihanouk boulevards
- **The park strip south of the monument**, toward the statue of King Father Norodom Sihanouk
- **Wat Phnom**, the hilltop temple at the north end of the riverside, for decorated approach roads

Specific concert stages and fireworks locations vary from year to year.

## Tickets, timing and what is open

The decorations and public squares are open spaces you simply walk into, with no ticket needed.

Coronation Day is a Cambodian public holiday. Ministries, banks and many offices close, while markets, cafés and riverside restaurants generally keep trading. The Royal Palace and Silver Pagoda can close to visitors around royal ceremonies.

## Joining the evening crowds respectfully

This is a royal occasion, and Cambodians treat the King's image seriously. Portraits deserve the same respect as a national flag. Posing jokingly beside them is frowned upon, and touching or defacing them is a serious offence.

A few local habits:

- **Palace dress, even when passing.** Shoulders and knees covered is the Royal Palace standard, and it reads as respect near any official site.
- **Stillness during the anthem.** When "Nokor Reach" plays at a ceremony, people stop walking and stay quiet until it ends.
- **Small notes.** US dollars and Cambodian riel circulate side by side. Street vendors often give change in riel, and many stalls also accept KHQR codes through local banking apps.
- **Ride apps.** PassApp and Grab work across the city and avoid haggling with tuk-tuk drivers in a crowd.
- **Meeting points.** Mobile signal can strain when the quay fills, so a landmark such as the Chaktomuk Conference Hall makes a useful meeting point.

Roads around the palace and the riverfront often close to traffic on celebration nights. Walking the last stretch is usually easier than taking a tuk-tuk to the barriers.

## Making a day of it

The daylight hours suit the sights the holiday is built around. The National Museum of Cambodia sits beside the Royal Palace in a red sandstone-coloured Khmer-style building, holding Angkorian sculpture. Wat Ounalom, the seat of Cambodian Buddhism, faces the quay a short walk north.

Late October is the tail of the rainy season. Afternoon downpours are still possible, so a compact umbrella is more useful than a sunhat in the evening.

## Other dates to know around it

October and November are busy months on Cambodia's calendar. King Father Norodom Sihanouk's Commemoration Day falls on October 15, and Independence Day is November 9, centred on Independence Monument. The Water Festival, Bon Om Touk, follows the lunar calendar and can land near these dates, bringing boat races to the same riverfront.
