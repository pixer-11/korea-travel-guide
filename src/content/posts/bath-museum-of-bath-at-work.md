## A Georgian Tennis Court Full of Lathes

Push open the door on Julian Road and the room keeps going upwards. The building went up in 1777 as a real tennis court, built for the indoor ancestor of lawn tennis that Georgian Bath's wealthier residents played. It's a tall, plain space of Bath stone, and today it's packed with workbenches, belts, pulleys and shop fittings.

That gap between what the building was made for and what fills it now is the reason to come. Most of Bath sells you the city's leisure: the baths, the balls, the terraces. This museum is about the people who made things, mended things and bottled things in the city. Fewer visitors find their way up here, so you usually get room to look properly.

## Mr Bowler's Back Premises

The centrepiece is the business of J.B. Bowler. He was a Victorian engineer, brass founder and mineral water manufacturer who opened in Bath in 1872. The firm kept trading until 1969, and over that century it seems to have thrown almost nothing away.

When it closed, the contents were saved and reassembled here as a walk-through set of rooms. You move through the engineering shop, the brass foundry area and the soft drinks factory, where fizzy drinks were mixed and bottled. Along the way you see the office, with its paperwork and ledgers, as it might have looked mid-shift.

What makes it absorbing is the clutter. Tools hang where a worker could reach them, and stock sits on shelves rather than behind glass. It feels less like a display and more like a firm that stepped out for lunch. Give this section the most time, and read the labels on the drinks side, where the recipes and bottling process are explained.

## The Workbench, the Car and the Stone

Beyond Bowler's rooms the museum widens out to other Bath trades. The ones to look for:

- **The cabinet maker's workshop**, a reconstructed bench-and-tools room showing how furniture was made by hand.
- **The Horstmann car**, built by the Bath firm of the same name. It's a reminder that this spa town once had a motor industry.
- **Bath stone displays**, covering the quarrying and mining of the honey-coloured limestone the Royal Crescent and nearly everything else here is built from.

The stone section pays off once you're back outside. It gives you a working-class backstory for the façades you'll pass on the walk downhill.

## Finding Julian Road

The museum is in the upper town, on the slope north of the city centre. From Bath Spa railway station or the bus station it's an uphill walk of roughly 20 to 25 minutes. Head through the centre towards the Circus, then carry on north until you reach Julian Road.

If you're already at the Royal Crescent or the Circus, it's only a few minutes further on foot. Parking in central Bath is limited, and the city runs park and ride services from its edges. Leaving the car outside the centre and walking up is usually easier than hunting for a space on these residential streets.

## Pacing an Afternoon Here

Google lists opening hours of 10:30am to 5pm, seven days a week. Smaller independent museums sometimes change their hours in winter, though, so confirm on the museum's own website before a special trip.

Most people need about 90 minutes, and longer if you read every label in the Bowler rooms. Avoid arriving after about 3:30pm if you want an unhurried look before closing. A visit fits neatly into a morning or afternoon in the upper town. Pair it with the Crescent and the Circus and you get a half-day in the elegant terraces and the workrooms that kept them supplied.

## Getting the Most From a Small, Volunteer-Minded Museum

This is an independent, charity-run museum, not a big council attraction, and that shapes how you get the most out of it.

- **Pay at the front desk.** The current admission price is published on the museum's official website, so check there rather than relying on old listings.
- **Ask questions.** The people on duty often know the collection in detail, and a question about a particular machine can get you far more than the label says.
- **Look closely, touch carefully.** Many objects are on open display rather than behind glass. Keep your hands off unless a sign invites you.
- **Tipping isn't part of museum culture in Britain.** If you enjoyed it, buying from the shop or making a donation helps a small trust directly.

Card payment is normal across Bath. Older visitors and anyone with mobility needs should remember the uphill approach and plan the walk accordingly.

## Linking It to the Upper Town

The museum is at the top of Bath's best-known stretch of Georgian architecture, so it's easy to build a loop around it.

- **The Royal Crescent** and the lawn in front of it, a short walk west.
- **The Circus**, the ring of townhouses a few minutes south.
- **The Assembly Rooms** on Bennett Street, where Georgian society held its balls.
- **Royal Victoria Park**, below the Crescent, for open space after a visit spent indoors.
- **The Museum of Bath Architecture** on the Vineyards, which goes deeper into how the city was designed and built.

Start at the Museum of Bath at Work, then let gravity carry you down past the Circus and into the centre. You'll be looking at that stone differently by then.
