## Finding the entrance

The tower stands on a traffic island, so you can't just walk up from the street. An underground pedestrian passage, entered from any of the four surrounding corners, surfaces directly beneath the structure. Signage inside is bilingual, and the walk takes under three minutes. Look for the ticket window at the base before heading up; there's no street-level door.

## What the Ming dynasty structure holds

Built in 1384, the tower originally stood half a mile west and was moved to its current spot in 1582 to mark the city's true center. The wooden superstructure sits on a brick platform roughly 9 meters high, with the whole structure reaching about 36 meters.

Inside, a small museum displays a set of replica bronze bells (the original Ming bell, cracked, is preserved separately) plus Qing-era furniture and calligraphy. Recorded bell-ringing and drum performances run several times a day at scheduled intervals, usually mid-morning and early afternoon.

The real draw for most visitors is the encircling balcony. From it you get a straight-line view down all four of Xi'an's main avenues, plus a clear look at the Drum Tower's rooftop across the square. Come at dusk and the tower is lit gold against the darkening skyline, visible from blocks away.

## Getting there and timing it right

The tower sits above Xi'an Bell Tower subway station, Line 2, with exits feeding straight into the underpass. It's also walkable from the Bell Tower Hotel area and about 10 minutes on foot from the South Gate of the city wall.

Despite the central address, this is one of Xi'an's less crowded major sights; tour groups tend to cluster at the Terracotta Army or the Muslim Quarter food streets instead. Weekday mornings right after opening are close to empty.

- Combine with: Drum Tower (5-minute walk), Muslim Quarter entrance at Beiyuanmen (2 minutes), city wall South Gate (10-minute walk)
- Time needed: 30-45 minutes for the tower alone
- Best light: late afternoon into dusk, when the exterior lighting comes on

## Buying tickets and reading the space

Tickets are sold at the underpass ticket window; a combined ticket with the Drum Tower is usually available and works out cheaper than two separate entries. Check current pricing at the counter, as rates shift seasonally.

There's no elevator, so the climb to the balcony is by stairs only, narrow and steep in sections. Wear shoes you can grip stairs in; there's a coat check but no large-bag storage inside.

Photography is allowed everywhere except directly beside the bell and drum artifacts, where signage marks the limit. Locals passing through the underpass use it as a shortcut between the four quadrants of the old city, not as a tourist stop, so don't expect crowds to thin the flow, just less camera-pointing than you'd see topside.
