## A piano in the city that trained him

Einaudi comes back to Milan on December 2, 2026. The composer was born in Turin in 1955, but his training happened here, at the Conservatorio Giuseppe Verdi, before he went on to study with Luciano Berio.

That makes a Milan date feel a little like a homecoming. The setting suits it too. Teatro dal Verme is a horseshoe-shaped hall with red seats and tiered balconies, much closer in scale to a concert room than to an arena.

## Teatro dal Verme, the room itself

The theatre sits on Via San Giovanni sul Muro, a block off Foro Buonaparte. It opened in 1872 and was named after Count Francesco Dal Verme, who built it.

Its past is operatic. Puccini's first opera, *Le Villi*, premiered here in 1884, and Leoncavallo's *Pagliacci* followed in 1892. Today it is home to the I Pomeriggi Musicali orchestra and hosts a steady calendar of chamber, symphonic and crossover concerts.

The seating splits into the *platea* (stalls) and the *galleria* (balcony). For a solo or small-ensemble piano programme, the galleria gives you a clear look down at the keyboard and the pianist's hands.

## What an Einaudi evening sounds like

His music is built from repetition. Short piano figures loop and shift slowly, often with strings or electronics layered underneath. The pieces people come for are well known:

- **"Nuvole Bianche"** and **"Una Mattina"**, from the 2004 album *Una Mattina*
- **"Le Onde"**, title track of his 1996 album
- **"Experience"**, from *In a Time Lapse* (2013)
- **"Fly"**, which reached a huge audience through the French film *Intouchables*

He also scored *Nomadland* and *The Father*, and in 2016 he played *Elegy for the Arctic* on a floating platform for Greenpeace. Which pieces make the Milan programme is his choice on the night. Expect an audience that includes classical regulars, film fans and people who found him through streaming playlists.

## Getting to Via San Giovanni sul Muro

The theatre is in central Milan, so the metro does most of the work:

- **Cairoli Castello (M1, red line):** the closest stop, a two- or three-minute walk.
- **Cordusio (M1):** about five minutes away along Via Dante.
- **Cadorna (M1, M2 and the Malpensa Express):** roughly ten minutes on foot, handy if you are coming from the airport.
- **Duomo (M1, M3):** a 10–15 minute walk down pedestrianised Via Dante.

Early December evenings in Milan are cold and often foggy. The walk along Via Dante is lit for Christmas by then, and Castello Sforzesco's tower closes the view at one end.

## Seats, coats and applause at the dal Verme

Italian theatres keep a few customs. Most of them are about the coat you arrive in.

- **The guardaroba.** Theatres in Italy typically run a cloakroom. Concertgoers in December usually hand over heavy coats instead of folding them onto their laps in a narrow row.
- **Doors and seating.** Regulars tend to reach the foyer with time to spare, because ushers seat latecomers only at a natural break, if at all.
- **Phones and photos.** Filming during the music is frowned on in a hall this intimate. Plenty of people raise a phone for the encore or the bows instead.
- **Applause.** Einaudi often runs pieces into one another. The audience usually waits for his hands to leave the keys before clapping.
- **Dress.** Milanese audiences dress neatly, with dark coats and smart shoes. Black tie is not expected.

Tickets carry a sector (*platea* or *galleria*), a row (*fila*) and a seat number (*posto*). Showing the ticket to an usher is the normal way to find your place.

## Before and after in Brera and the Castello

The theatre sits between two of central Milan's best evening areas. Brera is a short walk north, a grid of cobbled streets full of bars where the city's *aperitivo* ritual plays out: a drink, usually a Campari spritz or a Negroni, with small plates alongside.

Milanese dinner runs late, so eating after the concert is completely normal. Corso Magenta and the streets around Cadorna have trattorie serving *risotto alla milanese* and *cotoletta*.

If you have the daytime free, Castello Sforzesco is a few minutes away and houses Michelangelo's unfinished *Rondanini Pietà*. Behind it, Parco Sempione runs up to the Arco della Pace.

The December 2 date also falls just before a big week for music in the city. On December 7, the feast of Sant'Ambrogio, Milan traditionally opens La Scala's opera season.
