## Two curved towers and a lit tree

City Hall frames the whole show. Viljo Revell's two curved concrete towers wrap around a low saucer-shaped council chamber, and Nathan Phillips Square spreads out in front of them. That open plaza is where Cavalcade of Lights happens. For the 2026–27 edition it runs from **November 28, 2026 to January 7, 2027**.

The season opens with Toronto's official tree lighting and a fireworks show. After that, the square stays busy every night with skating and live performances into early January. The tradition goes back decades: the City of Toronto first held Cavalcade of Lights in 1967, two years after the new City Hall opened.

## How the season unfolds

Opening night is the one people plan around. The big tree is switched on, fireworks go up over the towers, and the square fills with families, office workers and visitors. The crowd faces the stage and the tree, with the towers lit up behind them.

After opening night the pace changes. On an ordinary weeknight you get:

- the lit tree and the decorations around the square
- skaters on the rink under the arches
- live performances on the programme
- the three-dimensional TORONTO sign, with a steady line of people taking photos in front of it

On the busiest nights the event feels like a festival. On quieter ones it is closer to a neighbourhood rink that happens to be lit up for the holidays. Both are worth seeing. The season ends on January 7, 2027.

## Tickets and the official schedule

You don't need a ticket for the square. Cavalcade of Lights has always been a free public event in an open civic plaza, so people simply walk in from Queen Street or Bay Street. Skate rentals and anything sold on site are separate, and those prices are posted at the rink.

The City of Toronto runs the event. It publishes the opening-night programme, the performance schedule and the rink details on its official website, toronto.ca. That page has the times for each night's performances, which change across the season.

## Getting to Nathan Phillips Square

The square sits at 100 Queen Street West, bounded by Queen, Bay and Hagerman streets and University Avenue's eastern edge near Osgoode Hall. Getting there by transit is simpler than driving, especially on opening night.

- **Subway:** Osgoode Station (Line 1) is one block west at Queen and University. Queen Station (Line 1) is one block east at Queen and Yonge, beside the Eaton Centre.
- **Streetcar:** the 501 Queen runs right along the square's southern edge.
- **Underground:** the PATH network links City Hall to the Sheraton Centre and the surrounding towers, which helps on bitter nights.
- **Parking:** an underground garage sits beneath the square. On opening night, though, most attendees use transit, because roads around Queen and Bay slow to a crawl.

## The rink under the Freedom Arches

The rink is what makes this event different from other tree lightings. In summer, the square's reflecting pool sits under a row of concrete arches. These are the Freedom Arches, built to honour people who struggled for freedom and democracy. Every winter the pool is frozen into a public skating rink, so you skate beneath the same arches.

During Cavalcade of Lights the arches are lit, and the tree glows at one end of the ice. City Hall's towers rise over the north side. From the rink, the TORONTO sign and the illuminated arches are both in view at once, which is the photo most visitors take home.

Toronto in late November and December is usually below freezing after dark. The wind also funnels across the open plaza. Regulars on the rink wear proper gloves and a hat, because the ice is no warmer than the air above it.

## Skating and standing like a Torontonian

Toronto's outdoor rinks have their own unwritten rules. On the City Hall ice, skaters usually go counter-clockwise in one steady loop. Slower skaters and beginners keep to the edge near the boards, and people stopping to fix a lace step off the ice. Anyone going against the flow, or stopping dead in the middle for a photo, gets a lot of looks.

Skate rentals and sharpening are offered at the rink building on the square's west side. On busy nights the rental line moves faster for people who bring their own skates. Locals often lace up on the benches and leave their bags with a friend who isn't skating.

On opening night, people who want a clear view of the tree usually get to the square well before the lighting and claim a spot facing the stage. Others stay at the edges near Queen Street so they can leave easily when the fireworks finish. Payment almost everywhere downtown is by tap card or phone, so few people carry much cash.

## After the lights: around City Hall

There is plenty within a few minutes' walk to fill the rest of the evening.

- **Old City Hall**, the Romanesque sandstone building with the clock tower, stands across Bay Street. Its facade is worth a look at night.
- **Osgoode Hall**, home of the Law Society of Ontario, sits behind its iron fence just west of the square.
- **CF Toronto Eaton Centre** runs north from Queen Station, with its long glass-roofed galleria. People often go there to warm up after skating.
- **Queen Street West** heads west past Osgoode toward independent shops, bars and restaurants.
- **The Art Gallery of Ontario** is about a ten-minute walk northwest along Dundas Street. Frank Gehry's glass-and-timber facade faces the street.

Most visitors spend one to two hours at the square on an ordinary night: a loop or two on the ice, a look at the tree, a photo at the sign. Opening night takes longer, because the crowd moves slowly before the fireworks and after them.
