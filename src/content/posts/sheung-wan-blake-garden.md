Blake Garden sits at the point where Kui In Fong meets Po Hing Fong, a pocket of paved terraces stepped into the slope above Hollywood Road. It's not a destination anyone flies to Hong Kong for, and that's exactly the point. This is a park built for the people who live on the hill above it, and visiting it means slowing down to their pace for half an hour.

## The garden's split personality

Blake Garden is really two spaces joined by a staircase. The lower terrace has a hard-surfaced court where elderly residents run through slow tai chi sets or badminton knock-ups most mornings.

The upper level, reached by a short flight of steps, holds shaded seating under mature trees and a children's play area. Neither half is large. What they share is a view down the terraced slope typical of Sheung Wan's old hillside layout, where staircases substitute for streets.

## Why it's named for a governor you've never heard of

The park takes its name from Sir Henry Blake, governor of Hong Kong from 1898 to 1903, a period when the colonial administration was laying out small recreation grounds across the newly dense hillside districts. Unlike Hollywood Road or Possession Street nearby, there's no plaque or monument making a fuss about this history.

The garden simply carries the name on its street signs, one of several colonial-era governors quietly commemorated in small Sheung Wan parks. Knowing this adds a little context, but it's not why you'd come.

## Reading the morning shift

Public parks in dense Hong Kong neighbourhoods run on an unspoken schedule, and Blake Garden is no exception. Early morning belongs to older residents doing stretching routines or walking slow laps before the day's heat sets in.

By late morning the court sees a shift toward domestic helpers with young children, using the play equipment before lunch. If you're purely sightseeing, this rhythm is worth watching rather than interrupting; find a bench on the upper terrace and let the neighbourhood carry on around you.

## When to actually show up

The measured crowd patterns here are useful and specific. On weekdays, the calmest window is 8am to 10am, before the morning exercise groups thin out and before any midday lunch-break foot traffic builds.

Weekends compress that calm window further, to roughly 8am and 9am, after which local families start arriving for the play area. Avoid Saturday and Sunday afternoons between 4pm and 9pm if you want the space to yourself; that's when it's busiest, as after-work and after-school crowds converge.

- **Quietest weekday window:** 8am–10am
- **Quietest weekend window:** 8am–9am
- **Busiest period to avoid:** weekends, 4pm–9pm

## Getting there without a map meltdown

Blake Garden has no grand entrance, which trips up visitors expecting a gate or a sign visible from Hollywood Road. The address is essentially the junction of Kui In Fong and Po Hing Fong, two narrow lanes that climb the hillside behind Hollywood Road's antique shops.

The nearest MTR stop is Sheung Wan Station, on the Island Line; from Exit A2, it's a walk of roughly 10 to 15 minutes uphill through the Ladder Street area. Wear shoes you don't mind climbing stairs in, since much of Sheung Wan's street grid here is stepped rather than flat.

## Sitting where the neighbourhood sits

There's no admission and no staff gate to navigate; the park operates like any other public space in the city, open from 7:30am to 10pm every day. The unwritten rule is simple: don't take over the court space reserved for exercise groups or badminton games already in progress.

Locals treat the benches on the upper terrace as shared seating, so it's normal to sit near someone without conversation. If you're passing through with a coffee or a bakery bun, this is the kind of park where nobody minds, but there's no vendor inside selling either.

## What to pair it with

Blake Garden works best as a five-minute detour rather than a headline stop, folded into a longer walk through Sheung Wan's antique and dried-seafood streets. Man Mo Temple on Hollywood Road is a short walk downhill and east, one of the district's most-visited religious sites.

The PMQ creative complex and the Ladder Street steps themselves are also within easy reach on foot. Because the park is small, most visitors spend 15 to 30 minutes here at most, enough time to sit, take in the terraced layout, and move on.
