## A Oaxacan kitchen moves to the capital

Cobarde started in Oaxaca. That is worth knowing before you sit down, because Oaxaca is the Mexican state cooks travel to study. Its moles, its corn and its mezcal set the standard for the rest of the country.

The Michelin Guide gave Cobarde a Bib Gourmand. Now it has a second home in Mexico City, a city that already has more ambitious restaurants than any visitor can get through. A newly arrived kitchen with a Michelin mention earns its place on a short list here, and Cobarde is one of them.

## Fine dining with a pop streak

The idea is serious cooking that refuses to take itself too seriously. Chef Pako Cortés runs the menu. He builds from a Mexican base and pulls in flavours from Asia, the Middle East and Eastern Europe.

That spread is unusual even in a city this cosmopolitan. Most of the capital's tasting-menu places stay close to Mexican sources. Cobarde lets a dish go somewhere else entirely and then come back.

Practically, this means:

- **Read the menu slowly.** Expect combinations you won't have met on other Mexico City menus.
- **Ask your server what anchors each plate.** A dish that sounds Eastern European may still be built on Oaxacan ingredients.
- **Order to share if the format allows.** With this many directions on one menu, tasting widely makes more sense than one main each.

We are not listing specific dishes here. The menu at a new opening changes, and the version in front of you is the only one that counts.

## Mezcal, the Oaxacan way

The drinks side follows the restaurant's roots. Cobarde pours a curated mezcal selection. That fits, since Oaxaca produces most of Mexico's mezcal and treats it as a drink to taste properly.

Mezcal is made from agave hearts roasted in pits before they are fermented and distilled. Different agave species and different villages give very different spirits. A curated list means someone has picked a range to show those differences.

How to drink it well:

- **Sip, don't shoot.** Mexicans who know mezcal take it in small sips, often from a short glass or a shallow clay cup.
- **Ask for the agave.** Espadín is the most common. Wild agaves such as tobalá usually taste more distinct.
- **Let the staff steer you.** Tell them what you're eating and how smoky you like it.
- **Pace it.** Mezcal is often bottled stronger than standard tequila.

## What the Bib Gourmand tells you

A Bib Gourmand is not a star. The Michelin Guide gives it to restaurants where inspectors found very good cooking at moderate prices. It's the guide's way of flagging a place you can visit without planning around a special occasion.

The Michelin Guide only began covering Mexico in 2024. Cobarde's Bib Gourmand puts it among the first Mexican restaurants the guide singled out for good value. Treat it as a mid-range table with ambitious cooking, not a luxury blowout.

We haven't been given prices. The current menu and prices will be on Cobarde's official channels or at the restaurant itself.

## Booking a table at a brand-new opening

This is a new address, and the details are still settling. Before you go, confirm three things on Cobarde's official website or social accounts:

- **The exact address and neighbourhood.** Mexico City is huge, and traffic makes a wrong guess expensive in time.
- **Opening days and hours.** These often change in a new place's first months.
- **Reservations.** A Michelin-recognised arrival tends to fill its tables quickly, so book rather than walk in.

Getting around, most visitors use app-based ride services or registered taxis in the evening. The Metro is cheap and fast during the day but gets packed at rush hour. A dinner reservation is easier to keep if you leave extra time for traffic.

## Sitting down, ordering and paying the bill

Dinner in Mexico City runs late compared with northern Europe or the US. Locals often eat their main meal mid-afternoon and go out to dinner around 9pm. An early booking usually means a quieter room, if Cobarde's hours allow it.

A few habits that will smooth the evening:

- **Bring a little Spanish.** "La cuenta, por favor" gets you the bill. Staff at ambitious restaurants in the capital usually speak some English, but trying Spanish first is the polite opener.
- **The bill won't come until you ask.** Bringing it unprompted reads as rushing you.
- **Tip on top.** Service is not normally included in Mexico City. Leaving 10 to 15 percent is standard for good service.
- **Check how the card terminal works.** Staff usually bring a portable machine to the table and may ask whether to add the tip on the card.
- **Ask before you photograph staff or the kitchen.** It's courteous anywhere, and especially in a small, chef-led room.

## Is it worth the trip?

Go if you already know Mexican food and want to see where a young Oaxacan chef takes it. The mix of flavours and the mezcal list together make Cobarde different from both a classic Oaxacan restaurant and a standard tasting menu.

Plan a full evening rather than a quick bite. Leave room for a second mezcal, and book before you set your heart on a date.
