## Two nights under the Big Egg

Tokyo Dome is the stage here. Locals call it the "Big Egg" for the white air-supported roof that rises over Bunkyo ward. For concerts it holds tens of thousands, and the 'Blood Saga' stop fills it on December 1 and 2, 2026.

The Tokyo shows sit inside a Japan dome run that also visits Nagoya and Osaka. Set times and the show length are not listed in our facts. Confirm timing and tickets on the official site.

## Getting tickets and getting in

Japanese K-pop dome shows rarely work on open, first-come sales. Seats are typically handed out by lottery, with fan club members entered in the first rounds. Later rounds open to registered users of the official ticketing platforms.

Before you count on a seat, check these points on the official channels:

- Whether your ticket comes through a Japanese fan club or a global one
- Whether it is digital, which usually means a smartphone app that needs a Japanese phone number
- Whether the name on the ticket must match photo ID at the gate
- Resale rules, since resale outside official channels is generally banned in Japan

Bring your passport. Name and ID checks are common at Japanese K-pop shows, and the ID has to match the name on the ticket.

## Reaching Tokyo Dome

The Dome has four stations close by, which spreads out the arriving crowd:

- **Suidobashi Station**: JR Chuo-Sobu Line and Toei Mita Line, a short walk from the Dome
- **Korakuen Station**: Tokyo Metro Marunouchi and Namboku lines, next to Tokyo Dome City
- **Kasuga Station**: Toei Oedo and Mita lines, linked to the Korakuen side

After the encore, Suidobashi's JR platforms are the most crowded. Walking to Kasuga or Korakuen usually gets you onto a train sooner. Tokyo Dome City wraps around the stadium with its rollercoaster, shops and restaurants. Koishikawa Korakuen, an Edo-period garden, is just west of it if you have time before doors.

Early December evenings in Tokyo are cold, and the merch queue is outdoors. Dress for a long wait outside followed by a warm arena.

## Dome etiquette, Japanese style

Japanese concert crowds follow some rules that surprise visitors. Unless the official rules say otherwise, assume filming and photography during the show are banned. Most fans stay in their own seat space and keep penlights and fan banners below head height so the people behind can see.

Merch stands open well before doors, and the queues are strictly marshalled. Staff with signs mark the end of each line, so join where they point and don't save places. Coin lockers at the nearby stations fill fast on show days. Bring only what you are willing to carry all night.

The exit is staggered. Staff announce in Japanese which seating blocks may leave, one block at a time. Stay put until your block is called; leaving early just puts you in a jam at the gate. Most fans pay for train fares with a rechargeable IC card such as Suica or Pasmo, which saves time at ticket machines after the show.

For anything that varies between tours, including bag limits, penlight rules and gate allocations, the official tour notices are the authority.
