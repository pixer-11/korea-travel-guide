## Meadows Where Soldiers Once Drilled

Start at the southern tip and walk north. Within ten minutes the city noise is gone. You are on gravel paths running between wide, mown meadows, with stands of old beech and oak and small streams cut off from the Isar. On hot days the banks fill with people lying on towels.

The park opened in 1789. Benjamin Thompson, later Count Rumford, planned it for the Bavarian elector, and Friedrich Ludwig von Sckell shaped the landscape. Sckell copied the English landscape style, which gave the park its name. Here nature looks loose and unplanned, with no clipped hedges or formal parterres.

Today the park stretches far up the Isar's west bank, and it is commonly described as bigger than New York's Central Park. It has 78km of paths for cycling and jogging. A full north-to-south walk takes several hours, so choose one or two areas instead of trying to see it all.

## The Standing Wave at Prinzregentenstraße

Most visitors start at the Eisbach. Just beside the Haus der Kunst, where Prinzregentenstraße crosses the channel, the water rushes out from under the bridge. It forms a standing wave, and surfers in wetsuits ride it all year round. They wait their turn on both banks and drop in one at a time.

You watch from the bridge railing or the stone edge. You can see good surfing here at almost any hour of daylight.

Don't treat the Eisbach as a swimming spot. The current is strong, the channel runs into tunnels, and people have drowned in it. Signs warn about this along the banks.

## From the Monopteros Hill to the Chinese Tower

Walk north through the Schönfeldwiese and climb the small hill to the Monopteros. It's a round Greek-style temple with Ionic columns, built in the 1830s. From the steps you look south over the treetops to the towers of the Frauenkirche and the rest of the old town. Sunset brings the biggest crowd, and you'll often hear guitars.

The meadows here are also where Munich sunbathes, and naked sunbathing has long been allowed in parts of the park. Nobody stares, and you shouldn't either.

Keep going to the Chinesischer Turm, a five-storey wooden pagoda. It stands over one of Munich's largest beer gardens, thousands of seats under chestnut trees. A brass band often plays from the tower's balconies on busy days.

Nearby, off the southern stretch, is the Japanese Teahouse. Munich built it on a little island for the 1972 Olympics.

## Seehaus and the Lake

North of the Chinese Tower, the Kleinhesseloher See opens up. It has three small islands and rowing boats out on the water. This is the lakeside beer garden in the park's description: the Seehaus, with tables right down at the water's edge. It feels smaller and calmer than the Chinese Tower, and many people come here for its views of the lake.

North of the lake, the Isarring motorway cuts the park in two. Cross it and the land gets wilder, with fewer people and longer meadows, and sheep grazing at times. Cyclists ride on to the Hirschau and Aumeister beer gardens. This half of the park is where the 78km of trails pays off.

## The Seven O'Clock Weekend Window

The numbers tell you exactly when to go. **On weekends the park is quietest between 7am and 8am**, and busy from 11am to 9pm. That busy stretch covers the whole sunny afternoon and evening. If you arrive at noon on a summer Saturday, expect queues at the Eisbach railing, full benches at the Monopteros and long lines for beer.

In that early hour you mostly share the paths with joggers and dog walkers. You'll often have the Monopteros steps to yourself, and the Eisbach surfers are already out. Breakfast first and a beer garden later works well. If you can only come in the afternoon, head north of the lake, where the 11am–9pm crowds are thinner.

One summer morning breaks this rule. The **Kocherlball**, a traditional dance at the Chinese Tower, is held early on a Sunday in July. It revives the gatherings of Munich's 19th-century cooks and servants, who danced at dawn before work. Thousands come in dirndls and lederhosen, so check the date before planning a quiet early walk.

## Carrying Your Maß the Bavarian Way

The beer gardens inside the park have their own rules:

- **Self-service is normal.** Take a tray, collect food from the stalls, and get your beer at the beer counter. A Maß is a one-litre mug.
- **There's a deposit on the glass.** You get it back when you return the mug to the return counter, so don't just leave it on the table.
- **Bringing your own food is a tradition.** In a traditional Bavarian beer garden you can unpack your own picnic at the bare tables. Tables with cloths or table service are for buying from the house.
- **Share tables.** Long benches are communal, so ask "Ist hier noch frei?" (is this seat free?) and sit down.
- **Carry some cash.** Cards are more common than they used to be, but small kiosks and older counters still prefer cash.
- **Prices are posted at the counters.** Check the boards before you order.

On the paths, cyclists move fast and paths are shared. Walk to the side, and listen for bells. Locals barbecue only in marked zones, and they take their rubbish home.

## Getting In and Getting Around

You can enter from many points. These are the most useful:

- **Southern tip and the Eisbach wave:** walk 10–15 minutes from Odeonsplatz (U3/U4/U5/U6) through the Hofgarten. You can also take tram 18 or bus 100 to the Nationalmuseum/Haus der Kunst stop.
- **Middle of the park, Monopteros and Chinese Tower:** U3/U6 to Universität or Giselastraße, then walk east through Schwabing for about 10 minutes.
- **Kleinhesseloher See and Seehaus:** U3/U6 to Münchner Freiheit, then walk east.

A bike is the best way to see the whole park. Rental shops and bike-share docks are spread around Schwabing. For a first visit, allow half a day: walk from the Eisbach to the Monopteros, the Chinese Tower and the lake, with a stop at one beer garden.

The park has no gates and no admission charge, so you can come and go at any hour.
