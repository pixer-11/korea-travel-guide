## A shrine built for the sea breeze

Most people walk straight to El Castillo. The wind god's temple sits off to the north on its own limestone knuckle, just above the turquoise cove. It is a compact, single-room Postclassic Maya structure on a raised platform. From the bluff path you see it from above, framed by the Caribbean on one side and the sea grape and palms on the other.

The dedication matters. In this period the Maya linked a wind deity to Kukulkán, the feathered serpent. A shrine on an exposed point, open to the trade winds, fits that. Tulum was a walled port city, and its coastal buildings doubled as markers for canoe traders steering through the reef.

## Getting there and moving around

The ruins sit just north of Tulum town, off Highway 307, at the north end of the Zona Hotelera. Getting there:

- **Bike:** the flat cycle path from town is the usual local way to arrive.
- **Taxi or colectivo:** both drop you at the main parking area on the highway side.
- **On foot from the lot:** it is a walk of several minutes to the entrance, and a small tram runs the same stretch.

Inside, the loop path follows the old city wall to the cliff edge. The temple is the last stop before the path turns back from the cove. Count on 1.5–2 hours for the full site, with about 15 minutes at this viewpoint.

## Reading the ruins without a crowd

Compared with the main ruins, this listing gets few reviews, and the temple's viewpoint stays calmer than the El Castillo terrace. That calm depends on timing. Coaches from the Riviera Maya usually arrive mid-morning, so getting through the gate at opening gives you an empty bluff and soft, low light on the stone.

Shade is scarce once you are past the entrance. Bring:

- A hat and plenty of water. There is very little cover on the grassy plaza.
- Reef-safe sunscreen if you plan to swim.
- Swimwear under your clothes. A wooden staircase leads down to the cove below the temple when it is open.

Between roughly spring and late summer, sargassum seaweed can pile up on the cove. It is worth checking local seaweed reports if the swim is your reason for coming.

## The rules at Tulum's cliff edge

Tulum is run by INAH, Mexico's national heritage institute, and its rules are enforced:

- **No climbing.** Every structure, the wind god's temple included, is roped off. Guards will call you back.
- **No drones.** Flying is prohibited at INAH sites.
- **Tripods and pro rigs** may need a separate permit. Handheld phone photos are fine.
- **Iguanas** sun themselves on the walls. Don't feed or chase them.

Tickets are sold at booths by the entrance. The current price is posted there and on INAH's official site, and the national park access charge is listed separately. Hours shift during the year and on holidays, so confirm on the official site that week.

For the best version of the classic shot, stand on the bluff south of the cove and look north. The temple, the beach and the open sea all fit in one frame. Licensed guides wait near the gate if you want the city's history explained along the wall.
