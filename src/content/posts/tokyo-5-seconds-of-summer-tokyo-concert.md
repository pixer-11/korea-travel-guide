## Getting a ticket in Japan

Concert tickets in Japan usually work differently from what overseas fans expect. Most major shows sell through a **lottery** (抽選, *chūsen*). Fans apply during a window, and winners are drawn afterwards. Only some shows offer first-come sales on a fixed release date.

Things that tend to apply to international acts in Tokyo:

- **Big ticketing platforms**: sales often go through Japanese vendors such as Ticket Pia, e+ (eplus) or Lawson Ticket. Some of these offer English pages for overseas buyers.
- **Electronic tickets**: many shows issue tickets through a smartphone app. Seat numbers sometimes appear only on show day.
- **Name and ID checks**: tickets are often registered to the buyer. Staff may check photo ID at the door.
- **Resale rules**: unofficial resale is restricted in Japan. Official resale services are the sanctioned route, and street-side sellers are a risk.

These patterns are typical of international acts playing Tokyo halls.

## Show day in Ariake: merch, lockers, doors

At Japanese concerts the day starts well before doors open. Merchandise stalls (物販, *buppan*) often open hours ahead. On the last stop of a world tour, the queue tends to be long. Fans line up in single file and staff with signs mark where the line ends.

A few patterns are common:

- **Coin lockers** at nearby stations fill early on show days.
- **Bags** may be checked at entry. Large luggage is generally not allowed inside concert halls.
- **Layers** count. Queues stand outside in the bay wind before a warm, packed hall.
- **Food**: convenience stores and the restaurants in Ariake's malls and hotels are the main options before doors. Venue food is usually limited.

Door and start times are printed on the ticket. Japanese shows usually start very close to the listed time.

## Inside the hall: Tokyo concert manners

Japanese crowds follow some unwritten rules.

- **Stay in your space**: if seats are assigned, people stand at their own spot. Pushing forward is not normal here.
- **Filming**: rules vary by show. Signs and announcements before the set spell them out, and staff do enforce bans.
- **Signs and fan items**: large signs that block other people's view are generally frowned on. Penlights and small handheld items are common.
- **Talk between songs**: when the band speaks, the room goes quiet. Shouting over them stands out.

Entry can stall when an e-ticket app is not loaded or needs a signal to display.

## The regulated exit, and the bay after dark

Crowds do not leave as soon as the lights come up. Many Tokyo venues use a **regulated exit** (規制退場, *kisei taijō*), releasing the crowd one block or floor at a time. An announcement calls each section in turn, and each area waits until it is called.

When a hall empties, the whole audience heads for the Yurikamome and Rinkai platforms together. Staff often run queues at station entrances. Stations one stop further along are usually calmer. Ariake's hotel lobbies and cafés also offer a place to wait out the rush.

The waterfront itself is a good place to slow down. Odaiba is next door, with its seaside park, and the Rainbow Bridge is lit at night. A Yurikamome ride back to Shimbashi crosses it, with central Tokyo spread out across the water.
