Two nights on Tokyo Bay end a world tour. Australian pop-rock band 5 Seconds of Summer close their Everyone's A Star! tour at SGC Hall Ariake on November 21 and 22, 2026. Final nights of a tour tend to feel different. The crew is tired, the band knows the set by heart, and the crowd knows it won't happen again.

This guide covers what is known: the dates, the venue, how Japanese concert ticketing works and how to move through Ariake on show day. Times and prices are not covered here. Confirm timing and tickets on the official site.

## The band behind the encore

5 Seconds of Summer formed in Sydney in 2011. The lineup is Luke Hemmings, Michael Clifford, Calum Hood and Ashton Irwin. They started out posting covers online and went on to arena-sized pop-rock with "She Looks So Perfect" and "Youngblood".

For a Tokyo audience this matters in a practical way. Japanese crowds at Western rock shows sing English choruses word for word. They also go quiet between songs so they can hear the band talk. Expect a room that is loud during songs and very still in between.

## SGC Hall Ariake and the bayside district

The venue sits in Ariake, in Koto ward. This is reclaimed land on Tokyo Bay, built in wide blocks of convention halls, sports venues and hotels. Tokyo Big Sight, the city's largest exhibition centre, is in the same district. So is Ariake Arena, which hosted volleyball at the Tokyo 2020 Olympics.

The streets are broad and the pavements wide. Wind comes off the water, and in late November evenings it bites. That matters most when you are standing in a merch line or waiting to leave after the encore.

Two rail lines serve Ariake:

- **Yurikamome**, the driverless elevated line from Shimbashi, which crosses the Rainbow Bridge with views of the bay
- **Rinkai Line**, which connects at Osaki with JR lines, and runs through to Shinjuku and Shibuya on some trains

The venue's official access page names the nearest station and walking route. Check it before you set out, because several stations sit within walking distance of different parts of the district.

## Getting a ticket in Japan

Concert tickets in Japan usually work differently from what overseas fans expect. Most major shows sell through a **lottery** (抽選, *chūsen*). You apply during a window, and winners are drawn afterwards. Only some shows offer first-come sales on a fixed release date.

Things that tend to apply to international acts in Tokyo:

- **Big ticketing platforms**: sales often run through Japanese vendors such as Ticket Pia, e+ (eplus) or Lawson Ticket. Some of these offer English pages for overseas buyers.
- **Electronic tickets**: many shows issue tickets through a smartphone app. Seat numbers sometimes appear only on show day.
- **Name and ID checks**: tickets are often registered to the buyer. Staff may check photo ID at the door.
- **Resale rules**: unofficial resale is restricted in Japan. Use the official resale service if one is offered, and treat street-side sellers as a risk.

The band's official site and the listed vendor are the only sources to trust for sale methods and dates.

## Show day in Ariake: merch, lockers, doors

The day starts well before doors open. Merchandise stalls (物販, *buppan*) at Japanese concerts often open hours ahead. On the last stop of a world tour, the queue can be long. Fans line up in single file and staff with signs mark where the line ends.

Plan around these points:

- **Coin lockers** at nearby stations fill early. Bring as little as you can.
- **Bags** may be checked at entry. Large luggage is generally not allowed inside concert halls.
- **Layers** matter. You will queue outside in the bay wind and then stand in a warm, packed hall.
- **Food**: convenience stores and the restaurants in Ariake's malls and hotels are your best option before doors. Venue food is usually limited.

Door and start times are printed on your ticket or posted on the official channels. Japanese shows usually start very close to the listed time.

## Inside the hall: Tokyo concert manners

Japanese crowds follow some unwritten rules, and a little preparation goes a long way.

- **Stay in your space**: if seats are assigned, people stand at their own spot. Pushing forward is not normal here.
- **Filming**: rules vary by show. Watch for signs and listen to announcements before the set. Staff do enforce bans.
- **Signs and fan items**: large signs that block other people's view are generally frowned on. Penlights and small handheld items are common.
- **Talk between songs**: when the band speaks, the room goes quiet. Shouting over them stands out.

Bring your ticket app fully loaded and your phone charged. Entry can stall if the e-ticket needs a signal to display.

## The regulated exit, and the bay after dark

You will not leave as soon as the lights come up. Many Tokyo venues use a **regulated exit** (規制退場, *kisei taijō*), releasing the crowd one block or floor at a time. An announcement calls each section in turn. Stay in place until your area is called.

Once outside, two whole audiences head for the Yurikamome and Rinkai platforms together. Staff often run queues at station entrances. If you are in no hurry, walk to a station one stop further along. Or you can wait out the rush in one of Ariake's hotel lobbies or cafés.

The waterfront itself is a good place to slow down. Odaiba is next door, with its seaside park, and the Rainbow Bridge is lit at night. A Yurikamome ride back to Shimbashi crosses it, with central Tokyo spread out across the water.

## Planning around November 21-22

The two dates fall on a Saturday and Sunday. That makes them good dates for fans coming from other parts of Japan or from overseas. It also means hotels near the bay, and around Shimbashi and Toyosu, are in demand for both nights.

Late November is autumn foliage season across Tokyo. If you have a free morning, the ginkgo trees in the city's parks are worth seeing. The days are mild and the evenings are cold, so pack for both.
