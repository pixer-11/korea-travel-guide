## Under the Iron and Glass

Look up before you look at the stalls. The Cardiff Market opened in 1891, and its roof is a long Victorian span of iron and glass. Daylight falls through it onto the aisles of the ground floor.

A gallery runs around the upper level, so there are two floors of trading. From the balcony rail you can look down over the whole hall, which is the easiest way to work out where everything is before you go back down into it.

This is no quiet backwater. It is one of the most-reviewed places in the city centre, consistently well rated, and on a Saturday it is shoulder to shoulder. Treat it as a working market that happens to be a landmark, not a museum.

## What Fills the Aisles

The mix is the point. Food sits next to hardware, and both share a floor with stalls selling secondhand goods. The editorial description is accurate: an array of merchants and food stalls spread over two levels.

What you can expect to find on a typical visit:

- **Welsh cakes**, the flat, currant-studded griddle cakes, often cooked on a hot plate where you can watch
- **Fresh fish and seafood**, including the cockles and laverbread that South Wales has long been known for
- **Butchers and greengrocers** serving people who live in the city and do their weekly shopping here
- **Hot food counters** for a sit-down or standing lunch
- **Non-food stalls** on both floors: records, books, clothes, odds and ends

Stalls change over the years, so walk the full loop before you commit to anything.

## Getting In from St Mary Street or Trinity Street

There are entrances at both ends. The St Mary Street side faces the city's main thoroughfare. The Trinity Street side sits opposite the medieval tower of St John the Baptist Church, which makes it easy to spot.

Cardiff Central station is roughly a ten-minute walk south, and Queen Street station is a similar distance to the east. The whole area is largely pedestrianised, so walk in. Don't try to drive to the door.

## Shopping the Stalls

Each stall is its own small business. That shapes how you should behave inside.

- **Payment varies.** Plenty of traders take cards and contactless, but not every one does. A little cash saves an awkward moment at a smaller stall.
- **Prices are set by each trader.** Check the board or label at the stall, and ask if nothing is posted.
- **Ask before photographing a stall up close.** Traders are working, and a lens in their face while they serve a queue is not welcome.
- **Queues form at the counter, not in a line down the aisle.** Stand where you can see the trader, and let regulars who know exactly what they want go first if you're still deciding.
- **Buy hot Welsh cakes to eat now and a bag to take away.** Fresh from the griddle is a different thing from a day-old packet.

There is no booking, ticket or entry charge to walk through.

## Timing Your Visit

At the moment it opens 8am to 5pm, Monday to Saturday, and it is closed on Sundays. Hours can shift around public holidays, so check the official listing if you're visiting over a bank holiday.

Weekday mornings, soon after opening, usually suit visitors best. Stalls are freshly stocked, and the city-centre lunch rush has not yet arrived. Saturday is the liveliest day, with weekend shoppers on top of the regulars.

Don't leave it until late afternoon. Some stalls start packing up before the official close, and the food counters can sell out of popular items.

## Around the Corner

The market sits within a few minutes' walk of Cardiff's main sights, so it slots neatly into a day on foot.

- **Cardiff Castle**, at the top of St Mary Street, roughly five minutes north
- **The Victorian and Edwardian arcades**, including the Castle, Morgan and Royal Arcades, threaded between the main shopping streets
- **St John the Baptist Church**, right outside the Trinity Street entrance
- **Principality Stadium**, a short walk west along Westgate Street

A good loop goes like this: start at the castle, cut through the arcades, eat lunch in the market and leave by Trinity Street.
