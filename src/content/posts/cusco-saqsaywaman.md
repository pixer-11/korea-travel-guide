## Walls that swallow a person

Stand at the base of the zigzag terraces and the top of the stones is above your head. Three tiers of limestone run in sawtooth lines along the northern edge of the site. Each block is cut to lock into its neighbours without mortar, and the joints are too tight to slide a card into.

The largest block is estimated at well over 100 tonnes. Across the flat grass esplanade sits the Rodadero, a hump of natural rock polished into grooves that people still slide down. Above the walls are the circular foundations of Muyuq Marka, one of the towers that once stood on the summit.

## A fortress, a battle, a quarry

Saqsaywaman was built under the Inca ruler Pachacuti and his successors in the 15th century. In 1536 it was the stronghold for Manco Inca's siege of Spanish-held Cusco. Juan Pizarro died in the fighting to retake it.

What you see today is only a fraction of the original complex. After the conquest, the Spanish used its upper walls as a quarry, and cut stone from here went into the colonial churches and mansions below. The huge lower blocks survived because they were too heavy to move.

On 24 June each year the esplanade fills for Inti Raymi, the restaged Inca festival of the sun. It is the busiest day of the year on the hill.

## Getting up the hill

From the Plaza de Armas, walk up Calle Suecia or climb through San Blas toward the Church of San Cristóbal. Then follow the path uphill. Expect 20–30 minutes on foot, steep and stepped.

The site sits at roughly 3,700 metres, higher than the city centre. If you have just flown in, a taxi to the upper entrance saves your lungs. Give the site itself 1.5–2 hours.

Pair it with nearby sites on the road north:

- **Cristo Blanco**: the white statue on the next hill, with a view straight down over Cusco
- **Q'enqo**: a carved limestone shrine, a short walk further on
- **Puka Pukara and Tambomachay**: easiest reached by taxi, and you can walk back downhill from there

## Beating the coach groups up the ramp

Saqsaywaman is one of the most visited sites around Cusco, and city tours arrive in waves. Currently the gates open at 7am and close at 5:30pm. Opening hours can change, so check the official Ministry of Culture site before you go.

Arrive between 7am and 9am, any day of the week, and you'll have the walls largely to yourself. On weekends, avoid 10am–1pm and 2pm–4pm. Those are the peak windows, when groups pile up at the big stones for photos.

How entry works:

- Admission is on the **Boleto Turístico del Cusco**, sold at the official COSITUC office in town and at the site's entrance. Current prices and versions are posted there.
- The partial ticket for this circuit also covers Q'enqo, Puka Pukara and Tambomachay, so plan them for the same day.
- Keep your ticket handy, because it gets stamped at each site.

Locals in traditional dress, often with a llama or alpaca, pose near the entrance. They expect a tip for each photo, so ask first. Stay on the marked paths and off the walls themselves. Guards enforce this. A brimmed hat and water matter here: the esplanade has almost no shade, and the sun at this altitude burns quickly.
