## Shophouses from the tin boom

Thalang Road is the spine. Two-storey shophouses line both sides, painted in pastels, with shuttered upper windows and plaster detailing over the arches. At street level a covered arcade, the "five-foot way", runs in front of the shopfronts. It gives you shade from one doorway to the next.

This district was built on tin. Hokkien Chinese merchants grew rich from mining in the 19th and early 20th centuries and built in a style that blended Chinese and European forms. Their Peranakan, or Baba, culture still shapes the food, the shrines and the old family homes here.

## Streets worth walking

The core is compact, and two hours on foot covers it. Stick to this grid:

- **Thalang Road**: the densest run of restored shophouses, lined with cafés, textile shops and old-style traders.
- **Soi Rommanee**: a short, narrow lane off Thalang, known for the most-photographed pastel frontages. It was once the red-light street.
- **Dibuk and Krabi Roads**: bigger mansions, including the Thai Hua Museum, a former Chinese school on Krabi Road.
- **Shrine of the Serene Light**: a small Chinese shrine tucked down an alley off Phang Nga Road, easy to walk past.

The Jui Tui shrine sits at the southwest edge of town. It is the centre of the Phuket Vegetarian Festival, held in the ninth lunar month, usually late September or October. For that week, processions and firecrackers take over the old streets.

## Getting in and eating

Phuket Town sits roughly 30 km south of the airport and is a short drive inland from Patong over the hill. Taxis and ride-hailing apps are the simplest way in. Local blue songthaews run from the town's market area to several beaches during the day.

Come hungry. The town is known for Hokkien-rooted dishes:

- **Mee Hokkien**: thick yellow noodles, stir-fried or in broth
- **O-tao**: a small oyster and taro omelette
- **Moo hong**: pork belly braised dark with black pepper
- **Dim sum**: a breakfast habit in the town's older teahouses

## Timing the old town right

The streets never close. The businesses along them keep their own hours, and many shut early in the evening, so check any specific shop or museum before you go. The walking itself is best early, before the midday heat bounces off the pavement, or after about 4pm, when the shopfronts light up.

Sunday evening changes Thalang Road. The Lard Yai walking street closes it to traffic, and stalls selling snacks and crafts fill the road. Expect elbow-to-elbow crowds. Arrive near the start if you want room to see the buildings, not just the backs of heads.

This is a well-known stop, not a secret, and tour groups pass through at midday. Behave as you would in a neighbourhood people actually live in. Many shophouses are still homes, so don't step into doorways for photos. Remove your shoes at shrine thresholds where you see others doing it. Small street vendors take cash, and QR payment is common. Tipping at noodle stalls isn't expected.
