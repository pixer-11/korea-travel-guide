## A month of light on Bangkok's river

Bangkok's widest street is water. From November 13 to December 13, 2026, VIJIT CHAO PHRAYA uses the Chao Phraya River as the stage for a light and sound festival. Temple walls, bridges and riverside buildings carry illumination displays after dark.

The Thai name, วิจิตรเจ้าพระยา, roughly means "the splendour of the Chao Phraya." Earlier editions were run by the Tourism Authority of Thailand with city and private partners. They concentrated on the central stretch of river, from Rama VIII Bridge in the north down past Sathorn. The organiser publishes the 2026 map of lit sites on its official channels.

## Where the light lands

There is no single venue. The festival is the river itself, so your view depends on which bank you stand on. Sites that carried displays in past editions include:

- **Wat Arun**, the Temple of Dawn, whose porcelain-studded prang rises from the Thonburi bank
- **Rama VIII Bridge**, the single-pylon cable-stayed bridge north of Banglamphu
- **Memorial Bridge** (Saphan Phut), the older steel crossing near Pak Khlong Talat flower market
- **Wat Kalayanamit**, with its tall white-and-red viharn facing the river
- Riverside complexes such as **ICONSIAM** and **Asiatique The Riverfront**

The line-up of illuminated buildings changes from one edition to the next. A building lit in an earlier year is not a guarantee for 2026.

## Reaching the water

Rail gets you closest to the piers. The river has no traffic lights, so the water is often quicker than any road.

- **BTS Silom Line to Saphan Taksin**: exit 2 leads straight down to Sathorn Pier, the main hub for river boats.
- **MRT Blue Line to Sanam Chai**: a short walk past Wat Pho takes you to Tha Tien Pier, directly across from Wat Arun.
- **Gold Line to Charoen Nakhon**: this drops you at ICONSIAM on the Thonburi side.
- **Cross-river ferries**: short, flat-bottomed shuttles link Tha Tien to Wat Arun and run in a few minutes.

Chao Phraya Express Boats run mostly on commuter hours, and evening service is thinner than in the daytime. After a late show, many visitors head back by Grab or metered taxi from the nearest main road.

## On the boats

Seeing the displays from mid-river gives a different angle. You see both banks at once, and the bridges pass overhead. Dinner cruises from piers near ICONSIAM, Asiatique and the Sathorn hotels have long worked this stretch. During the festival they route past the lit landmarks.

The Chao Phraya Tourist Boat, marked with a blue flag, is the simpler hop-on option. Cruise operators and the ticketing partners named by the organiser list their festival sailings and prices themselves. No figure appears here because none was supplied.

## Watching from the bank like a Bangkokian

Most people watching from land never pay for a seat. In earlier editions, the public riverbanks were open to anyone, and families spread out along the promenades. Here is how the evening usually works:

- **Santichaiprakan Park**, beside the white Phra Sumen Fort in Banglamphu, faces Rama VIII Bridge head-on. Locals sit on the lawn and on the river wall.
- **Tha Tien's riverside bars and rooftops** look straight across at Wat Arun. They fill fast on festival nights, and regulars book tables days in advance.
- **Lhong 1919** and the Khlong San waterfront give a Thonburi-side view back towards the old city.

On the express boats, a conductor walks the deck rattling a metal tube of coins and tickets. Passengers pay in cash, ideally with small notes. The seats at the back are set aside for monks, and Thai passengers leave them empty.

If you step into temple grounds on the way, keep your shoulders and knees covered. Riverside parks near the water are mosquito territory after dark, so regulars carry repellent.

## Timing an evening

The festival falls in Bangkok's cool, dry season. Evenings are the most comfortable of the year for standing outdoors, and rain is uncommon. This is also the start of high tourist season. Hotels along Charoen Krung and the riverside fill up, and visitors who want a river view usually reserve rooms well in advance.

Reasoning from the calendar, weekend nights draw more local families than weeknights. One lit area plus a boat ride or riverside dinner takes most people two to three hours. Loy Krathong falls on the full moon of the twelfth Thai lunar month, usually in November. In years when it overlaps the festival, the banks are crowded with people floating krathongs as well as light-watchers.
