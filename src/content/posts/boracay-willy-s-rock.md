## Why go

Willy's Rock is the single image most people already have of Boracay before they've ever booked a flight: a squat, black volcanic outcrop rising out of turquoise shallows, with a small statue of the Virgin Mary tucked into a shrine niche on its seaward side. It's not a grand monument — it's barely taller than a person standing on the sand beside it — but it anchors Station 1's stretch of White Beach the way a lighthouse anchors a harbor.

Every long-time Boracay visitor has a photo here, and the rock has become shorthand for "you were really on White Beach."

## What you'll actually see

At low tide you can walk straight out to the rock across a strip of packed, rippled sand, feel the water go from ankle- to knee-deep, and touch the same porous black stone that's been battered smooth by the surf. Someone has usually left fresh or plastic flowers at the little shrine ledge, and it's common to see people pause, cross themselves, or just rest a hand on the rock before backing off to frame their shot.

At high tide, the rock seems to float — the sand disappears and the base is submerged, which is when it photographs best from the boardwalk rather than up close. Behind you, the beachfront restaurants, dive shops, and sunset bars of Station 1 stack up under coconut palms, and the boardwalk itself gets a steady flow of pedicabs, vendors selling grilled corn and fruit shakes, and the horse-and-carriage rides that clip along the sand nearby.

## Getting there

Willy's Rock sits directly on White Beach at Station 1, on the northern end of the beach in Malay, Aklan. If you're coming from Cagban Jetty Port (where the pump boats from Caticlan arrive), a tricycle or e-trike to Station 1 takes roughly 15–20 minutes depending on traffic and where along the island your accommodation sits. Once you're on the beachfront path — locally just called "the boardwalk" — you simply walk north; the rock is impossible to miss, usually with a small knot of people around it.

There's no gate, no ticket booth, and no official opening hours, since it's a natural feature sitting in open shoreline.

## When to go

Weather matters more than season here: Boracay's dry season (roughly November through April) gives you calmer water and better odds of a clean sunset shot, while the wetter months (June–October) bring squalls that can flatten the whole scene for an afternoon. Within any single day, tide matters more than time — check a tide chart for White Beach and aim for low tide if you want to walk right up to the rock rather than just view it from the sand.

Sunset (roughly 5:30–6:30pm depending on the month) is when the sky turns the water behind the rock orange and pink and is genuinely the best light, but it's also when the boardwalk fills with everyone else who had the same idea.

## How to visit like a local

Come early — before 8am — or accept the sunset crowd and work around it. The rock itself rated 4.6 from over 1,300 reviews precisely because so many people make the pilgrimage, so treating it as a five-minute photo stop rather than a destination in itself is the realistic approach. There's no fee and no vendor stationed at the rock demanding payment for photos, though nearby beach photographers may offer to shoot your picture for a tip — agree on the amount before they start, or just use your own phone.

Dress is casual beachwear; nobody expects modest dress for a quick wade-out, though a few visitors do pause quietly at the shrine ledge out of respect rather than climbing on it for a pose, which locals appreciate. The mistake most first-timers make is showing up at 1 or 2pm in flat high tide, midday sun, and peak boardwalk foot traffic, then wondering why their photo looks washed out and crowded — shift your visit by a few hours and the same spot feels completely different.

Pair it with a slow walk along the rest of Station 1's boardwalk, where the sunset bars start filling chairs on the sand as the light goes gold.
