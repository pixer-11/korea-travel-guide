## Across the road from the Temple of Heaven

Most visitors come for the temple and never look back across the road. Hongqiao Market sits on Tiantan Donglu in Dongcheng, facing the east gate of the Temple of Heaven. It's a plain, boxy building, and its stalls are packed floor after floor.

The Chinese name is 红桥市场 (Hóngqiáo Shìchǎng), "Red Bridge Market." It draws around a million visitors a year, and its reputation rests on one thing: pearls. Almost everything else here is a warm-up for the upper floors, where strands hang in rows behind glass counters.

Don't confuse it with Shanghai's Hongqiao airport or rail hub. Taxi apps and drivers who hear only "Hongqiao" may assume you mean Shanghai. Showing the characters 红桥市场 clears that up at once.

## Reading the building floor by floor

Each floor sells its own kind of goods. The layout is simple once you know it, and knowing it saves you from spending an hour at the bottom and running out of patience before the good part.

- **Lower floors:** bags, shoes, clothing, luggage, small electronics and phone accessories. Much of it is unbranded or branded in ways you should take with a pinch of salt.
- **Middle floors:** more clothing, watches, silk items and souvenirs such as chopstick sets and cloisonné trinkets.
- **Upper floors:** pearls and jewellery. Freshwater strands, loose pearls, settings, jade and coral share the space.

The aisles are narrow and each stall is a small glass-fronted booth. Vendors call out to anyone who slows down. If you want to browse in peace, keep walking at an even pace until something catches your eye.

## The pearl floor, and how to judge a strand

This is the reason to come. Stall after stall lays out freshwater pearls sorted by colour: white, cream, peach, lavender, dyed black. Many vendors will knot a necklace or set earrings while you wait, so you can pick loose pearls and a clasp and leave with a finished piece.

Quality varies enormously, often within a single counter. A few checks help you tell a good strand from a filler one:

- **Roundness:** roll the strand slowly across the glass. Near-round pearls roll smoothly, while baroque or off-round ones wobble.
- **Lustre:** good pearls throw back a sharp reflection of the overhead lights. Dull, chalky surfaces mean lower grade.
- **Surface:** look for pits, rings and blemishes. A few on a budget strand are normal. Many means it's cheap stock.
- **The tooth test:** real pearls feel slightly gritty when rubbed gently against your front teeth. Glass or plastic feels smooth.
- **Matching:** hold the strand up and check that size and colour stay consistent from clasp to clasp.

Vendors will often perform their own tests to prove authenticity. Watch with interest, but run your own checks too. Ask for the strand to be restrung on silk with knots between the pearls if it isn't already, as knots stop the whole thing scattering if it breaks.

## Haggling at Hongqiao

Bargaining here isn't optional. The first figure you hear is an opening move, and vendors expect a counteroffer well below it. Many type numbers on a calculator and hand it to you, so you can negotiate without a shared language.

A few habits make the process smoother:

- **Decide your ceiling first.** Know what a piece is worth to you before you ask the price, and stick to it.
- **Stay friendly.** A smile and a firm number work better than theatrics. Vendors respond to buyers who clearly know what they want.
- **Walking away is a real tactic.** Identical goods sit a few stalls along, and a vendor will often call you back with a better number.
- **Don't counter unless you mean it.** Once a vendor accepts your offer, backing out is considered bad form.
- **Bundle purchases.** Buying a necklace and earrings together gives you room to push the total down.

Mobile payment dominates in Beijing, and Alipay and WeChat Pay are the default at most stalls. Both apps now accept many foreign bank cards, so set one up before your trip. Carry some cash as a backup, since not every booth takes foreign cards directly.

On the lower floors, treat any famous brand logo with scepticism. Counterfeit goods can also cause trouble at customs in some home countries, so check your own country's rules before loading up.

## Getting to Tiantan Donglu

The subway is the simplest route. Take **Line 5 to Tiantandongmen Station** (天坛东门, "Temple of Heaven East Gate"). The market is a short walk away on the same road, opposite the temple's east entrance.

From central Beijing, Line 5 connects with Line 1 at Dongdan and with Line 2 at Chongwenmen. Both are quick transfers from the Wangfujing area. If you take a taxi or a DiDi, enter the address in Chinese characters to avoid any Shanghai mix-up.

No opening hours are listed for the market here, so check current times before heading over. Like many Beijing malls, it runs on daytime retail hours rather than as a night market.

## Pairing it with the Temple of Heaven

Hongqiao works best as half of a day. The Temple of Heaven's east gate is right across the road, so you can walk from the Hall of Prayer for Good Harvests to the pearl counters without getting back on the subway.

The logical order is temple first, market second. Temple parks are at their best early, when residents gather for tai chi, dancing and card games. Then cross over and shop without worrying about carrying bags round a UNESCO site all afternoon.

Allow about 1.5 to 2 hours for the market if pearls are the goal. Serious buyers comparing several stalls can easily spend longer. After that, Qianmen Street and the hutong lanes of Dashilar are a short ride west, handy for dinner.

## Before you go

The market is well visited but rarely talked up the way the Silk Street market is, so it can feel less theatrical. That doesn't make it a bargain by default. The work of comparing stalls and negotiating is still yours.

Bring a small pouch or case for any jewellery you buy, plus a photo of a piece you like if you're trying to match it. Keep receipts and business cards from vendors. Many stalls will repair a strand if you come back to the same counter later in your trip.
