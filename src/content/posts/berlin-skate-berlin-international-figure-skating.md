## Five days of ice in a February city

Skate Berlin runs from February 9 to 13, 2027. It is an international figure skating competition on the 2026–27 season calendar, which puts it in the stretch of winter when skaters are finishing their programmes ahead of the championship months.

That timing shapes the event. By February, skaters have been performing these programmes since autumn. The choreography has been reworked, the jump layouts adjusted, and the costumes are often second versions. What you see in Berlin is close to each skater's finished season.

## What a competition day looks like

Figure skating competitions follow a fixed structure, and knowing it makes the day easier to read. Singles skaters perform a short programme first and a longer free skate later. Pairs follow the same pattern. Ice dancers perform a rhythm dance and then a free dance.

Between groups, a resurfacing machine circles the rink and leaves a fresh sheet of ice. Each group gets a short warm-up together before skating one at a time. When a programme ends, the skater sits in the "kiss and cry" area beside the boards and waits there for the score.

Scores appear on the arena display. They are split into a technical score for jumps, spins and step sequences, and a component score for skating quality and presentation. Watching the technical panel's calls come up is half the fun once you know what to look for.

## Getting across Berlin in winter

Berlin's transport is run mainly by BVG (U-Bahn, trams and buses) alongside the S-Bahn. One ticket system covers all of them. The city splits into fare zones A, B and C, and most venues inside the city sit in zones A and B.

A few practical points:

- **Paper tickets need validating** in the small machines on platforms or inside trams. An unvalidated ticket counts as no ticket during an inspection.
- **The BVG app** sells tickets that need no validation.
- **Trams dominate the eastern districts**, where U-Bahn lines are sparse.
- **Night services run**, so a late evening session does not strand you.

## Teddy bears, flowers and the cold seats

Skating has a ritual you will not see at most sports. After a programme ends, fans throw soft toys and wrapped flowers onto the ice. Young skaters, often called "flower kids", skate out and collect them before the next competitor starts.

The unwritten rules matter because loose items can wreck a blade. Flowers are wrapped in plastic, toys have no loose parts, and nothing goes on the ice in the middle of a programme. Fans of a particular skater often bring a banner or a national flag as well.

Then there is the temperature. An ice rink is kept cold on purpose. In February, Berlin's outdoor highs often sit only just above freezing. People who sit through a whole session usually wear a warm layer under their coat and keep it on in the stands.

## Watching from the stands

Skating crowds keep a specific kind of quiet. You stay seated while someone is skating, and you move to and from your seat during the resurfacing breaks, between skaters, or in the warm-ups. Ushers often hold people at the entrance until a programme finishes.

Applause has its own rhythm too:

- **Clap for a landed jump** or a strong spin as it happens. That is normal and welcome.
- **Go quiet as a skater takes their opening pose** and the music is about to begin.
- **Cheer every skater**, including those who fall. Supporting someone through a tough skate is standard rink manners.
- **Phones on silent**, and no flash photography during a programme.

Arena kiosks in Germany often add a *Pfand*, a deposit, to drinks served in bottles or reusable cups. You get it back when you return the container, so most spectators carry their empties back to the counter. Cards are now widely accepted, but small kiosks sometimes still prefer cash.

## Making a trip of it

Five days of competition leave room for the city. Most international spectators choose a few sessions rather than all of them, often the free skates and free dances, where the final placings are decided.

February in Berlin is good indoor weather. Museum Island in Mitte, with the Pergamon and Neues Museum among its institutions, takes a full day. The Reichstag dome and the East Side Gallery, the longest surviving section of the Berlin Wall, fit into a short gap between sessions.

In the evenings, the city's districts each eat differently. Kreuzberg and Neukölln are known for Turkish and Middle Eastern food. Prenzlauer Berg has cafés and Mitte has the late bars. A warm döner between sessions is a very Berlin way to recover from a cold rink.
