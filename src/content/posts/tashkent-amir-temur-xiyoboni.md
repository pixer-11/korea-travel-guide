Most visitors walk past this square on the way to somewhere else, which is exactly why it's worth a deliberate stop. Amir Temur Xiyoboni is a compact circular park, ringed by mature plane trees, with the bronze statue of Amir Timur on horseback as its only real landmark. There's no ticket booth, no queue, no gate. You just walk in.

The statue itself replaced a Karl Marx monument in 1994, and before that a statue of Stalin stood on the same spot, then briefly a globe. Layered history on one plinth. Timur sits high on his horse, sword sheathed rather than drawn, a deliberate choice meant to project statesmanship over conquest.

## Getting your bearings on the square

The park is small enough to see everything in one loop. Paved paths radiate out from the statue in a wheel pattern, lined with benches and clipped hedges.

Around the perimeter you'll spot some of Tashkent's more recognisable buildings:

- **Hotel Uzbekistan**, the slab-like Soviet-era tower on the east side
- **The State Museum of Timurids**, a domed building a short walk northeast
- **Amir Temur Xiyoboni metro station**, one of the closest stops, on the Chilonzor line

None of this requires a map app once you're standing at the statue; the square is the hub and everything else radiates from it.

## Why it's worth the twenty minutes

This isn't a headline attraction and it doesn't try to be. At a rated 4.6 with under 200 reviews, it draws far fewer visitors than Chorsu Bazaar or Independence Square nearby, which is part of the appeal.

You get shade, benches, and a genuine slice of local life rather than a monument overrun with tour groups. Office workers cut through on lunch breaks. Elderly couples sit on the benches near the fountains. Nobody is selling you anything.

The square works best as a pause, not a destination. Combine it with a walk down Sailgoh Street or a coffee at one of the cafés facing Hotel Uzbekistan, and you've turned a five-minute photo stop into a proper half hour.

## Reading the statue and its plaques

Take the time to walk the full circle around the statue base. Uzbek-language plaques describe Timur's role as founder of the Timurid Empire and patron of Samarkand's architecture, and the inscriptions carry a phrase often translated as "Strength is in justice," which has become something of a national motto since independence.

The horse and rider are cast in dark bronze, elevated on a stone plinth high enough that photos usually need some distance to fit the whole thing in frame. Morning light from the east works well if you're shooting the statue's east-facing side; by late afternoon the sun swings around and puts the west face into better light instead.

## Visiting without disturbing the calm

Uzbeks generally treat the square as a public park rather than a shrine, so there's no dress code or ticket to manage. Still, a few habits will make you blend in rather than stand out.

- Keep to the paved paths; the lawns are decorative, not for sitting on
- If locals are seated on a particular bench in the shade, especially older residents, look for another rather than crowding in
- Photography is unrestricted and free; nobody will ask you to pay for a photo permit here, unlike at some Tashkent museums

Early morning, before the day heats up, is when the square feels most like a neighbourhood park rather than a tourist stop. Fountains typically run during the day and are switched off overnight, so an evening visit after dark will find them quiet.

## Making it part of a longer walk

Amir Temur Xiyoboni isn't going to fill an afternoon on its own, and it shouldn't have to. Treat it as a waypoint between Independence Square, a fifteen-minute walk west, and the Museum of Applied Arts further east.

Tashkent's central district rewards this kind of loose, on-foot wandering more than a tightly scheduled itinerary. The square gives you a shaded bench to regroup, check a map, and decide where to go next.
