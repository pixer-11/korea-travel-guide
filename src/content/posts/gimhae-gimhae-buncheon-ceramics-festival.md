Picture a tea bowl the colour of wet slate, brushed over with white slip so fast that you can still count the bristle marks. That bowl is buncheong. Gimhae has made this kind of pottery for centuries, and its November festival puts the city's working potters and their tables out in the open.

The Korean name is 김해분청도자기축제 (Gimhae Buncheong Dojagi Chukje). The title says "Buncheon" and Korean signage says "Buncheong", but they mean the same festival.

## Grey clay, white slip: what buncheong means

Buncheong is a stoneware made from grey or iron-rich clay. Potters coat it in white slip, then decorate it before the glaze goes on. It flourished in the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries during the early Joseon dynasty, before white porcelain took over at court.

The term itself is modern. The art historian Go Yu-seop coined it in the 1930s as a short form of *bunjang hoecheong sagi*, meaning grey-green ware dressed in powder.

The same decorating techniques appear again and again on buncheong tables. Knowing their names makes it much easier to talk with the potters:

- **Sanggam**: inlay, where lines are carved into the clay and filled with white slip
- **Inhwa**: stamping, with small repeated flower or dot motifs pressed into the surface
- **Bakji**: sgraffito, where slip is scraped away to leave the design in raised white
- **Johwa**: incising, with fine lines drawn through the slip to the grey clay below
- **Cheolhwa**: iron-brown painting over the white ground
- **Gwiyal**: the loose brushed-slip finish
- **Deombeong**: dipping, which gives a whole pot a soft white coat

Some surviving early pieces were inscribed with the names of the government offices that ordered them, and Gimhae is among those names. That is why the festival here feels like a continuation of local work rather than a revival.

## Five days in early November

The 2026 edition was set for Friday, November 6 to Tuesday, November 10. That put both a full weekend and two weekdays inside the window.

Ceramics festivals in Korea tend to be busiest on the Saturday and Sunday, when families come out from Busan and Changwon. Weekdays usually move at a slower pace, with more time at each potter's table.

Early November in South Gyeongsang is autumn proper. Mornings and evenings are cool, and most people wear a layer they can take off at midday. The festival is traditionally an outdoor event of stalls and demonstrations, and a light jacket makes a long afternoon much easier.

## Jillye, the potters' end of town

Gimhae's ceramics are concentrated in Jillye-myeon, a semi-rural district on the western edge of the city. Earlier editions of the festival were held around the Gimhae Buncheong Pottery Museum (김해분청도자관) in Jillye.

The Clayarch Gimhae Museum of Art (클레이아크김해미술관) is also in Jillye. It is a museum dedicated to architectural ceramics. Its main building is clad in coloured ceramic tiles, so it looks like a giant glazed vessel sitting in the hills.

Together the two museums put the history of the craft and its contemporary, building-scale uses within the same district. Festival visitors often combine the pottery stalls with a Clayarch visit on the same day.

## At the potters' tables

Buying from a maker works a little differently from buying in a shop. These habits help:

- **Ask before you lift.** A quick gesture toward the piece and "만져 봐도 돼요?" (*manjyeo bwado dwaeyo?*, "may I touch it?") is the polite way. Use both hands, one under the base.
- **Turn it over.** Most potters stamp or sign the foot, and Korean buyers look there first. The foot ring also shows how the piece was trimmed.
- **Talk technique.** Naming the decoration ("inhwa?") usually opens a real conversation, even with very little shared language.
- **Pay simply.** Card payment is widespread in South Korea, and mobile payment is common too. A small amount of cash still helps at a table run by a single maker.
- **No tipping.** Tips are not part of Korean retail culture. Saying "감사합니다" (*gamsahamnida*) is enough.

Prices are set by each potter and are usually marked at the table or given on request. Pieces range from everyday rice bowls and cups to large display jars. Hands-on clay sessions have featured at past editions, and families tend to queue for those first.

## Getting your bowls home

Ceramics travel badly in a checked suitcase. Most potters wrap purchases in paper or bubble wrap. International visitors usually carry their pieces in hand luggage and nest small cups inside larger bowls.

A soft tote bag is the most useful thing to have at a pottery fair. Festival bags fill up quickly, and the walk back to a taxi or bus stop can be longer than it looks.

## Getting to Jillye from Busan and central Gimhae

Gimhae International Airport is on the city's eastern edge, close to Busan. The Busan–Gimhae Light Rail Transit links Sasang in Busan with the airport and central Gimhae, including the station for Suro King's Tomb.

Jillye is a fair distance west of the light rail line. From central Gimhae, most visitors take a taxi or a city bus out to the pottery district, and taxis are the simplest choice for anyone carrying ceramics. From Busan, the usual route is the light rail into Gimhae, then a taxi onward.

## Gaya Gimhae beyond the kilns

Gimhae was the seat of Geumgwan Gaya, one of the Gaya confederacy kingdoms. Its old centre pairs naturally with the pottery district.

- **Suro King's Tomb (수로왕릉)**: the burial mound of the legendary founder of Geumgwan Gaya, set in quiet walled grounds.
- **Gimhae National Museum (국립김해박물관)**: Gaya-period ironwork and grey stoneware. It also explains where Gimhae's ceramic tradition began, long before buncheong.
- **Daeseong-dong Tumuli (대성동고분군)**: an ancient Gaya burial ground near the museum, with a small on-site museum of its own.

The Gaya pottery in the National Museum and the buncheong of Jillye together span two thousand years of local clay. Busan's coast is also close, and many visitors stay in Busan and come over to Gimhae for the day.
