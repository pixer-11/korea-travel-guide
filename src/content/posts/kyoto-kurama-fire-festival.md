## What happens at Kurama on October 22

This is a Shinto fire ritual, not a parade for spectators. Villagers of Kurama carry pine torches, some over two meters long and heavy enough to need two hands, from their houses down the single main street to Yuki Shrine.

The torches are lit progressively through the evening, and by full dark the street is a corridor of open flame. Bonfires burn outside homes along the route beforehand; men in traditional dress chant "sairei, sairyo" as they haul the torches. The night ends with a shrine ritual and, in some years, a portable mikoshi carried down the shrine's stone steps.

## Getting to Kurama and finding the venue

Kurama is the last stop on the Eizan Electric Railway line from Demachiyanagi Station, about a 30-minute ride from central Kyoto. The village is small: one main road runs from the station straight past Yuki Shrine's torii gate.

There's no separate festival venue to hunt for. The event fills the entire street between the station and the shrine, so stepping off the train puts you at the site. The road typically narrows into a dense, standing-room crowd once torches start moving.

## Tickets and timing

There's no admission ticket for standing along the street to watch the torches pass; this is a public festival on public roads. Some structured viewing or shrine-related access may exist in a given year, and train operators sometimes run special restrictions or one-way crowd control on the return trip to Demachiyanagi.

Start times shift from year to year rather than following a fixed schedule. The Eizan Railway sets its own crowd management and train schedules for each festival night.

## Watching it like Kurama does

Locals treat this as a religious event first, spectacle second. A few customs and conditions shape the night:

- House entrances and the torch-bearers' path stay clear; the street is narrow and torches are heavy, hot, and moving.
- Photography is generally fine, though flash close to the torch-bearers' faces is frowned upon.
- Late October nights in the hills above Kyoto are cold, and viewing means standing for hours.
- Trains back to the city get congested late; the railway has historically imposed queuing or one-way exit systems on festival night.
- Space near Yuki Shrine's gate, closest to the shrine ritual itself, fills first, well before dusk.

The most common mistake is treating this as a photo-op parade and pushing forward for a better shot. The torches are real fire, carried by residents, on a street that doesn't widen for tourists.
