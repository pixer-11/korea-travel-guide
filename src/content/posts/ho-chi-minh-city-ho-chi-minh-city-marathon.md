Lê Duẩn Boulevard is wide, straight and lined with tamarind and oil trees. At its northern end stand the cream gates of Independence Palace. On a normal day this stretch carries a river of motorbikes. On Sunday, 17 January 2027, the motorbikes give way to runners: the Ho Chi Minh City Marathon, Vietnam's largest road race, starts and finishes here.

The palace makes an unusual start line. It's a 1960s modernist building, all concrete brise-soleil screens and long horizontal lines. It's also where the Vietnam War ended in April 1975, when a tank came through those gates. Few marathons start in front of a building with that much history.

## Sunday, 17 January 2027, at Independence Palace

The race takes place on **Sunday, 17 January 2027**. The start and finish are near Independence Palace (Dinh Độc Lập), also called Reunification Palace, in District 1.

That puts the race hub in the city's most walkable quarter. These landmarks are all within a few minutes of the finish:

- **Notre-Dame Cathedral Basilica of Saigon**, the red-brick French cathedral at the top of Đồng Khởi Street
- **Saigon Central Post Office**, beside the cathedral, with its iron-vaulted hall
- **Tao Đàn Park**, a shady green block next to the palace grounds
- **Bến Thành Market**, a short walk southeast

Most visiting runners stay in District 1 so they can walk to the start.

## Racing the sunrise

The hour matters more than the date. Mid-January falls in the middle of Saigon's dry season, the most comfortable stretch of the year. Days are still hot, though, and the humidity is real once the sun is up.

That's why big road races in Ho Chi Minh City traditionally start in the dark, before dawn. Most of the field runs the early kilometres under streetlights. The finishers on the longer distances come back as the heat builds.

The exact start time for each distance sits with the race information. Runners from cooler climates often slow their target pace on purpose. A personal best on Lê Duẩn is a different thing from one in Berlin.

## Getting to the start in the dark

Getting there is where the pre-dawn start bites. Roads on the course close for the morning, as at any city marathon. Some streets you would normally take are shut.

Runners staying in District 1 usually walk. The palace is roughly 15 minutes on foot from the Nguyễn Huệ walking street area. From further out, people mostly take ride-hailing apps such as Grab or Be. Drivers tend to drop runners a block or two from the closures, not at the gate.

Race morning has its own logistics. Supporters usually agree a meeting point beforehand, away from the finish chute. The cathedral steps and the edge of Tao Đàn Park are both easy to describe to a driver or a friend.

## Entries, bibs and where the details live

All entry information is on the race's official website. That includes the distance categories, registration, race-pack collection and the course map.

The marathon is open to international runners. That's a big part of why visitors fly in for it. There's a full marathon at the standard 42.195 km, plus shorter categories. The website lists the current set.

Bib and race-pack collection is a separate trip to the expo in the days before the race. Overseas runners usually arrive in the city with a day or two to spare to fit it in. They also use that time to adjust to the humidity.

## Watching from the boulevards

You don't need a bib to feel the race. District 1's boulevards are broad, with wide pavements. That gives spectators room to stand without blocking anyone.

The palace end is the obvious place to watch. That's where the start crowd gathers and where the finish noise builds. Some supporters walk a few blocks out along the course instead. There they can watch runners pass, then cut back on foot for the finish.

These are what spectators usually carry:

- A small torch or phone light for the walk before dawn
- Water, because the pavement heats up fast after sunrise
- A charged phone with the course map saved offline

## Race-morning manners in District 1

A city marathon in Vietnam brings its own etiquette. The street life doesn't stop for the race. It moves aside.

Street-food sellers and coffee carts often set up near the course, as they do anywhere crowds gather in Saigon. They usually take cash in Vietnamese đồng, so carry small notes. Ride-hailing apps work with in-app payment or cash.

Shout "**Cố lên!**" (roughly "keep going!") and Vietnamese runners will hear you. It's the standard cheer at any sporting event in the country. Locals out for their morning exercise in Tao Đàn Park take the race in their stride. Runners give the park's regulars room on the paths.

The palace is a national historic site, not just a backdrop. Runners and supporters usually keep the celebrating to the race zone outside the fence.

## After the finish: phở and the palace

Once the medals are handed out, the morning is still young. That's the real bonus of an early start. District 1's breakfast culture is in full swing as finishers walk out of the chute.

The usual recovery choices are close by:

- **Phở**, the beef or chicken noodle soup that many Saigon shops serve from early morning
- **Bánh mì**, the baguette sandwich sold from street carts
- **Cà phê sữa đá**, strong drip coffee over ice with condensed milk, which is not ideal straight after 42 km and very popular anyway

Independence Palace is open to visitors as a museum, and its hours vary. The official site has the current schedule. It keeps the 1975 furnishings, the war-room maps in the basement and the rooftop helipad. It makes a good unhurried visit the day after the race, when your legs are past their worst.

The War Remnants Museum is a few blocks northeast. The tree-lined lanes around the cathedral are a gentle recovery walk.

Mid-January also falls a few weeks before Tết, the Lunar New Year. The city starts its seasonal build-up around then. That's worth knowing if you stay on for a few days.
