## A town handed over to elephants

During the round-up, the elephants have right of way in Surin. The 2026 festival was set for **November 19-21, 2026**. Organisers announced more than 200 elephants for Surin, a provincial capital in Thailand's lower Isan region, near the Cambodian border.

At the round-up, the elephants walk the main roads with their mahouts on their necks. Later they fill a stadium for staged battles, races and a game of football. On feast day they line up along tables piled with fruit and vegetables. The festival is the reason most international visitors ever come to Surin.

## Why Surin, and why elephants

The elephants have deep roots in this province. The Kuy (also written Kui or Suay) people of Surin have caught, trained and lived with elephants for centuries. Many of the animals at the festival come from Kuy villages around Ban Ta Klang in Tha Tum district, north of town. Elephants and families still share homesteads there.

The show traditionally plays to that history, with:

- **Mock battles** with elephants in old war costume, recreating fights from the Siam-Burma wars
- **Elephant football**, which is chaotic and funny and draws the loudest cheers
- **Tug-of-war** between one elephant and a team of people, who usually lose
- **Kuy capture demonstrations** using ropes and lassos, showing how wild elephants were once taken
- **Costumed processions** with traditional dance and drumming before the elephants come on

## The feast and the stadium show

The festival has two main settings. One is the elephant banquet in town, where long rows of tables run down the street. They hold bananas, watermelon, pineapple, sugar cane and pumpkins, and the elephants work their way along them. Crowds stand right beside the tables. It is the closest spectators get to the animals all weekend.

The other is the main performance. It has traditionally been held at **Si Narong Stadium** in Surin town, with grandstand seating facing a dirt arena. The programme is typically long and staged, with commentary over loudspeakers, often in Thai and English.

Across the three days, the exact schedule for each event can change from year to year. The official sources for the festival are the Tourism Authority of Thailand (TAT) and Surin province.

## Getting to Surin and staying over

Most visitors come overland from Bangkok. The State Railway of Thailand's northeastern line stops at Surin railway station, close to the town centre. It's a long ride, and many people take a sleeper or an early day train. Buses run from Bangkok's Mo Chit terminal, and Surin has no commercial airport of its own.

Rooms are the tight part. Surin is not a large town, and hotels fill quickly for festival week. When the town is full, guesthouses further out and in nearby provinces such as Buriram take the overflow, with a journey in each day.

The feast and the stadium show together take at least two nights. Ban Ta Klang adds a day and shows where the elephants live the rest of the year.

## Seats, street etiquette and the elephants themselves

Most people decide where to watch long before the show starts. A few norms shape how the round-up works for spectators.

- **Stadium tickets.** Reserved grandstand seats are usually sold through the official channels or local tour operators.
- **Street spots.** The kerbside spots at the feast go to whoever turns up first, and there's no assigned standing room.
- **Distance from the elephants.** Spectators keep their distance unless a mahout invites them closer. Walking behind an elephant is unsafe, and feeding one needs the mahout's say-so.
- **Cash in Thai baht.** Street stalls and vendors selling fruit for the elephants mostly take small notes. Big hotels take cards, but plenty of street vendors don't.
- **Heat and dust.** November is in Isan's cool, dry season, but the arena is open and the ground is churned dirt.
- **A few words of Thai.** *Chang* means elephant, and *khop khun khrap/kha* is thank you. Both go down well with mahouts and stallholders.

## Going in with your eyes open

Elephant shows in Thailand are debated. Animal welfare groups criticise performances such as football and painting, and many of the elephants here work in tourism the rest of the year. The festival puts the handling of the animals in plain view, so visitors can judge for themselves what they are comfortable paying for.

Elephants without the performance are found in the villages around Ban Ta Klang. There the elephants live alongside their families, and a visit shows Kuy life outside festival week.
