## A town handed over to elephants

For three days, the elephants have right of way in Surin. The festival is announced for **November 19-21, 2026**. More than 200 elephants come into this provincial capital in Thailand's lower Isan region, near the Cambodian border.

They walk the main roads with their mahouts on their necks. Later they fill a stadium for staged battles, races and a game of football. On feast day they line up along tables piled with fruit and vegetables. The festival is the reason most international visitors ever come to Surin.

## Why Surin, and why elephants

The elephants have deep roots in this province. The Kuy (also written Kui or Suay) people of Surin have caught, trained and lived with elephants for centuries. Many of the animals at the festival come from Kuy villages around Ban Ta Klang in Tha Tum district, north of town. Elephants and families still share homesteads there.

The show plays to that history. You'll see:

- **Mock battles** with elephants in old war costume, recreating fights from the Siam-Burma wars
- **Elephant football**, which is chaotic and funny and draws the loudest cheers
- **Tug-of-war** between one elephant and a team of people, who usually lose
- **Kuy capture demonstrations** using ropes and lassos, showing how wild elephants were once taken
- **Costumed processions** with traditional dance and drumming before the elephants come on

## The feast and the stadium show

The festival has two main settings. One is the elephant banquet in town, where long rows of tables run down the street. They hold bananas, watermelon, pineapple, sugar cane and pumpkins, and the elephants work their way along them. Crowds stand right beside the tables. This is the closest you will get to the animals all weekend.

The other is the main performance. It has traditionally been held at **Si Narong Stadium** in Surin town, with grandstand seating facing a dirt arena. Expect a long, staged programme with commentary over loudspeakers, often in Thai and English.

Over three days, the exact schedule for each event can change from year to year. Confirm timing, venue and tickets on the official source, either the Tourism Authority of Thailand (TAT) or Surin province.

## Getting to Surin and staying over

Most visitors come overland from Bangkok. The State Railway of Thailand's northeastern line stops at Surin railway station, close to the town centre. It's a long ride, so many people take a sleeper or an early day train. Buses run from Bangkok's Mo Chit terminal, and Surin has no commercial airport of its own.

Rooms are the tight part. Surin is not a large town, and hotels fill quickly for festival week. Book as early as you can. If town is full, look at guesthouses further out or in nearby provinces such as Buriram, and plan your transport in.

Give it at least two nights so you can see both the feast and the stadium show. Add a day for Ban Ta Klang if you want to see where the elephants live the rest of the year.

## Seats, street etiquette and the elephants themselves

Most people decide where to watch from long before the show starts. Here is how to get a good view without getting in the way.

- **Buy stadium tickets in advance where you can.** Reserved grandstand seats are usually sold through the official channels or local tour operators. Check the official site for how sales work.
- **Arrive early for the street events.** The kerbside spots at the feast go to whoever turns up first, and there's no assigned standing room.
- **Keep your distance unless a mahout invites you closer.** Never walk behind an elephant, and don't feed one yourself without the mahout saying yes.
- **Carry small notes in Thai baht.** Street stalls and vendors selling fruit for the elephants mostly take cash. Big hotels take cards, but plenty of street vendors don't.
- **Dress for heat and dust.** November is in Isan's cool, dry season, but the arena is open and the ground is churned dirt.
- **Learn a few words.** *Chang* means elephant, and *khop khun khrap/kha* is thank you. Both go down well with mahouts and stallholders.

## Going in with your eyes open

Elephant shows in Thailand are debated. Animal welfare groups criticise performances such as football and painting, and many of the elephants here work in tourism the rest of the year. Seeing the festival lets you judge for yourself. You can watch how the animals are handled, then decide what you're comfortable paying for afterwards.

If you'd rather see elephants without the performance, visit the villages around Ban Ta Klang. There the elephants live alongside their families. The trip shows you Kuy life outside festival week.
