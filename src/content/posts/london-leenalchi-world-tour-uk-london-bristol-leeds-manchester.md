Picture a stage with no guitar at all: two bass guitars, a drum kit and a row of singers who sound like they've walked out of a Joseon-era storytelling hall. That's a Leenalchi show. The band's UK run takes place from **November 26 to 29, 2026**, with stops in London, Bristol, Leeds and Manchester.

## The sound you're walking into

Leenalchi takes pansori and puts it on a groove. Pansori is Korea's centuries-old sung storytelling, and the band builds on it rather than borrowing from it. The singers use the rough, strained, deliberately cracked tone that pansori training produces. Underneath, the bass lines are clean and repetitive, close to dance music.

The band is named after Lee Nal-chi, a celebrated pansori singer of the late Joseon period. Much of its best-known material comes from *Sugungga*, the "Underwater Palace Song". It's the comic tale of a turtle who tricks a rabbit into visiting the Dragon King. You don't need Korean to follow the drama, because pansori singers act out every character with voice and gesture.

## Where "Tiger Is Coming" came from

Most UK audiences first met the band through one song. "Tiger Is Coming" (*Beom Naeryeo-onda*) soundtracked the Korea Tourism Organization's 2020 "Feel the Rhythm of Korea" videos. In those clips, Ambiguous Dance Company danced through Seoul, Busan and other cities in hanbok-meets-streetwear outfits.

The videos went viral around the world, and the track became a calling card. At live shows the band plays it as a full performance piece, not a quick nod to the hit. Fans who came for that one song usually leave knowing three or four more.

## Shouting back: how a pansori crowd behaves

In a traditional pansori performance, silence is not the polite option. Listeners call out *chuimsae*, short shouts of encouragement aimed at the singer. They go up when a line lands or a note holds. The most common ones are:

- **"Eolssigu!"**: a joyful "that's it!"
- **"Jota!"**: "good!"
- **"Jalhanda!"**: "well done!"
- **"Eolsseeong!"**: a drawn-out cheer after a big passage

The singers often teach these from the stage. Korean audience members in the room tend to lead the way, and UK crowds usually catch on quickly. By "Tiger Is Coming", much of the room is moving. Fans often copy the loose, bouncy dance from the tourism videos.

Phones go up for that song more than any other. Otherwise these shows reward watching rather than filming, because the singers' faces carry half the story.

## Making a night of it in London

London has a large Korean community, and it shapes what people do before and after the gig. The Korean restaurant cluster around Soho and Tottenham Court Road is the natural pre-show stop for central venues. There you'll find barbecue grills built into the tables, bibimbap served in hot stone bowls, and fried chicken alongside beer.

New Malden, in south-west London, is the city's best-known Korean neighbourhood. It has grocery stores, bakeries and restaurants along the High Street. Fans travelling in from outside London often build a daytime visit there around the evening concert.

If you're following the band across more than one city, rail links make it workable. Trains from London Euston run to Manchester Piccadilly, from King's Cross to Leeds, and from Paddington to Bristol Temple Meads.
