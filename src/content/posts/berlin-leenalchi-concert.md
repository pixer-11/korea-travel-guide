## A tiger in the stables

Picture brick vaults and cast-iron columns. Gretchen sits in the old stables of a Prussian guard dragoon regiment on Obentrautstraße in Kreuzberg. Horses once stood where the dance floor is now, and the arched ceilings and pillars from that era still carry the sound.

On November 15, 2026, LEENALCHI plays that room. The South Korean group is known as a pansori-fusion band. Their songs take sung storytelling from 19th-century Korea and put it over a rhythm section that sounds closer to a dance club than a concert hall.

## Who LEENALCHI are

The band formed in Seoul in 2019. It takes its name from Lee Nal-chi, a Joseon-era performer remembered as both a tightrope acrobat and a pansori singer. It's a fitting choice for a group that treats a centuries-old tradition as something to move to.

LEENALCHI started out with two bass guitars, a drummer and several trained pansori vocalists. Fans of Talking Heads or post-punk often hear the family resemblance straight away: tight, repetitive grooves, deadpan movement, and voices pitched somewhere between chant and shout.

Pansori itself is a genre recognised by UNESCO. Traditionally, one singer performs an epic story for hours, accompanied only by a barrel drum. LEENALCHI cut those stories into songs of a few minutes and swap the drum for low end.

## "Tiger is Coming" and the Sugungga story

Most people in a Berlin crowd know one song. "Beom Naeryeo Onda" ("Tiger is Coming") broke out of Korea in 2020. That year it soundtracked the "Feel the Rhythm of Korea" tourism videos, made with Ambiguous Dance Company, which racked up views worldwide.

The track comes from the band's debut album, *Sugungga*. The record reworks one of the five surviving classical pansori epics. In the story, a turtle travels from the Dragon King's undersea palace to fetch a rabbit's liver as a cure. The rabbit has other ideas.

You don't need Korean to follow it. The singers play the characters with their voices and bodies: the tiger growls, the rabbit bargains, and the bass keeps pushing underneath. Plenty of first-timers leave humming a melody they couldn't spell.

## Getting to Gretchen in Kreuzberg

Gretchen's address is Obentrautstraße 19–21, 10963 Berlin. Your easiest options are:

- **Mehringdamm** (U6, U7): about a five-minute walk, the usual choice
- **Möckernbrücke** (U1, U3, U7): a short walk west along the canal side of the district
- **Night transport**: the U-Bahn runs through the night on Friday and Saturday. Other nights, night buses take over after the trains stop

The streets around the club are mostly residential, and the entrance is easy to walk past. Concertgoers usually head down Mehringdamm and turn into Obentrautstraße, keeping an eye out for the courtyard and the brick stable building.

## Inside the vaults: how a Gretchen night works

The room is a concert floor, so most of the audience stands. The columns break up sightlines in places. People who want a clear view of the singers tend to drift to the middle, where the vaulted ceiling opens up above the stage.

In November you'll arrive in a heavy coat. Berlin clubs almost always run a coat check (*Garderobe*), and most locals use it rather than holding a parka through a dancing set. Bar drinks often come with a *Pfand*, a deposit you get back when you return the glass or bottle.

Some Berlin venues still lean towards cash at the bar and cloakroom, though cards are increasingly accepted. Many Berliners carry a few notes just in case. Smoking and photo rules depend on the venue, and staff at the door explain them.

## Shouting "eolsu!": how the crowd joins in

Pansori has a built-in call-and-response. In the tradition, listeners and the drummer shout encouragement called *chuimsae* at key moments: "eolsu!", "jota!" (good!) or "jalhanda!" (well done!). Silence is not the polite response here. A well-timed shout tells the singer the story is landing.

Korean fans at LEENALCHI shows often bring this habit with them. Berlin audiences pick it up fast once a few voices start. The cue is usually a held note, a sudden stop in the groove, or the moment a character gets the better of another.

The band's choreography is part of the fun too. Their deliberately stiff, gestural moves from the tourism videos became a meme of their own. Expect to see people around you copying the hand movements during "Tiger is Coming".

## Making a night of it around Mehringdamm

Gretchen is on the edge of Kreuzberg 61, one of the city's most walkable pockets. A few nearby options for before or after the show:

- **Curry 36**, right by Mehringdamm station, Berlin's best-known currywurst stand
- **Bergmannstraße**, a street of cafés, bars and restaurants a few minutes south
- **Viktoriapark**, with its hill and the Schinkel-designed National Monument for the Wars of Liberation at the top
- **Deutsches Technikmuseum**, near Möckernbrücke, for a daytime visit the same weekend

If you're travelling in for the gig, staying in Kreuzberg means the walk home from the club is short. Neighbouring Schöneberg works too, with Friedrichshain an easy U-Bahn ride away. The U6 also gets you to Friedrichstraße and Mitte in a few stops.
