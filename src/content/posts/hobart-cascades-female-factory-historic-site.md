## High walls under the mountain

Degraves Street is a quiet residential road in South Hobart. Then the houses stop and a wall begins, tall, sandstone and windowless, running along the street with kunanyi/Mount Wellington rising behind it. That wall is most of what survives, and it was built to do one job: keep women in and the world out.

Inside, much of the site is open ground. The yards hold grass, gravel and the outlines of buildings that are long gone, so you fill in the cells, workrooms and nurseries yourself.

## What this place was

Between 1828 and 1856 this was the main female factory in Van Diemen's Land. Convict women transported from Britain and Ireland were held here between assignments, punished here for offences, and set to work here. The jobs included laundry, sewing and picking oakum.

Pregnant women were sent here too, and the infant death rate in the nursery was severe. The site is now one of the eleven places that make up the Australian Convict Sites World Heritage listing, alongside Port Arthur. It is the one that tells the women's side of the convict story.

## Walking the yards

The site is small, and the visit is mostly outdoors. Give yourself an hour to read the yards at your own pace, or closer to two if you join a guided or dramatised tour. Those tours are the reason many people come. They put individual convict women's records into a voice, which lands harder than any panel.

What to look for:

- **The perimeter walls**, which show how enclosed daily life was
- **Interpretive markers** tracing where the vanished buildings stood
- **The Matron's Cottage**, the surviving building, and its exhibits
- **Names and convict records** placed through the grounds

Hobart weather changes fast at the foot of the mountain. Bring a warm layer even in summer, and a waterproof shell outside it. There is little cover in the open yards.

## Getting to South Hobart

The site sits about 2.5 kilometres from the city centre. The most rewarding approach is on foot along the **Hobart Rivulet track**, a shared path that follows the creek upstream from near the city. It ends close to Degraves Street, and the walk takes roughly 30 to 40 minutes on a gentle uphill.

Metro Tasmania buses run from the city along Macquarie Street towards South Hobart and the Cascade Brewery area. Check the Metro journey planner for the current route. Street parking is available nearby if you are driving, but the surrounding streets are residential, so respect driveways.

## Before you go through the gate

Book the tours ahead. Times are set and groups are capped, so turning up and hoping can mean a long wait or a missed slot. Booking runs through the Port Arthur Historic Site Management Authority, which operates the site. Ticket prices are posted on its official website and at reception, and card payment is the norm in Tasmania.

Go early in the day if you can. You get more time in the yards before the 4:30pm close, and a first tour leaves room to wander afterwards. Hours are the same every day of the week, 9:30am to 4:30pm.

Treat it as a memorial as much as a museum. Many visitors are Tasmanians tracing a convict ancestor, and you may see someone standing over a name. Keep voices low and step around them. If you think you have a convict woman in your own family tree, ask at reception: researchers here know where the records live.

## The rest of the afternoon

The **Cascade Brewery**, Australia's oldest operating brewery, is a short walk up the road. Its stone facade and the hillside gardens make a natural second stop, and you can book a brewery tour there too. Turn the Rivulet walk into a loop: up the track to the factory, on to the brewery, then back downhill into the city.

If the factory stays with you, plan a day trip to **Port Arthur** on the Tasman Peninsula. It shows the men's side of the same convict system, and your visit here gives it context. Closer in, **Battery Point** and **Salamanca Place** are both reachable on foot when you get back to town.
