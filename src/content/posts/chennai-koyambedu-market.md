## A market that never locks its gates

Koyambedu Market runs through the night. Its listed hours are 24 hours, seven days a week, and that fits how a wholesale market works. Produce from farms across Tamil Nadu and the neighbouring states comes in by lorry overnight. Traders have to sell it on to retailers, hotel kitchens and street vendors before the city wakes up.

This is not a tourist market dressed up for visitors. It is Chennai's food supply chain, laid out in concrete sheds and open lanes. You come to see how a city of millions gets fed. You will spend your time dodging handcarts and learning the Tamil names for vegetables.

It is also very well known. Chennai residents shop here in bulk, photographers come for the pre-dawn light, and plenty of visitors add it to a city itinerary. Expect company, especially in the flower section before festivals.

## Three markets, one sprawling complex

The official name is the Koyambedu Wholesale Market Complex. It opened in the 1990s to take the wholesale trade out of the congested lanes near George Town, and it is often described as one of the largest perishable-goods markets in Asia. It splits into distinct zones, and it helps to know which one you want:

- **Vegetable market:** the largest part, with rows of stalls stacked with onions, tomatoes, drumsticks, gourds, greens and chillies, sold mostly in sacks and crates.
- **Fruit market:** the Anna Fruit Market block, where the Google pin for this listing sits (T/A-118). Bananas hang in whole bunches and seasonal fruit arrives in cartons and baskets.
- **Flower market:** the most photographed section, piled with loose blooms sold by weight for temples, weddings and garlands.

Give yourself one and a half to two hours to walk all three without rushing. If you only have time for one, pick the flower market.

## Jasmine by the kilo

The flower market is the part that sticks with people. Chennai gets through flowers in huge quantities. They go into temple offerings, wedding garlands, car dashboards and the strands of jasmine (*malli* in Tamil) that women pin into their hair. Here they arrive loose, heaped on sheets and sold by weight rather than by the stem.

Marigolds come in orange and yellow drifts, alongside roses, chrysanthemums (*samanthi*) and tuberose. Garland-makers work at the edges, threading blooms at speed. Around big festivals such as Pongal, Vinayagar Chaturthi and Diwali, and through the wedding season, the trade swells and prices move sharply. The lanes pack tight on those days.

You can buy a small quantity of jasmine for your hotel room. Most flower traders will sell a smaller amount if you ask politely, though this is a wholesale floor first.

## Getting to Koyambedu

The market sits on the western side of the city, beside the Chennai Mofussil Bus Terminus (CMBT), the city's main long-distance bus station. That puts it on the network whichever way you travel.

- **Metro:** Koyambedu Metro Station on the Green Line (Line 2) is the closest stop. CMBT station on the same line also works, depending on which side of the complex you want.
- **Auto-rickshaw or ride-hail:** tell the driver "Koyambedu market" and name the section, such as "flower market" or "fruit market". The complex is big enough that the drop-off point matters.
- **Bus:** a huge number of city routes end at or pass the Koyambedu and CMBT area.

In the pre-dawn hours the metro will not be running, so an auto or app cab is the realistic option then.

## Walking the lanes without blocking the trade

This is a working floor, and the people here are at work. A few habits will make your visit smoother and win you some goodwill.

- **Porters have right of way.** Men carrying sacks on their heads or pushing loaded carts do not stop for you. When you hear a shout behind you, step to the side.
- **Wear closed shoes you can wash.** Floors are wet, and squashed produce and flower waste collect in the gutters. Sandals are a poor choice.
- **Ask before photographing people.** A gesture towards your camera usually works. Most traders are fine with it, but some are too busy, and a stall mid-sale is not the time.
- **Pay with UPI or small notes.** UPI QR codes are common across Chennai's markets, but foreign visitors often cannot use UPI. Carry small denominations, since a trader may not have change for a large note.
- **Bargain lightly and only if you mean to buy.** Haggling is normal for wholesale quantities. Asking the price of everything with no plan to buy wastes a trader's peak hours.
- **A few Tamil words help.** *Evvalavu?* means "how much?", and *vanakkam* is a greeting. Traders appreciate the effort.

Prices change with the day's supply. Ask at the stall, never assume.

## How the seasons change the stock

What you see depends on when you come. Summer brings mangoes to the fruit market in bulk, in varieties such as Banganapalli and Alphonso, stacked in straw-lined crates. In the cooler months you will find more winter vegetables, and Pongal in January turns sugarcane and turmeric plants into a feature of the lanes.

The northeast monsoon, roughly October to December, can leave the open areas muddy. The market keeps trading through it, but pack for wet ground. Festival weeks are the most dramatic time for the flower section and also the most crowded.

On timing, go before dawn if you want to watch lorries unload and wholesale deals close fast. That is the reason the market runs all night. A later morning visit is calmer to walk, but the best stock will already have been bought.

## Before and after the market

Koyambedu is not a sightseeing district, so most visitors combine the market with their onward travel. It works well just before a long-distance bus from CMBT, or on the way into the city from the western suburbs.

Small tea stalls and tiffin shops around the complex serve the traders. A tumbler of filter coffee or a plate of idli after an early market walk is the local routine. For a proper sit-down meal or shopping, the Anna Nagar and Vadapalani areas are a short auto ride away.
