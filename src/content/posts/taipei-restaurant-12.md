## What sets Restaurant 12 apart

Restaurant 12 earned its One MICHELIN Star recently, part of a new wave of chefs treating Taiwan's farms and coastline as a pantry rather than a backdrop.

Chef Jonatan Motos Ferran isn't a local name with a predictable resume. He's cooked professionally across Spain, Mexico, and the Middle East before landing in Taipei, and that itinerary shows up on the plate. Expect classic French technique, sauces, precise plating, but built around ingredients you'd recognize from a Taiwanese wet market rather than imported staples.

## What to expect at the table

Tasting-menu restaurants at this level in Taipei are almost always a set multi-course format rather than à la carte, paced over a couple of hours. Come hungry, come with time, and don't schedule anything tight immediately afterward.

Given the chef's background, expect the kitchen to lean on French plating and sauce work while sourcing seafood, vegetables, and perhaps game or offal cuts from Taiwanese producers. Don't expect a Chinese-Taiwanese menu with French garnish; expect the reverse structure, French technique in service of local ingredients.

## Ordering and etiquette at a Taipei tasting counter

Taiwanese fine dining shares a lot of French-restaurant etiquette by now, but a few local habits are worth knowing before you sit down.

- Tipping is not customary in Taiwan; a service charge, if any, is usually built into the bill rather than added at the table.
- Payment is typically by card; carrying some cash is still wise as backup for smaller or newer venues.
- Arrive on time. Tasting menus are timed to the kitchen's pass, and a late arrival can throw off a tightly choreographed service for the whole room.
- If you have dietary restrictions, flag them when booking, not at the table; a chef-driven kitchen like this often plans substitutions in advance rather than on the fly.
