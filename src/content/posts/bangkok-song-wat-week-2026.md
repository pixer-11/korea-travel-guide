## Four days on a working wholesale street

Song Wat Road is no festival ground. It is a long, narrow trading street where shophouse shutters roll up each morning onto sacks of dried chillies, coffee beans and sesame, and pickup trucks load at the kerb.

From November 19 to 22, 2026, Song Wat Week moves food, art, creative projects and brand collaborations onto that same strip. The draw is the contrast. An installation might sit two doors from a shop that has sold star anise by the kilo for decades.

## Song Wat Road between the river and Sampeng

The road runs parallel to the Chao Phraya, one block in from the water. Sampeng Lane, Chinatown's packed wholesale alley, sits a block inland, with Yaowarat Road beyond that.

It was laid out during the reign of King Rama V, when river trade drove Bangkok's commerce. The architecture shows it:

- two- and three-storey shophouses with deep ground-floor trading rooms
- old warehouses whose back doors once opened toward the river
- narrow sois cutting down to piers and old Chinese clan houses

In recent years, cafés, small galleries and painted murals have moved in among the wholesalers. Song Wat Week builds on that mix, and the street keeps working through it.

## Dates, tickets and what's on

Song Wat Week 2026 runs from Thursday, November 19 to Sunday, November 22, 2026. It is held along Song Wat Road itself, so the venue is the street and the buildings that open onto it.

The programme covers four strands: food, art, creative projects and brand collaborations. Our facts don't include a lineup, set times or entry prices, and we won't guess at them. The organiser publishes the dates, venue details and any ticket links on its official channels.

Street events like this are usually free to walk through, with some individual workshops or pop-ups run separately. Treat that as a general pattern, not a promise for this edition.

## Getting to Song Wat by boat, train or on foot

The river is the easiest way in. Chao Phraya Express boats stop at Ratchawong pier, and Ratchawong Road leads straight up from the pier and crosses Song Wat within a couple of minutes.

By train, Wat Mangkon station on the MRT Blue Line is the closest. From there it is a walk south through Chinatown's lanes, past Sampeng, to the road. Expect roughly 10–15 minutes depending on the route and the crowds.

Taxis and Grab cars can reach the area but crawl through Chinatown traffic. Many visitors take a car to the edge of the district and walk the last stretch. Song Wat is narrow, and on a busy festival evening it is faster on foot.

## Sacks, spices and shutters: the street's everyday trade

This is what makes Song Wat Week unusual. The street has a daily rhythm of its own, and the event lays itself over it.

Look past the programme and you'll find the trade that gave the road its name in Bangkok's food world:

- dried goods shops with open sacks of chillies, peppercorns and dried shrimp
- coffee and tea wholesalers working from old shopfronts
- shops selling cooking oils, seeds and Chinese pantry staples in bulk

Many of these are wholesale businesses with family histories. They tend to open in the morning and close in the late afternoon, which is a habit of the street, not a fixed schedule. Visitors who want both the shops and the festival often walk the road in daylight, then come back as the evening programme fills it.

## Moving through Song Wat Week with the street

Remember that this is a working road. People here are earning a living, and a little awareness goes a long way.

- **Keep loading bays clear.** Trolleys and trucks still move goods during the day; step aside when they come through.
- **Ask before photographing shopkeepers.** The shophouse interiors are photogenic, but they are workplaces, and a gesture toward your camera is usually enough of a question.
- **Pay the Thai way.** Small food vendors in Bangkok mostly take cash or a scan of the PromptPay QR code on the counter, and international cards don't always work there. Tipping at street stalls isn't expected.
- **Learn the name.** Taxi drivers know it as *Thanon Song Wat* (ถนนทรงวาด).
- **Say thanks.** A smile and *khop khun khrap/kha* (thank you) is the standard courtesy.

November falls at the start of Bangkok's cooler, drier season. Evenings are usually the most comfortable time on the street, and that is when festival-goers tend to gather. The sois off Song Wat are useful for stepping out of the flow.

## Around the edges: Yaowarat, Talat Noi and Wat Mangkon

Song Wat Week is easy to fold into a longer Chinatown day.

- **Yaowarat Road**: Chinatown's main artery. After dark it fills with street food stalls and neon shop signs, a few minutes' walk inland.
- **Sampeng Lane (Soi Wanit 1)**: a tight, covered wholesale alley of fabrics, trinkets and packaging, running parallel to Song Wat.
- **Talat Noi**: the riverside neighbourhood to the east, with old Chinese houses, the So Heng Tai mansion and plenty of murals.
- **Wat Mangkon Kamalawat**: Bangkok's best-known Chinese Buddhist temple, near the MRT station of the same name.

Most visitors spend two to three hours on Song Wat itself. Add a Yaowarat dinner or a Talat Noi wander and it easily becomes an afternoon and evening.
