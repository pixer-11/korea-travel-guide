## A plaster room from 1315

The building is small. A narrow door on Calle Judíos opens onto a little courtyard, and past that is one prayer hall, almost square. A women's gallery runs above it. Every wall is covered in carved stucco: vine and geometric patterns set in bands of Hebrew script, which quote the Psalms and give the building's own dedication.

That inscription dates the room to 1314–1315 and names its founder, Isaac Moheb. It is the only medieval synagogue still standing in Andalusia. The only other ones in Spain are the two in Toledo.

Its later uses kept it standing. After the Jews were expelled in 1492, the room served as a hospital for rabies patients. After that it became a chapel for the shoemakers' guild. Workers found the stucco under later plaster in the 1880s, and Spain declared it a national monument in 1885.

## Finding Calle Judíos

The walk is the best part of getting there. From the Mezquita-Catedral, head northwest through the whitewashed lanes of the Judería towards the Puerta de Almodóvar, the old gate in the city walls. Calle Judíos runs just inside it.

On the way you pass:

- **Plaza de Tiberíades**, just past the synagogue, with the seated bronze statue of Maimonides, the Córdoba-born philosopher
- **Casa de Sefarad**, a small museum of Sephardic Jewish life, across the lane
- **Zoco Municipal**, a courtyard of craft workshops a few doors away
- **Puerta de Almodóvar**, with the walls and a stretch of garden running south outside the gate

Old-town lanes are too narrow for cars. Leave the car in a car park outside the historic centre and walk in.

## Timing the one small room

It keeps short hours, and the room fills quickly. It opens 9am to 3pm, Tuesday to Sunday, and is **closed all day Monday**. That catches out a lot of people who plan a Monday in Córdoba. There are no afternoon or evening openings, so fit it into your morning.

On weekends it is busiest from 11am until closing at 3pm, and by then a queue often reaches out along the lane. Come at 9am opening, before the day-trippers arrive. A Tuesday to Friday morning is the easiest option.

Plan on 20 to 30 minutes. Most of that is standing still and reading the walls, which is all a room this size calls for.

## Reading the walls properly

How you enter is set by the building's size. Only a small group fits inside at once, so staff usually hold visitors at the courtyard until space frees up. Wait there instead of pushing into the doorway. Entry details, including any current charge, are posted at the door and on the official Junta de Andalucía site.

Inside, look up before you look around. The best stucco runs high on the east wall around the niche where the Torah scrolls were kept, and along the gallery above. Stand in the middle of the hall to take in all four walls, then move close to see how the Hebrew letters are cut into the pattern.

This is a heritage site, not a working congregation, but treat it as a place of worship. Keep your voice down, and don't lean on the walls: the stucco is 700 years old. Afterwards, go across to Casa de Sefarad. It explains the community that built this room and lost it within two centuries.
