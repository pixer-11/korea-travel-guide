## Five days of panels in the hills of Bali

The 2026 festival was set for 21 to 25 October. The 2025 edition ran from 29 October to 2 November, so late October is when it usually happens. Up to 170 writers and artists come from around the world, which makes it Southeast Asia's largest literary festival.

Janet DeNeefe founded it in 2004 as a response to the 2002 Bali bombings, which badly hurt the island's tourism. The programme mixes international names with Indonesian writers. It features novelists, poets, journalists and activists, with some sessions in Bahasa Indonesia and many in English.

## Where it happens in Ubud

Ubud is a town in the hills, roughly 1 to 1.5 hours by road from Ngurah Rai International Airport (DPS). The drive depends heavily on traffic through Denpasar and Gianyar. There is no train, and buses are not a practical way to get there. Most visitors use a private driver or a pre-arranged airport transfer.

In recent editions, the main programme has been based along Jalan Raya Sanggingan, at the north-western edge of town. Side events have been spread through central Ubud. The venues can change from year to year.

Sanggingan is within walking distance of that main programme area. It is also close to the Campuhan Ridge Walk, a grassy spine between two river valleys that is at its best early in the morning. Ubud Palace and Ubud Art Market are a short drive down the hill, on Jalan Raya Ubud.

## Tickets, passes and planning

The programme usually has two layers:
- **Main programme:** panel sessions, sold as passes
- **Special events:** workshops, literary lunches, book launches and evening performances, ticketed separately

Some community and children's events have been free in past years. Resellers and third-party listings are not a reliable source for ticket types or prices.

October falls at the end of Bali's dry season, with warm, humid days and the chance of an afternoon downpour. Many sessions are held in open-sided pavilions, which give shade but are not air-conditioned. Useful items in this climate:
- a light layer for evenings, when the hills cool down
- a refillable water bottle
- a small umbrella

## Moving between sessions in festival week

Ubud's traffic is the real constraint. The main roads are narrow, and during big events they fill with festival-goers, scooters and tour vans. Getting between venues takes longer than the distances suggest. Many regulars stay at one venue for a day, walk, or hire a scooter rather than wait for a car.

App-based ride-hailing (Grab, Gojek) works in parts of Ubud. Some central streets restrict pickups by app drivers, so riders often walk to a main road or use a local driver. Most locals pay drivers in cash in rupiah, and the fare is usually agreed before the ride.

Small palm-leaf offerings called *canang sari* sit on pavements and doorsteps. The custom is to step around them, not on them. Temple compounds require a sarong and sash, which are usually available to borrow at the entrance.

Book signings usually follow sessions, often with the festival's bookshop selling titles on site. Popular authors draw long queues soon after a session ends. Popular special events can sell out, while main-programme sessions are easier to fit around them.
