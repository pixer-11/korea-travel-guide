## Ice on the parade ground

Independence Square is Tashkent's ceremonial centre. It's a wide, formal space of fountains, tree-lined avenues and the white Senate building, built for state occasions. In January the festival fills it with carved ice, music and food stalls.

The festival runs through January 2027, and the exact days change each year. It spreads out from the square into the nearby parks. That makes it a walking event, with no single gate or hall.

What it offers, according to the festival's own description:

- **Ice sculpture competitions**, with carvers working blocks into finished pieces
- **Traditional music performances**, with Uzbek instruments and song in the open air
- **Seasonal food stalls** for warming up between sculptures

## Reaching Mustaqillik Maydoni

The metro gets you closest. Mustaqillik Maydoni station on the Chilonzor Line (Line 1) is named after the square and opens onto its edge. Tashkent's older stations are decorated halls in their own right, so the ride counts as part of the outing.

Taxis booked through ride-hailing apps are cheap by European standards and common in the city centre. At festival peaks, drivers usually drop people a block or two away from the square, on the surrounding avenues.

Landmarks within walking distance:

- **The Arch of Good and Noble Aspirations** (Ezgulik Arch), carved with storks, at the square's entrance
- **The Mourning Mother monument** and its memorial gallery, inside the square
- **Amir Timur Square**, with the equestrian statue, about a 15-minute walk east
- **Sayilgoh Street**, the pedestrian "Broadway", which runs off Amir Timur Square

## Doing the square the Tashkent way

Families make up much of the crowd. Grandparents, parents and small children tend to move through together and stop at every sculpture for photos. The pace is slow, and people usually loop the square more than once.

- **Paying:** Uzbek som in cash is the default at small stalls. Many vendors also take local cards or the Payme and Click apps, but foreign cards are hit-and-miss at stalls.
- **Food:** the stalls sell seasonal food. Around Tashkent in winter that usually means hot tea (*choy*), samsa from the tandoor and skewered meat.
- **Greetings:** "Assalomu alaykum" works anywhere. "Rahmat" means thank you.
- **Photos:** performers and carvers are usually happy to be photographed. Asking first with a gesture is the polite norm.
- **Official zones:** the Senate building and state areas of the square stay off-limits even during the festival. Police presence is normal here, so follow their directions.

## Dressing for a Tashkent January

Tashkent's January has a continental chill. Daytime temperatures hover around freezing, nights drop below it, and snow is possible. Ice sculptures only survive in that cold, which is why the festival sits in midwinter.

Most visitors spend one to three hours here, depending on how many stages and parks they cover. Sculptures look different by daylight than when lit after dark. Many people come back for a second look once the lamps along the avenues are on.

The square's paving is wide and flat but can be icy. Sturdy footwear with grip makes more difference here than an extra layer. Warm gloves help too, if you're holding a camera or a cup of tea for long.
