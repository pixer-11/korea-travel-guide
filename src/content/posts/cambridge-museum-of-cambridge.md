## An old inn on Castle Hill

Castle Street climbs north out of the city centre. Most visitors never get this far. Cross Magdalene Bridge, keep walking uphill past the colleges, and the street quietens. On a corner stands a timber-framed building that looks older than everything around it, because it is.

This is the Museum of Cambridge. For centuries the building was the White Horse Inn. In the 1930s it became the Cambridge and County Folk Museum. The colleges tell the university's story. This place tells the story of everyone else in the town and the surrounding county.

## Low ceilings, crooked floors, ordinary lives

Inside, the building sets the pace. A 17th-century timber frame gives you small rooms joined by narrow, steep stairs. The floors are uneven and the doorways are low, so taller visitors learn to duck. The museum feels more like a house you are wandering through than a gallery.

The collection is about everyday Cambridgeshire life. Expect:

- Domestic objects from kitchens and households
- Tools and objects from local trades and working life
- Paintings of the town and county
- Folklore, including the charms and folk cures people once relied on

The folklore material is the part people remember. It is a glimpse of how ordinary people tried to keep illness and bad luck away long before modern medicine. Read the labels slowly. In a museum this size, the detail is the point.

## Getting to Castle Street

The address is 2-3 Castle St, CB3 0AQ. From the Market Square, walk down Bridge Street, cross Magdalene Bridge over the Cam and carry on uphill. The walk takes roughly 10–15 minutes and passes Magdalene College on the way.

From Cambridge railway station it is a long walk across the city. A bus into the centre is the easier option, then walk up the hill. Driving into central Cambridge is slow and parking is tight, so most visitors use the Park & Ride sites on the edge of the city instead.

## Picking your day

The opening days catch people out. At the moment the museum opens:

- Monday: 10am–5pm
- Friday: 10am–5pm
- Saturday: 10am–5pm
- Sunday: 11am–5pm
- Tuesday, Wednesday, Thursday: closed

If your trip falls midweek, plan around it. Hours at small independent museums change with the season and over holidays, so check the museum's official website before you set off.

An hour is enough to see every room without rushing. Add longer if you plan to sit in the tearoom. Arrive well before the last hour of the day, because a quick lap through the stairs and small rooms takes longer than you would expect.

## Stairs, admission and the tearoom

Pay at the front desk when you arrive. The current admission is listed on the museum's official site and at the door. Many British museums ask whether you want to add Gift Aid, a UK tax scheme that lets a charity claim extra on your ticket. Say yes only if you are a UK taxpayer.

A few things that help in a building this old:

- The stairs are narrow, so let people coming down pass first
- Large rucksacks bump into displays in tight rooms, so ask at the desk about leaving yours
- Visitors with limited mobility should contact the museum ahead to ask which floors are accessible

The tearoom belongs to the visit too. Stopping for tea under the old timbers is part of how this building has always worked, first as an inn and now as a museum. The museum is under the radar, so you are unlikely to be fighting for a table. Weekends can still bring families and small groups.

## The rest of Castle Hill

The museum sits in one of the most rewarding small corners of Cambridge. Within a few minutes' walk you will find:

- **Kettle's Yard**, the house and gallery of modern art next door on the same corner
- **Castle Mound**, the grassy remains of the Norman castle, with one of the best free views over the city's spires
- **St Peter's Church**, a tiny medieval church tucked just off Castle Street
- **Magdalene Bridge and the Cam**, at the foot of the hill, where the punts gather

A good half-day goes like this. Start at the Museum of Cambridge, climb Castle Mound for the view, see Kettle's Yard, then walk downhill to the river. You will have seen a side of the city that most day-trippers, queueing at King's College, never reach.
