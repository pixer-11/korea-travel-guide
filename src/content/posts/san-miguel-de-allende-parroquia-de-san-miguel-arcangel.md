## Pink stone over the Jardín

Look up from the main square. A cluster of pinnacles rises over the Jardín Allende, carved from rose-coloured local cantera stone and stacked like a wedding cake drawn by someone who had only ever seen Gothic cathedrals in pictures. That is more or less what happened.

The Parroquia de San Miguel Arcángel is the parish church of San Miguel de Allende. It's also the reason most people know what this town looks like. Laurel trees in the square are clipped into dense green blocks, and the spires sit directly behind them. You can frame the church from almost any bench.

This is one of the most-reviewed sights in central Mexico, and it isn't quiet. Plan for company and you will enjoy it more.

## A mason, a postcard and a facade

The church underneath is far older than the front. The original parish church went up in the late 17th century in a plainer colonial style. In the late 19th century, a local self-taught mason named Zeferino Gutiérrez was given the job of rebuilding the facade and tower.

The usual story is that he worked from postcards and engravings of European cathedrals. Cologne and Chartres are the names people mention most. He had no formal architectural training, and the result is Gothic in outline rather than in structure: pointed arches, needle spires and tracery applied to a colonial body.

That mismatch is the charm. Stand at the far end of the Jardín, then walk up close. The detail gets busier the nearer you get, and the pink stone shifts colour with the light. In 2008 the historic town, this church included, was inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

## Inside the sanctuary

The interior is calmer than the facade promises. The nave is tall and the walls are pale. The altars were reworked in the 19th century in a restrained neoclassical style, so you won't find the gilded baroque you get in some Mexican churches.

Things to look for:

- **The Señor de la Conquista**, a revered Christ figure made with the indigenous technique of corn-stalk paste, kept in a side chapel
- **The height of the nave**, which you only feel once you stand under it
- **The view back out** through the main door to the Jardín, a frame of light and green

Twenty to thirty minutes inside is plenty. Add another half hour for the facade and the square.

## Working around the split schedule

The opening hours trip people up. Doors open at 9am every day except Sunday, which is listed as closed. The midday break runs 2pm to 4pm, and the afternoon session doesn't happen every day.

- **Monday, Tuesday, Wednesday, Friday:** 9am to 2pm, then 4pm to 7pm
- **Thursday and Saturday:** 9am to 2pm only
- **Sunday:** closed

Saturday is the day to be careful. Between 10am and 2pm the church and the square fill up, and you'll be sharing the nave with tour groups and wedding parties. If Saturday is your only day, get there right at 9am.

On a weekday, the 4pm reopening is a good bet. The afternoon light turns the cantera stone warmer, and you can step outside at 7pm just as the square comes alive.

## Stepping inside a working church

This is an active parish, not a museum. People come here to pray, and services can happen during visiting hours. A few habits will keep you on the right side of everyone:

- **Cover your shoulders** and skip very short shorts. A light scarf in your bag solves this.
- **Take off your hat** at the door.
- **Lower your voice** and keep your phone silent.
- **No flash**, and no photos at all if a Mass, baptism or wedding is under way.
- **Stay at the back** during a service rather than walking up the side aisles.

There's no ticket to buy. If you want to leave something, use the donation boxes inside. If you hire one of the guides who wait in the Jardín, agree on the length of the tour first. Weddings often spill onto the steps on Saturdays, so give the bridal party room.

## Late September: the archangel's feast

This church gives its name to the town, and its patron's feast is the biggest week on the local calendar. Around 29 September, the day of San Miguel Arcángel, the Jardín fills with processions, dancers in feathered costume and the *Alborada*. That's a pre-dawn firework display aimed at the spires.

Book a room early if you're coming that week. It's loud and crowded and it goes on late, which is the whole point. The church's normal schedule may change around the fiesta, so check locally before you plan a visit inside.

## Getting to Centro

San Miguel has no airport or rail service of its own. Most international visitors fly into Querétaro (QRO) or Del Bajío near León (BJX) and then take a shuttle or a bus. Long-distance buses also run from Mexico City's Terminal Central del Norte.

From the bus station, a short taxi ride gets you to the Jardín. Once you're in Centro, walk. The streets are steep cobblestone and parking is scarce. The church's address is simply Principal S/N, on the south side of the square, and the spires work as a compass from almost anywhere in town.

## Around the Jardín after the doors close

The church shuts, but the square around it doesn't. Right on the corner of the Jardín is the Casa de Allende, birthplace of independence hero Ignacio Allende and now a history museum. The Templo de San Rafael sits right next to the Parroquia.

In the evening, the facade is floodlit. Mariachi bands gather in the Jardín for hire, and street vendors sell snacks from carts. Many rooftop terraces on the surrounding blocks face the spires, so a drink at sunset gives you the view most people come for. Just be back at the door by 4pm the next day if you want the inside too.
