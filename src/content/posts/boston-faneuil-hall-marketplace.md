## Granite, cobblestones and a domed roof

Three long market buildings run side by side toward the harbour. The middle one, Quincy Market, is fronted by Doric columns at each end and topped by a domed rotunda. On either side, North Market and South Market face it across wide pedestrian lanes paved in brick and cobblestone.

At the western end stands Faneuil Hall itself, the brick meeting house from 1742. A gilded grasshopper weathervane turns on its cupola. Between the two, you are standing in one of the most-visited public spaces in the United States. It is consistently well rated, and nobody here will mistake it for a quiet corner.

## How the three markets fit together

The layout is easy once you see it from above. Quincy Market sits in the centre, built in 1826 to designs by Alexander Parris and named for Mayor Josiah Quincy. Its long central corridor, the Colonnade, is the food hall. Counters line both walls, with the seating under the rotunda.

North Market and South Market hold the shops, bars and sit-down restaurants. Many open onto the promenade with outdoor tables in warmer months. The whole complex was restored and reopened in 1976 as one of America's first "festival marketplaces". That model of reusing old market buildings for shopping and eating was later copied in cities across the country.

Faneuil Hall is a separate building with its own purpose:

- **Ground floor:** shops and a National Park Service visitor centre for Boston National Historical Park
- **Second floor:** the Great Hall, where colonists argued against British taxation and which earned the nickname "Cradle of Liberty"
- **Top floor:** the museum of the Ancient and Honorable Artillery Company

Faneuil Hall's hours are not the same as the marketplace's. Check the National Park Service site for the building's schedule and ranger talk times.

## Chowder under the rotunda

The Colonnade is where most people eat. It works like a food court with more history on the walls. Stalls run the length of the hall, and you order at each counter, collect your food and carry it to the tables under the dome.

New England seafood is the headline. Across the stalls you will usually find:

- **Clam chowder**, often served in a sourdough bread bowl
- **Lobster rolls**, cold with mayonnaise or warm with butter
- **Fried clams and fish and chips**
- **Boston cream pie and cannoli** for dessert

Vendors change over the years, so walk the full length of the hall before choosing. Seats under the rotunda fill fast at lunch. On a mild day, many visitors take their food out to the benches along the promenade instead.

## When to come, and when to stay away

Timing matters more here than almost anywhere else downtown. On weekdays the marketplace is calmer across its whole schedule, from 10am opening to 9pm close. Any weekday visit will feel easier than a weekend one.

Weekends are a different place. Try not to arrive between 12pm and 8pm on Saturday or Sunday. That is when the Colonnade becomes a slow shuffle and the performers draw deep crowds on the cobblestones.

If a weekend is your only option, aim for 7pm to 8pm on Saturday, the quietest weekend window. That window disappears on Sunday: hours are shorter, 11am to 7pm. Monday to Saturday, the doors open at 10am and close at 9pm.

Plan roughly one to two hours for food and a wander. Add another hour if you are going inside Faneuil Hall or joining the Freedom Trail, which passes right by the hall.

## Getting there on the T

Driving is a poor choice here. Downtown garages are pricey and the surrounding streets are congested, so take the MBTA subway. Several stations are a short walk away:

- **Government Center** (Green and Blue lines): cross City Hall Plaza and walk down the steps, about five minutes
- **Haymarket** (Orange and Green lines): a few minutes' walk south past the Holocaust Memorial
- **State Street** (Orange and Blue lines): walk north up Congress Street
- **Aquarium** (Blue Line): come from the waterfront side, across the Rose Kennedy Greenway

The address is simply Boston, MA 02109. Map apps will drop you on the promenade.

## Tipping the buskers and other marketplace manners

Street performers are part of the deal here. Acrobats, jugglers and musicians work set spots on the cobblestones, especially the open space between Faneuil Hall and Quincy Market's west portico. If you stay for a full show, drop cash in the hat at the end. That is how they are paid.

Paying for everything else is simple:

- Cards and phone payments are accepted almost everywhere in the Colonnade and the shops
- At sit-down restaurants in North and South Market, tip 18 to 20 percent, as is standard in Boston
- At food-hall counters, a tip jar or screen prompt is optional

For the food hall, split up. One person holds a table under the rotunda while the others queue at different counters. Clear your own tray afterwards; there is no table service in the Colonnade.

On the promenade, keep moving through the performer circles. Stopping in a walkway to film blocks the only route between the buildings.

## Walking out from the market

The marketplace works best as a starting point. Head a block north to the Blackstone Block, the cluster of narrow old lanes around the Union Oyster House, which has served diners since 1826. On Fridays and Saturdays, the open-air Haymarket produce stalls set up on Blackstone Street nearby.

Cross the Rose Kennedy Greenway and you reach the North End. Its Italian bakeries and Paul Revere House are a ten-minute walk away. Heading the other direction, the red-brick line of the Freedom Trail leads toward the Old State House and on to Boston Common.

Go east along the Greenway instead and the harbour opens up at Long Wharf. From there, ferries and harbour cruises leave toward the Boston Harbor Islands in season.
