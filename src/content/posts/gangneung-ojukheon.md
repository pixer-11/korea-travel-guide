## Why go

Ojukheon isn't just an old house — it's the closest thing Korea has to a shrine for its most famous mother-son pair. Shin Saimdang, celebrated painter and calligrapher, raised her son Yi Yulgok here, and he went on to become one of the Joseon Dynasty's most influential Confucian scholars. Both now appear on Korean banknotes, which tells you how deeply this site sits in the national memory.

Walk through the gate and you're stepping into a 500-year-old residential compound, not a reconstruction dressed up for tourists. The black bamboo growing along the property gives the site its name — "ojuk" means black bamboo — and you'll spot the stalks rustling near the original wooden buildings.

## What you'll see

The compound spreads across several structures, each with its own role in the family's story.

- **Mongryongsil** — the small room where Yi Yulgok was born, marked simply and quietly.
- **Munseongsa Shrine** — houses his memorial tablet, still used for ancestral rites.
- **Ojukheon House** — the oldest wooden building, its dark bamboo grove pressing right up against the eaves.
- **Yulgok Memorial Hall (museum)** — replicas and information panels covering Saimdang's paintings and Yulgok's writings.

Give the museum real time. The interpretive panels explain Confucian family life and Saimdang's artistic legacy in a way the outdoor buildings alone can't convey.

## Getting there

Ojukheon sits at 24 Yulgok-ro 3139beon-gil, a few kilometers from central Gangneung and Gyeongpo Beach. Most visitors arrive by taxi or local bus from Gangneung Station or the intercity bus terminal — the ride takes roughly 15–20 minutes depending on traffic.

If you're already at Gyeongpo Beach or Gyeongpodae Pavilion, Ojukheon makes an easy add-on, since it sits on the same side of the city, just inland.

There's an on-site parking lot if you're driving or have arranged a taxi for the day, which is common for visitors combining this with other Gangneung sights.

## When to go

The site is open daily, with no listed closing day — a rarity for a Korean historic site, so you're not locked out on a Monday like at many palaces.

Because it's a well-known, heavily visited landmark, midday on weekends and holidays brings tour buses and school groups. Arrive right at 9am or after 3pm for a calmer walk through the grounds.

Spring and autumn are the most comfortable seasons — the bamboo and pine trees look sharp against clear skies, and Gangneung's summer humidity hasn't set in yet.

Plan for 60 to 90 minutes if you want to see the house, shrine, and museum without rushing.

## How to visit like a local

Buy your ticket at the entrance booth — there's no need to book ahead, and lines are rarely long outside peak holiday weekends. Payment is straightforward with card or cash at the counter.

Because this is a very popular stop on the Gangneung sightseeing circuit, the best move is timing, not secrecy — go right at opening to beat the tour buses that tend to arrive mid-morning.

A few habits locals and frequent visitors follow:

- Remove shoes if you enter any building interior where it's requested.
- Keep voices low near Munseongsa Shrine — it's still an active memorial site, not just a photo backdrop.
- Don't touch or climb on the black bamboo; it's part of the protected grounds, not a prop.

The most common tourist mistake is treating this as a quick photo stop and skipping the museum. The memorial hall is where Saimdang's artwork and Yulgok's philosophy actually come alive — without it, the house alone can feel a little bare.

If you read Korean or have a translation app, the panel texts add real depth on Confucian family structure and the mother-son relationship that made this site historically significant.

Pair your visit with nearby Gyeongpo Beach or the Chojeong Sculpture Park for a fuller half-day loop around this side of Gangneung.
