## Inside the river's bend

The Avon decides the shape of this place. The river, Ōtākaro in te reo Māori, loops around the gardens, so most paths end at a willow-lined bank with ducks working the shallows. The gardens fill roughly 21 hectares inside Hagley Park, the large green space west of the central city.

They date from 1863. That year an English oak was planted to mark the marriage of Prince Albert Edward and Princess Alexandra, and it still stands. Around it the collections grew over the next century and a half: roses, native bush, herbs, a water garden and a row of glasshouses.

This is one of the most visited places in the city, and the reviews are consistently strong. Don't expect it to yourself on a fine weekend. Do expect enough room that it rarely feels crowded once you get past the Rolleston Avenue gates.

## A first loop that covers the highlights

You can wander without a plan, but one loop takes in the best of it. The map boards at the entrance and the visitor centre show where each collection sits. A sensible first circuit:

- **Peacock Fountain**, the ornate cast-iron fountain near the Rolleston Avenue side, restored after years in storage
- **Central Rose Garden**, laid out in a circle of formal beds around a central point
- **Cuningham House**, the tropical glasshouse (more below)
- **New Zealand Native Section**, for plants you'll later spot in the hills outside the city
- **Water Garden**, with ponds and bog plants close to the river
- **The riverbank path** back toward the museum end

That route takes about two hours at an easy pace. Add the Heritage Rose Garden and the herb garden if roses or kitchen plants are your thing. The visitor centre is a modern glass-and-timber building with a café inside, and it makes a natural place to start or finish.

## Cuningham House: the tropics under glass

In a Canterbury winter, this glasshouse is the warmest room in town. Cuningham House is a heritage conservatory from the 1920s with a raised gallery that runs around the inside. From up there you look down into the tops of palms, bromeliads and tropical foliage.

The humidity is real, so take a layer off before you go in, and glasses will fog for a minute. Close by, the smaller Foweraker House holds alpine plants and ferns in cooler conditions. It's an interesting contrast after the heat.

The conservatories keep shorter hours than the outdoor grounds. If they're the reason you came, check the times on the council's Botanic Gardens page and get there well before the gates close.

## Reading the seasons

The calendar decides what you'll see. Christchurch calls itself the Garden City, and the gardens run on the Southern Hemisphere year:

- **Spring (September–November):** blossom trees, rhododendrons and azaleas, plus daffodil drifts in the surrounding Hagley Park woodland
- **Summer (December–February):** the rose gardens at their peak, long evenings and picnic weather on the lawns
- **Autumn (March–May):** English trees turning gold and red along the river, many of them planted in the gardens' first decades
- **Winter (June–August):** bare branches, frosty mornings and Cuningham House at its most welcome

Opening hours follow the seasons too. This week the gates open at 7am and close at 8:30pm, but closing time moves through the year. Check the official page before planning a late visit.

## Getting in from Rolleston Avenue

The main entrance is on Rolleston Avenue, right beside Canterbury Museum. From Cathedral Square it's an easy walk west of around 10–15 minutes along Worcester Boulevard, which takes you past the Arts Centre.

The city's heritage tram loop stops on Rolleston Avenue near the museum, a scenic if slow way to arrive. Local buses come into the central Bus Interchange on Lichfield Street, a short walk from the gates. If you drive, parking is around the edges of Hagley Park rather than inside the gardens. Spaces near Rolleston Avenue fill first.

## Picnics, punts and garden manners

Locals treat this place as a backyard, and the rules reflect it. Rugs on the lawns are normal and so is a takeaway lunch. Using the bins is expected. Read the signs at the gate for dogs and bikes. The gardens are run as a plant collection, so rules here are stricter than in the open fields of Hagley Park.

Don't feed bread to the ducks: it's bad for them and for the river. Stay on the paths in the native and rose beds, and don't pick anything. Labels next to most plants give the botanical name, and a lot of the native ones show the Māori name too. They're worth reading.

Punting on the Avon leaves from the Antigua Boat Sheds on the southern edge of the gardens, where a guide in Edwardian dress poles you along under the willows. It's popular, so book ahead on a sunny weekend. Pay by card at the café and boat sheds; contactless is standard across New Zealand, and tipping isn't expected.

## Beyond the gates

The gardens make an easy centre for a half-day on foot. Next door, Canterbury Museum and the Gothic Revival stone buildings of the Arts Centre, Te Matatiki Toi Ora, line Rolleston Avenue and Worcester Boulevard. The Christchurch Art Gallery, Te Puna o Waiwhetū, is a few blocks further east.

Behind you, Hagley Park opens out into wide playing fields and tree-lined walking and cycling tracks. Mona Vale and Riccarton Bush, the city's other heritage gardens, are short drives west if one garden isn't enough.
