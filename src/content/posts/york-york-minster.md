Stand on the cobbles of Minster Yard and tip your head back. The west front rises in pale magnesian limestone, with twin towers, carved niches and a huge traceried window over the doors. Even from the far side of the square, it is hard to fit the whole building into a phone screen.

Inside, it takes a moment to get your bearings. The nave runs away from you under a high vault, and the coloured light from the windows falls on bare stone floors. Building went on for about 250 years, from 1220 to 1472. You can see the Gothic style change as you walk from one end to the other.

## The glass is the reason to slow down

Give the windows most of your time. York Minster holds one of the largest collections of medieval stained glass in the world, and three windows stand out:

- **The Great East Window**: made by the glazier John Thornton between 1405 and 1408. It is roughly the size of a tennis court and shows the Creation and the Book of Revelation. It is the largest expanse of medieval stained glass in Britain.
- **The Five Sisters Window**: five tall lancets in the north transept, filled with silvery-grey thirteenth-century grisaille rather than pictures.
- **The Rose Window**: in the south transept, with Tudor roses marking the 1486 marriage of Henry VII and Elizabeth of York. It survived a serious fire in 1984.

Bring binoculars or use your phone's zoom. The upper panels are too high to read with the naked eye.

## Under the floor, up the tower

There is more here than the cathedral floor. Below it, the Undercroft exhibition takes you down to the foundations, where Roman York (Eboracum) lies under the medieval church. Constantine was proclaimed emperor in this city in 306 AD. His bronze statue sits outside the south transept, which makes a good meeting point.

The central tower climb is the other half of the visit. It has 275 steps up a narrow spiral staircase, with no lift and no easy way to turn back. From the roof you look over the red pantiles of the old city to the North York Moors. It is not suitable for anyone uneasy with tight spaces or heights. Check tower slot availability when you buy your ticket.

## Before you leave the building

Two interiors are easy to walk straight past:

- **The Chapter House**: an octagonal room off the north transept with no central column holding up its vault. Its stone canopies are carved with faces, animals and the odd grotesque.
- **The choir screen**: fifteen stone kings of England, from William the Conqueror to Henry VI, standing in a row between the nave and the choir.

Allow around two hours for the main floor and the Undercroft. Add the tower and it is closer to three.

## Timing it around the crowds

This is one of the most-visited buildings in the north of England, so timing matters. The calmest hour is 10am to 11am on weekdays. Arrive at the 9:30am opening and you will be inside before the quiet window starts.

On weekends, try not to turn up between 11am and 4pm. That is the busiest stretch, and the Rose Window and Undercroft entrance get crowded. Sundays are short, from 12:45pm to 3:15pm only, because worship comes first. If Sunday is your only day, go straight to the glass.

## Visiting a working cathedral

York Minster is a church before it is an attraction, and visitors are expected to behave that way:

- **Tickets**: buy online ahead or at the entrance. The current price is posted on the Minster's official website, so check there before you go.
- **Services are free**: anyone can attend a service without a sightseeing ticket. Choral Evensong is the one most visitors choose. Check the official service times, arrive early and stay for the whole service. It is worship, not a concert.
- **Hourly prayer**: staff may ask for a minute of silence for a short prayer. Stop walking, lower your voice and wait.
- **Guides**: volunteer guides in the nave are free to approach. They will point out details you would miss, such as the Dragon's head jutting from the nave roof.

## On the way out

Walk round the north side into Dean's Park, a lawn with the best clear view of the Minster's length. Treasurer's House is just behind it. From there, Bootham Bar gives you steps up onto the city walls.

Head south instead and Stonegate leads to the Shambles in a few minutes. York railway station is about a fifteen-minute walk away, over Lendal Bridge.
