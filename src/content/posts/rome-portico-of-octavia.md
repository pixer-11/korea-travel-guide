## Columns with a church inside them

Two fluted marble columns hold up a cracked pediment. A clumsy brick arch fills the gap where more columns once stood. Behind it, the small church of Sant'Angelo in Pescheria sits inside the ancient porch as if it had always been there.

This is the surviving monumental entrance of a much bigger colonnaded enclosure. The first version, the Porticus Metelli, went up in the 2nd century BC around two temples, dedicated to Jupiter Stator and Juno Regina. Augustus rebuilt it and named it for his sister Octavia. The inscription you can still read across the top records a restoration under Septimius Severus and Caracalla after a fire.

## Reading the ruin

A raised metal walkway runs over the excavated ancient ground level. From it you can look down at the old paving, well below today's street. Give it 20 to 30 minutes, or longer if you go into the church.

What to look for:

- **The Severan inscription** on the architrave, dated to the early 3rd century AD.
- **The medieval brick arch**, a patch put in after the original columns collapsed.
- **The fish-market plaque** on a nearby pillar. For centuries this was Rome's fish market, the *pescheria*, which gave the church its name. The Latin text claims the heads of fish longer than the slab for the city officials.
- **Column fragments** lying loose in the excavated area below the walkway.

## Around the Ghetto

The Portico marks the edge of the Jewish Ghetto, which Pope Paul IV walled off in 1555. From the columns, Via del Portico d'Ottavia runs east. Its restaurants put tables straight onto the stones, and fried *carciofi alla giudia* (Jewish-style artichokes) are the dish the street is known for.

Within five minutes on foot:

- **Theatre of Marcellus**, the curved arcades immediately south.
- **Great Synagogue of Rome** and the Jewish Museum, towards the river on Lungotevere de' Cenci.
- **Fontana delle Tartarughe**, the turtle fountain in Piazza Mattei.
- **Tiber Island**, across the Ponte Fabricio.

There's no metro station nearby. Tram 8 stops at Arenula/Cairoli, a short walk away. Piazza Venezia is about ten minutes on foot along Via del Teatro di Marcello.

## Timing it around Shabbat and the crowds

The monument is open to the street 24 hours a day, with no gate and no ticket booth. That makes early morning the obvious move. It's calmest from 7am to 8am on weekdays and from 7am to 10am on weekends, when the only other people on the walkway are neighbours heading out and delivery vans.

Try not to come between 12pm and 10pm on a weekend. That's when the lunch and dinner crowds fill the street and the walkway gets congested.

This is a living Jewish neighbourhood, so its week shapes what you'll find open:

- **Friday evening to Saturday evening** is Shabbat. Many kosher restaurants and shops close, and the synagogue doesn't take tourist visits.
- **Saturday night**, the street fills again once Shabbat ends.
- **Menus** often mark dishes as kosher or not. Kosher places don't mix meat and dairy, so don't ask for parmesan on a meat dish.
- **Bills** in Roman restaurants often add a cover charge. Tipping is modest and optional; rounding up is normal.

Dress modestly and keep your voice down if you step into Sant'Angelo in Pescheria. It's a working church, not part of the ruin.
