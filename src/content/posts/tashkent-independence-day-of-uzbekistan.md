## What Independence Day actually is

September 1 marks the day Uzbekistan declared independence from the Soviet Union in 1991. It's the country's biggest civic holiday, and Tashkent throws the largest party.

Streets fill with flags in the national colours — blue, white, green, red. Government buildings drape themselves in bunting days in advance, so the city dresses up well before the date itself.

## The main event: National Park

The centrepiece is National Park (also signed as Independence Park), a sprawling green space in central Tashkent built around a reflecting pool and the Ezgulik Arch monument. For the night of September 1, 2026, the programme listed a large stage production — folk music, choreographed dance troupes in traditional dress, and a fireworks finale over the water.

This isn't a ticketed concert in the Western sense. It's a public civic celebration, free to attend, more like a national holiday broadcast than a festival with a gate. Timing and programme details sit with the Tashkent city and Uzbekistan tourism authorities.

- **Venue:** National Park (Independence Park), central Tashkent
- **Date:** September 1 annually
- **Cost:** Free, open-air public event
- **Programme:** Folk performances, craft displays, fireworks

## Beyond the park: craft and food around the city

The show at National Park is the anchor, but celebrations ripple outward. Pop-up stalls selling somsa, plov, and non bread appear near major squares, alongside craftspeople demonstrating suzani embroidery and ceramics.

Amir Timur Square and the area around the Tashkent TV Tower often see their own smaller gatherings, family picnics, and impromptu dance circles. These secondary spots offer a calmer taste of the same holiday spirit.

## Getting there and getting around

Tashkent's metro is the easiest way to reach the centre on a holiday when road closures are common. The Mustaqillik Maydoni (Independence Square) and Bunyodkor stations on the Chilanzar and Yunusabad lines sit within a manageable walk of the park and surrounding avenues.

Taxis and ride-hailing apps run throughout the evening, though traffic near the venue slows and detours are common as crowds build. Journeys from outside the city centre take longer than usual.

## When to go and how long to stay

The formal show typically builds toward evening, with fireworks as the closing moment after dark. Families tend to turn up early to claim space along the park's paths and around the pool, and the walkways near the water fill first.

Two to three hours covers the performances, a walk through the park, and the fireworks. Late August into the first days of September is warm in Tashkent, with hot afternoons and mild evenings.

## How to visit like a local

Uzbek families treat this as a day for strolling, not rushing. Most people bring a blanket or simply stand along the park's paved walkways near the pool.

- **Payment:** Food stalls and vendors mostly deal in cash (Uzbek som); small bills are the norm.
- **Timing:** Late afternoon is calmer than the evening crush.
- **Etiquette:** It's a proud, family-oriented occasion — dress is respectful and multiple generations come out together.
- **Common mistake:** Assuming a ticket is needed. There isn't one for the public park celebration; the crowd control is about space, not entry.
- **Language tip:** The holiday is called "Mustaqillik kuni" in Uzbek — useful when asking locals for directions or timing.

Exact stage schedules and any road closures are set by city authorities each year.
