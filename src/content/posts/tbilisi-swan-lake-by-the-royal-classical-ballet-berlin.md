## One night of Tchaikovsky on Rustaveli

December 3, 2026 is the date. The Royal Classical Ballet of Berlin, a touring classical company, brings *Swan Lake* to the Griboedov Theatre in Tbilisi for one evening. It is one stop on a tour, so there is no long run of shows and no second chance later in the week.

The ballet itself needs little introduction. Tchaikovsky's score premiered in Moscow in 1877. The story follows Prince Siegfried, who falls for Odette, a princess turned into a swan by the sorcerer Rothbart. Then he is tricked by Odile, her dark double. The same ballerina traditionally dances both roles, and that double role is what most of the audience comes to see.

## The Griboedov Theatre and its corner of the city

The venue is the Griboedov Theatre, Tbilisi's long-established Russian-language drama theatre, named after the writer and diplomat Alexander Griboedov. It sits at the lower end of Rustaveli Avenue, close to Liberty Square and the gilded statue of St George on its column.

This is a historic proscenium theatre, not a modern arena. That means a traditional auditorium with stalls and upper tiers, and a stage built for drama. For ballet, the stalls and the front of the first tier usually give the clearest view of the dancers' footwork.

## Getting to Liberty Square

The simplest route is the metro.

- **Metro:** Liberty Square station (Tavisuplebis Moedani) on Line 1. From the exit, the theatre is a few minutes' walk along the start of Rustaveli Avenue.
- **On foot from the Old Town:** walk uphill from the Leselidze (Kote Afkhazi) Street area to Liberty Square in about 10–15 minutes.
- **Taxi:** ride-hailing apps such as Bolt are widely used in Tbilisi. On a show night, the Rustaveli traffic near Liberty Square moves slowly.

December evenings in Tbilisi are cold. Locals heading to the theatre usually come in a proper winter coat and leave it in the cloakroom.

## What happens on stage

*Swan Lake* is traditionally staged in four acts, alternating between palace scenes and the moonlit lakeside. Touring productions often cut it down. A typical evening runs a little over two hours with at least one interval, but this company's exact running order is its own.

Moments worth watching for:

- **Act II's lakeside scene**, where Odette first appears and the corps de ballet moves as a single flock of swans.
- **The Dance of the Little Swans**, four dancers with linked arms moving in tight unison.
- **The Black Swan pas de deux** in Act III, built around Odile's famous run of 32 fouettés.
- **The finale**, which ends tragically or happily depending on the production's version.

## Theatre manners on Rustaveli

Tbilisi's theatres keep the old cloakroom custom. Coats go to the *garderob* attendant before you take your seat, and you get a numbered tag back. At the end of the show the cloakroom queue is the slowest part of the night. Regulars often wait in their seats for the first rush to clear.

People dress smart-casual. Some wear evening dress, but nobody is turned away for jeans and a good sweater. Photography and filming during the performance are generally not allowed in Georgian theatres, and ushers do enforce it.

Applause works as it does at any classical ballet. The audience claps after big solos and the Black Swan fouettés, not just at the curtain. Programmes and announcements here are often in Georgian and Russian. A quick read of the plot beforehand helps if you speak neither.

## Before and after the curtain

You are on the city's main cultural avenue. Walking up Rustaveli you pass the Georgian National Museum, the Parliament building, Kashveti Church, the Rustaveli Theatre and the Tbilisi Opera and Ballet Theatre. That walk takes 15–20 minutes and is lit up in the evening.

For dinner, most theatregoers head downhill into the Old Town. The streets around Leselidze and Kote Afkhazi are lined with restaurants serving khinkali and khachapuri. Card payment is normal in central Tbilisi, so you are unlikely to need cash for a meal after the show.
