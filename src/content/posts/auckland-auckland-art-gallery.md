## Stone Château, Timber Canopy

Two buildings make up this gallery. The older one faces Kitchener Street: a French Renaissance-style block from 1887, with a steep roofline and a clock-tower corner. It was built as the city's library and gallery and looks like it was lifted from the Loire.

Walk past it and the 2011 extension opens up. Its kauri-timber canopies fan out over the entrance like a stand of trees, and glass walls let Albert Park's big trees show through. The redevelopment won World Building of the Year at the 2013 World Architecture Festival. Look up before you look at the art.

## What Hangs on These Walls

The collection is the largest public art collection in New Zealand, at more than 15,000 works. It runs from European old masters to contemporary installations. What you won't find anywhere else is the depth of New Zealand and Māori art.

Rooms worth finding first:

- **Goldie and Lindauer portraits.** These are 19th and early 20th century paintings of Māori rangatira (chiefs) and kuia (elders), with tā moko (facial tattoos) rendered in close detail.
- **Colin McCahon.** His text-filled, almost scriptural landscapes are the backbone of New Zealand modernism.
- **Frances Hodgkins.** The gallery holds a strong body of work by the expatriate painter.
- **Contemporary Māori and Pacific artists**, shown in rotating displays on the upper levels.

Major touring exhibitions change through the year. The official website lists what is on and whether a show needs a separate ticket. Entry details are posted there too.

## Getting to Kitchener Street

The gallery is at 1 Kitchener Street, on the corner with Wellesley Street East. It's an uphill block east of Queen Street. From Britomart Transport Centre, where the trains and most harbour ferries come in, the walk is about 10–15 minutes up through the CBD.

Buses along Queen Street and Wellesley Street stop within a few minutes' walk. Parking in the central city is expensive and scarce. Public transport or walking makes more sense, especially if you're combining the gallery with the waterfront.

## Timing Your Visit

At the moment the gallery opens 10am–5pm every day. Hours can change for holidays and special events, so check the official site before you set out.

The numbers on crowds are clear. On weekdays it's calmest from 10am to 11am and again from 4pm to 5pm. At weekends, the only quiet window is 10am–11am. Avoid arriving between 12pm and 4pm on a Saturday or Sunday. That's when the popular exhibitions fill up and the timber-roofed atrium gets loud.

Plan on two to three hours for the collection plus one exhibition. A focused hour can cover the Māori portraits and the architecture. If you have a free weekday afternoon, arriving at 4pm gives you a quiet final hour, though not enough time for a big show.

## Moving Through Toi o Tāmaki

The Māori name, Toi o Tāmaki, appears on signage alongside the English one. Staff use it, and so can you. Tāmaki is the Māori name for the Auckland isthmus.

A few habits make the visit smoother:

- **Treat the portraits as ancestors, not just paintings.** Many descendants regard the Goldie and Lindauer works as embodying their tūpuna (ancestors). Speak quietly in those rooms, and don't pose for selfies in front of them.
- **Check photography signs room by room.** The permanent collection is usually more relaxed than loan exhibitions, and flash is generally not allowed.
- **Leave large bags behind.** Bulky backpacks are normally checked at the front desk rather than carried into the galleries.
- **Ask about free guided tours.** Volunteer guides run collection tours. The front desk and the website have the current schedule.
- **Pay by card.** Like most of Auckland, the gallery shop and ticket desk run almost entirely on contactless payment.

If you're starting from the waterfront, go in the morning, around the 10am opening. You'll get the quiet slot and still have the afternoon for the harbour.

## After the Gallery

You come out straight into Albert Park, a Victorian park with fountains, formal flower beds and old trees on a rise above the CBD. It's the easy place to sit and talk over what you've just seen. The University of Auckland campus continues uphill beyond it.

Next door on Lorne Street is Auckland Central City Library. Queen Street's shops are a block west. The Sky Tower is about a 10-minute walk southwest if you want to see the city from above afterwards.
