## Seven years away, two nights back

Jason Mraz had not played Japan in seven years. This leg of his Asia tour was set for two dates, November 10-11, 2026. One night was at Tokyo Garden Theater and the other at Festival Hall in Osaka.

The announcement gave the pair of dates together without saying which city got which night. It was a short run, so seeing both shows meant moving between cities with little time to spare.

## Inside Tokyo Garden Theater

The venue is a modern indoor hall built for concerts. It sits inside Ariake Garden, a shopping and hotel complex on reclaimed land on Tokyo Bay, and opened in 2020. Capacity is around 8,000.

The seating is what stands out. Tiers climb steeply on three sides of the stage, so the upper levels sit closer to the performer than you would expect in a hall this size. That suits an artist whose shows are built on an acoustic guitar and songs like "I'm Yours", "Lucky" and "I Won't Give Up". A seat high up still feels close.

## Reaching Ariake on a show night

Ariake is on the bay side of Koto ward, past Odaiba. It is easy to reach but has fewer train lines than central Tokyo. There are two main routes:

- **Yurikamome line:** Ariake-tennis-no-mori Station is the closest stop. The driverless train runs from Shimbashi, crosses the Rainbow Bridge and gives a view of the bay on the way.
- **Rinkai Line:** Kokusai-Tenjijo Station is a slightly longer walk. Some Rinkai trains run through from the JR Saikyo Line, which helps from Shinjuku or Shibuya.
- **From Toyosu:** the Yurikamome also runs to Toyosu, so Ariake can be reached from the Yurakucho Line that way.

After an encore, thousands of people leave at once for the same few platforms, and queues at the Yurikamome are common. Eating in Ariake Garden first gives the crowd time to clear.

## Tickets the Japanese way

Concert ticketing in Japan works differently from most countries, and it trips up a lot of visitors. Many big shows sell through lotteries (抽選, chūsen) on platforms such as e+, Ticket Pia and Lawson Ticket. Only some seats go on a straight first-come basis.

How it usually works:

- **Official channels.** Legitimate ticket links come from the artist's or promoter's tour page.
- **Names and IDs.** Japanese tickets are often issued in the buyer's name, and staff may check ID at the door. A passport serves as ID.
- **Digital tickets** usually live in a ticketing app on the buyer's phone, which needs installing and logging into in advance.
- **Unofficial resale.** Resold tickets can be refused at the door. Where a show offers an official resale system, that is the safe way to get a spare.

No price list came with this tour's announcement.

## Concert etiquette in Ariake

Japanese audiences are attentive, and visitors find that the most surprising part. People clap and sing along on the big choruses, then go quiet for the slower songs. Talking during a ballad gets noticed.

Filming and photography are often banned at Japanese concerts. The rules are printed with the ticket and posted at the venue, and staff enforce them politely but firmly.

Merchandise sales usually open well before doors, and fans queue in lines that staff keep organised. Fans after a tour shirt tend to head to the merch line first. Coin lockers fill up early on show days, so travelling light helps.

## Before and after the show

Ariake has plenty to fill an afternoon. Ariake Garden has restaurants and shops under the same roof as the theater, which makes it the easiest place for dinner. Tokyo Big Sight, the exhibition centre with the upside-down pyramids, is a short walk away. Odaiba's waterfront is a few Yurikamome stops back towards the city.

For the Osaka date, Festival Hall stands in Nakanoshima, the river island in central Osaka. Its nearest stations are Higobashi on the Yotsubashi Line and Watanabebashi on the Keihan Nakanoshima Line. The hall is known for its acoustics and its red-carpeted grand staircase.
