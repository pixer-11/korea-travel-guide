## A parade that happens after dark

Most Lunar New Year parades run in daylight. San Francisco holds its parade at night. On February 20, 2027, an illuminated procession for the Year of the Ram moves through Downtown, Union Square and Chinatown. It is billed as the biggest Lunar New Year celebration outside Asia.

The night setting is what makes it. Floats are lit from inside, lion dancers' heads catch the streetlights, and firecrackers going off along the route leave smoke hanging between the office towers. The Chinese Chamber of Commerce has run the parade for decades. Its roots in the city go back to the Gold Rush era.

## What comes down the street

The parade is long. Most spectators stay for well over an hour, and the order follows a familiar pattern:

- **Lion dance troupes** from local kung fu schools, with drums and cymbals marching alongside
- **Lit floats** from community groups, businesses and civic organisations
- **Marching bands** from schools around the Bay Area
- **The Miss Chinatown USA float**, carrying the pageant winner and her court
- **Gum Lung, the Golden Dragon**, which closes the parade: more than 200 feet long and carried by a large team of dancers, surrounded by a wall of firecrackers

People who leave before the dragon miss the main event. The crowd is at its thickest along the final stretch.

## Getting to the route

The route traditionally starts near Market Street and heads up through the Union Square area, then turns onto Kearny Street toward Chinatown. Street closures make driving downtown slow on parade night, so most attendees take transit.

- **BART and Muni Metro:** Montgomery Street and Powell Street stations both put you a few blocks from the Union Square stretch.
- **Muni T line:** Union Square/Market Street and Chinatown–Rose Pak stations serve the Stockton Street corridor, one block from Kearny.
- **Cable cars:** the Powell lines pass close to Union Square but fill up quickly on parade night.

The organiser publishes the official route map, along with grandstand ticket links, on its parade website.

## Holding a spot on Kearny Street

Curbside viewing is free and unreserved. Families usually claim their spots on the Kearny Street and Post Street sidewalks well before the first drums. They bring folding stools and a layer for the February chill. The grandstand bleachers suit anyone who would rather have a guaranteed seat with a clear sightline.

A few habits from parade night are useful to know:

- Children sit at the front of the curb and adults stand behind them. Pushing through to the front is frowned on.
- Firecrackers are loud and set off close to the crowd. Families with small children or dogs usually stand further back from the dragon.
- People greet each other with "Gung hay fat choy," the Cantonese New Year greeting you'll hear all over Chinatown.
- After the parade, the crowd spills into Chinatown's restaurants along Grant Avenue and Stockton Street. Dim sum houses and noodle shops near Portsmouth Square stay packed.

During the day, Grant Avenue is the place to see the neighbourhood decorated for the New Year. Red lanterns hang over the street from the Dragon Gate at Bush Street northward, and bakeries stack their counters with New Year sweets.
