## Why go

Nowhere else will you stand this close to the actual bronzes, mosaics and frescoes pulled from Pompeii and Herculaneum. The Museo Archeologico Nazionale di Napoli isn't a museum of replicas or scattered fragments — it's where the Bourbon kings shipped the best material from the Vesuvian excavations, plus the entire Farnese collection of Roman sculpture inherited through the family line.

Walk in and the scale hits you first: this is a former cavalry barracks and university building, all thick masonry and long enfilade galleries, converted into a palace of antiquities in the late 1700s. The ceilings soar, the light in the Great Hall is filtered and cool, and every room seems to hold something you've seen in a textbook.

## What to see

Start on the ground floor with the Farnese Marbles — the Farnese Hercules, muscles exaggerated to the point of caricature, and the Farnese Bull, carved from a single colossal block and among the largest surviving sculptures from antiquity. From there, head up to the mezzanine for the mosaics: the Alexander Mosaic from the House of the Faun in Pompeii, an extraordinary battle scene rendered in tiny tesserae, is worth the whole visit on its own.

The top floor holds the frescoes and the notorious Gabinetto Segreto (Secret Cabinet), a small side room of erotic Roman art from Pompeii that was locked away from the public for two centuries — it's now open, but check at the entrance, as access sometimes requires a separate ticket or guided slot. Don't rush past the Egyptian collection either; it's one of Italy's most significant, with sarcophagi, mummies and papyri that get a fraction of the foot traffic of the main galleries.

## Getting there

The museum sits at Piazza Museo 19, at the top edge of the centro storico, an easy walk from Via Toledo. The closest metro stops are Museo and Cavour, both on Line 1, each about a five-minute walk from the entrance — Museo station's platform itself is decorated with archaeological-themed art, a nice preview. If you're coming from the main train station, Napoli Centrale, it's a short ride or a 20-minute walk up Via Foria.

The museum is also a natural pairing with a Pompeii or Herculaneum day trip: many visitors see the ruins in the morning and the museum's finds in the afternoon, or vice versa, since seeing both in either order deepens the other.

## When to go

Check the museum's current opening hours and any weekly closing day before you go, since these can change with the season. Weekends are consistently busiest between 10am and 6pm, so if you're visiting on a Saturday or Sunday, arriving right when the doors open is the single best way to get the Farnese Hall and the mosaic corridor to yourself before tour groups arrive.

Weekday mornings tend to be calmer across the board. Plan for two hours if you're moving quickly through highlights, three or more if you want to linger over the mosaics and frescoes.

## How to visit like a local

This is one of Naples' most-visited sites, not a quiet find, so the smart move is timing rather than hoping for an empty room. Buy your ticket online in advance where possible to skip the entrance queue, especially around midday on weekends. Once inside, most visitors funnel straight to the Farnese sculptures and the Alexander Mosaic; if you peel off toward the Egyptian collection or the numismatic (coin) rooms first, you'll often have entire galleries to yourself before doubling back to the highlights later in your visit.

Italians and repeat visitors tend to treat this as a slow, room-by-room walk rather than a checklist — the collection rewards lingering over specific objects rather than speed-walking to the famous names. Payment is standard card or cash at the ticket desk; there's no tipping culture inside Italian state museums. If you want context on the frescoes and mosaics, renting the audio guide or booking one of the guided tours at the entrance is genuinely useful here, since object labels can be sparse in places.

Save the Gabinetto Segreto for when you have patience left — it's small and can queue up on its own even when the main galleries have thinned out.
