Most big-city marathons end on an open road. Frankfurt ends indoors. The final metres run off the street and into the Festhalle, a domed exhibition hall from 1909 on the Messe grounds. Runners cross the line under lights, with a crowd packed around them on every side.

That finish is the reason many international runners pick this race. Frankfurt is Germany's longest-established city marathon and its second-largest by finishers. The 2026 edition is set for **October 25, 2026**, and it draws over 20,000 participants from around the world.

## A race that finishes under a roof

The Festhalle is the part people remember. It is a big, round hall with a glass-and-steel dome, normally used for concerts and trade fairs. On race day the finish straight runs down the middle, and the noise bounces off the curved walls.

Outside, the Messeturm stands over the start area. It is the tall, pencil-shaped tower with the pointed top, one of the most recognisable buildings on the Frankfurt skyline. The starting lines form on the broad avenues next to the fairground. The towers of the banking district are close by.

The course is flat. Frankfurt has a long-standing reputation as one of the faster marathons in the world, and elite fields have set fast times here more than once. If you are chasing a personal best in autumn, that is the attraction.

## The date, the Messe and what to check

The facts that hold steady:

- **Date:** Sunday, October 25, 2026
- **City:** Frankfurt am Main, Hesse
- **Start and finish zone:** the Messe Frankfurt grounds, by the Messeturm and the Festhalle
- **Scale:** over 20,000 participants

Start times, wave allocations, the exact route and bag-drop arrangements can change from year to year. Confirm timing and tickets on the official site. Do not rely on a route map from a previous edition.

In past years, number pick-up and the race expo have been held at the Messe on the days before the race. Check the official schedule for the pick-up window. Plan to arrive in Frankfurt by Saturday at the latest.

## The extra hour: race day meets the clock change

This date has a quirk worth knowing. October 25, 2026 is the last Sunday of October. That is the night Central Europe switches from summer time back to standard time. At 3am, clocks go back to 2am.

For runners, this is a gift: an extra hour in bed before a morning start. It is also a trap. Your phone updates itself. A hotel-room alarm clock or an older watch may not.

- Set your alarm on your phone, not the bedside clock.
- Check the race start time against the new local time, not summer time.
- If you flew in from another continent, your body clock is already confused. Write the start time down in plain numbers the night before.

## Entries, not tickets

Spectators do not need a ticket. The course runs along public streets, and you can stand anywhere outside the barriers for free.

For runners, the "ticket" is a race entry. You buy it through the official registration platform. Entry fees, categories and any relay or shorter-distance options are listed there, and the price is printed on the registration page. Big autumn marathons in Europe can fill up, so register early if you have your heart set on this one.

Inside the Festhalle, seating around the finish has historically been limited. Check the official site for how access works for friends and family.

## Picking your spot on the kerb

The start area by the Messeturm is the easiest place to watch. It is close to transport and gives you the atmosphere of thousands of runners setting off. The Festhalle finish is the emotional high point, but it gets crowded and access can be controlled.

Along the course, the city-centre stretches and the river crossings draw the biggest crowds. Pick two or three points on the official route map that you can link by tram or U-Bahn. That way you see your runner more than once.

What to bring:

- Warm layers. Late October in Frankfurt is usually cool, and standing still for hours feels colder than it is.
- A sign with your runner's name, large enough to read at speed.
- The live-tracking app, if the race offers one, so you know when to look up.

## Getting around a city with closed roads

On race day, many roads are closed and driving across town is slow. Use the trains. Frankfurt's public transport network is run under the RMV system, and it works well for spectators.

- **Messe S-Bahn station** sits right by the fairground on the S-Bahn trunk line.
- **Festhalle/Messe U-Bahn station** on line U4 drops you close to the hall.
- **Frankfurt Hauptbahnhof**, the main station, is within walking distance of the Messe.
- From **Frankfurt Airport**, S-Bahn lines S8 and S9 run to the Hauptbahnhof in about a quarter of an hour.

Book a hotel near the Hauptbahnhof or the Messe and you can walk to the start. That saves you a crowded early train on race morning.

## Sunday in Frankfurt: shops shut, taverns open

Germans take the Sunday rest day seriously. Most supermarkets and shops close on Sundays, and race day is a Sunday. Buy your breakfast, gels and post-race snacks on Saturday. Shops inside the Hauptbahnhof are the usual exception when you forget something.

Cash still matters here more than in many European cities. Cards are widely accepted, but small bakeries, kiosks and older taverns may prefer cash. Carry some euros.

After the race, do what Frankfurters do and cross the river to **Alt-Sachsenhausen**. This is the old apple-wine quarter on the south bank of the Main. The local drink is *Apfelwein*, called *Ebbelwoi* in dialect. It is a dry, sour cider served in a grey stoneware jug called a *Bembel*, which you pour into ridged glasses known as *Gerippte*.

At the traditional taverns you often sit at long shared wooden tables, and nobody minds strangers squeezing in. Order the regional dishes:

- **Grüne Soße**, a cold herb sauce served with boiled eggs and potatoes
- **Handkäs mit Musik**, a sour-milk cheese marinated with onions
- **Frankfurter Rippchen**, cured pork chop with sauerkraut

Tipping is done by rounding up when you pay, not by leaving coins on the table. Tell the server the total you want to pay as you hand over the money. You can usually walk to Sachsenhausen from the city centre over the Eiserner Steg footbridge.
