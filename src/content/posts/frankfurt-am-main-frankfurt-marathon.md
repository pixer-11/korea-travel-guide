Most big-city marathons end on an open road. Frankfurt ends indoors. The final metres run off the street and into the Festhalle, a domed exhibition hall from 1909 on the Messe grounds. Runners cross the line under lights, with spectators packed around them on every side.

That finish is the reason many international runners pick this race. Frankfurt is Germany's longest-established city marathon and its second-largest by finishers. The 2026 edition was set for **October 25, 2026**. The race draws over 20,000 participants from around the world.

## A race that finishes under a roof

The Festhalle is the part people remember. It is a big, round hall with a glass-and-steel dome, normally used for concerts and trade fairs. On race day the finish straight runs down the middle, and the noise bounces off the curved walls.

Outside, the Messeturm stands over the start area. It is the tall, pencil-shaped tower with the pointed top, one of the most recognisable buildings on the Frankfurt skyline. The starting lines form on the broad avenues next to the fairground. The towers of the banking district are close by.

The course is flat. Frankfurt has a long-standing reputation as one of the faster marathons in the world, and elite fields have set fast times here more than once. For runners chasing a personal best in autumn, that is the attraction.

## The extra hour: race day meets the clock change

The 2026 date had a quirk. October 25, 2026 is the last Sunday of October. That is the night Central Europe switches from summer time back to standard time. At 3am, clocks go back to 2am.

For runners, that meant a gift: an extra hour in bed before a morning start. It was also a trap. Phones update themselves. A hotel-room alarm clock or an older watch may not.

- Phone alarms switch to the new time automatically; bedside clocks often do not.
- Race start times are given in local time, which after the change is standard time, not summer time.
- Runners who fly in from another continent deal with jet lag on top of the clock change, and a start time written down in plain numbers removes any doubt.

## Picking your spot on the kerb

The start area by the Messeturm is the easiest place to watch. It is close to transport and gives you the atmosphere of thousands of runners setting off. The Festhalle finish is the emotional high point, but it gets crowded and access can be controlled.

Along the course, the city-centre stretches and the river crossings draw the biggest crowds. Spectators often pick two or three points on the route that link up by tram or U-Bahn, so they see their runner more than once.

What spectators usually bring:

- Warm layers. Late October in Frankfurt is usually cool, and standing still for hours feels colder than it is.
- A sign with the runner's name, large enough to read at speed.
- The live-tracking app, when the race offers one, to know when to look up.

## Getting around a city with closed roads

On race day, many roads are closed and driving across town is slow. The trains are the easier option. Frankfurt's public transport network is run under the RMV system, and it works well for spectators.

- **Messe S-Bahn station** sits right by the fairground on the S-Bahn trunk line.
- **Festhalle/Messe U-Bahn station** on line U4 drops you close to the hall.
- **Frankfurt Hauptbahnhof**, the main station, is within walking distance of the Messe.
- From **Frankfurt Airport**, S-Bahn lines S8 and S9 run to the Hauptbahnhof in about a quarter of an hour.

Hotels near the Hauptbahnhof or the Messe are within walking distance of the start, which avoids a crowded early train on race morning.

## Sunday in Frankfurt: shops shut, taverns open

Germans take the Sunday rest day seriously. Most supermarkets and shops close on Sundays, and the 2026 race was set for a Sunday. Runners usually buy breakfast, gels and post-race snacks on Saturday. Shops inside the Hauptbahnhof are the usual exception when something is forgotten.

Cash still matters here more than in many European cities. Cards are widely accepted, but small bakeries, kiosks and older taverns may prefer cash, so a few euros in your pocket help.

After a race, Frankfurters cross the river to **Alt-Sachsenhausen**. This is the old apple-wine quarter on the south bank of the Main. The local drink is *Apfelwein*, called *Ebbelwoi* in dialect. It is a dry, sour cider served in a grey stoneware jug called a *Bembel*, which you pour into ridged glasses known as *Gerippte*.

At the traditional taverns you often sit at long shared wooden tables, and nobody minds strangers squeezing in. The regional dishes to look for:

- **Grüne Soße**, a cold herb sauce served with boiled eggs and potatoes
- **Handkäs mit Musik**, a sour-milk cheese marinated with onions
- **Frankfurter Rippchen**, cured pork chop with sauerkraut

Tipping is done by rounding up when you pay, not by leaving coins on the table. You tell the server the total you want to pay as you hand over the money. Sachsenhausen is usually a walk from the city centre over the Eiserner Steg footbridge.
