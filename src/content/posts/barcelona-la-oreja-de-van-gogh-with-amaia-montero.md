## The voice that came back

The shows were billed as Amaia Montero's return to the band. She sang with the San Sebastián group from its start in 1996 until 2007. In that time they made the albums that defined Spanish pop for a generation: *Dile al sol*, *Lo que te conté mientras te hacías la dormida* and *Guapa*.

The Barcelona dates were announced as reunion shows built around the band's most popular songs. These are the songs a generation learned as teenagers: "La playa", "Cuídate" and "Rosas". It is the material of a big Spanish pop singalong, the kind where the singer can step back from the mic and the crowd carries the chorus.

## Four nights in a 1929 stadium

The shows were set for November 6, 7, 26 and 27, 2026. That made two separate weekends, Friday and Saturday each time. The venue for all four was the Estadi Olímpic Lluís Companys, the stadium on the slope of Montjuïc above Plaça d'Espanya.

The stone façade dates from the 1929 International Exposition. The stadium was rebuilt inside its original walls for the 1992 Olympics, which opened there. It is named after Lluís Companys, the Catalan president executed in 1940.

The stadium has no roof. Montjuïc evenings in November are cool and can be damp, and a light layer is the usual answer once a crowd warms up.

## Getting up Montjuïc

The climb is the part people forget. These are the main routes:

- **Metro Espanya (L1, L3):** the classic approach. The walk goes up Avinguda de la Reina Maria Cristina between the Venetian towers, past the Magic Fountain and the National Art Museum of Catalonia (MNAC). Outdoor escalators run up the steepest stretches. The walk takes 20 to 25 minutes.
- **Metro Paral·lel (L2, L3):** the Funicular de Montjuïc climbs through the tunnel to Parc de Montjuïc. From there it is a shorter, flatter walk to the stadium.
- **Bus:** several TMB lines climb the hill. On concert nights roads near the Anella Olímpica are often restricted, and event-night service can differ from the usual timetable.

Driving is a poor fit here. The surrounding parking fills fast on event nights, and leaving afterwards is slow.

## Concert night, Barcelona style

Barcelona runs late, and so do concert crowds. Many people eat dinner first and arrive near showtime. That makes the last stretch of the walk from Espanya the most crowded part of a concert night, while earlier arrivals have an easier entry and more time to find their section.

At shows like this the songbook does the talking. People sing every word in Spanish, and lighters have long since become phone torches. "Puedes contar conmigo" and "20 de enero" are among the choruses fans know best, the ones that get sung back at full volume.

When the lights come up after a stadium show, tens of thousands of people head down the hill at once. Espanya station gets packed. Two alternatives are common:

- Walking down through Poble-sec to Paral·lel, which is usually easier than the main avenue.
- Staying up the hill for a while to let the first wave clear.

Payment around the stadium and in nearby bars is mostly by card or phone. Tipping is not expected, though rounding up at a bar is common.

## Around the stadium and down the hill

The stadium is part of the Anella Olímpica, the 1992 Olympic ring. Next to it stand the Palau Sant Jordi arena and Santiago Calatrava's white Montjuïc Communications Tower. In daylight, the terraces in front of MNAC look out over the whole city toward Tibidabo.

For food, Poble-sec sits at the foot of the hill. Carrer de Blai is lined with pintxos bars where people take skewered snacks from the counter and pay by counting the sticks. It works for a quick pre-show bite or a late post-show plate.

The two-weekend schedule suited a longer stay in the city. A Friday show left Saturday free for Montjuïc's gardens and the Joan Miró Foundation, both a short walk from the stadium.
