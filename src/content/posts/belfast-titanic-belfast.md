## A silver hull on Queen's Island

Look at the building before you go inside. Its four angular points each take the shape of a ship's prow, and they rise to 38 metres, the height of Titanic's hull. The outside is clad in thousands of silver aluminium shards. They pick up whatever colour the Belfast sky happens to be.

The museum opened in 2012, a century after the sinking. It sits at the top of the slipways where Harland & Wolff built Titanic and her sister ship Olympic. The outline of those slipways is marked across the ground outside, so you can walk the length of the ship before you've bought a ticket.

## Nine galleries, one direction

The route runs one way and follows the story in order. You go at your own pace, with no guide and no set timings inside:

- **Boomtown Belfast:** the city of linen mills, ropeworks and shipyards around 1900.
- **The Shipyard:** a lift carries you up a steel gantry. A small car ride then drops you through a recreated yard of rivets and hammering.
- **The Launch:** windows look straight down onto the slipway itself.
- **The Fit-Out:** reconstructed first, second and third-class cabins.
- **The Maiden Voyage, The Sinking, The Aftermath:** the crossing, the distress messages and the inquiries that followed.
- **Myths & Legends:** how film and popular memory retold the story.
- **Titanic Beneath:** the wreck on the seabed, seen partly through a glass floor.

Most people need two to three hours. The mood changes at The Sinking, where survivor testimony and the Morse distress calls take over from the engineering. If you're short on time, don't rush this part.

## Reaching Titanic Quarter

The museum is east of the River Lagan, a short hop from the city centre. You have four options:

- **Glider G2:** the rapid-transit bus from the city centre stops close to the building.
- **Train:** Titanic Quarter station, on the Bangor line, is a walk of roughly 10 to 15 minutes away.
- **On foot:** about 25 minutes from City Hall, following the river past the Lagan Weir and the tiled Big Fish sculpture.
- **Car:** there is parking beside the building. Check the official site for current arrangements.

The riverside walk is the best of these. You see the yellow Harland & Wolff cranes coming closer across the water for most of the way.

## Booking your slot and pacing the galleries

Entry is sold in timed slots. Booking ahead on the official Titanic Belfast website is the reliable way to get the time you want. That matters most in summer, when cruise ships dock at Belfast, and during school holidays. The current ticket options and prices are listed on the same site.

Doors open at 8:30am every day of the week and close at 6pm. The first slot of the day usually gets you ahead of the coach groups, which tend to arrive later in the morning. Check the last entry time too, because it's earlier than closing.

A few practical habits help:

- The Shipyard ride is where queues usually build, because each car seats only a few people. Walking briskly through Boomtown gets you there before the people who entered with you.
- Card and contactless payments are standard in the café and shop. Nobody expects a tip at a counter.
- The early galleries are packed with text panels. Skim them and save your attention for The Launch and The Fit-Out.

## The dockside beyond the museum

The museum is only one part of the old shipyard. Leave an extra hour or two for the area around it:

- **SS Nomadic:** the last surviving White Star Line vessel. This small tender ferried passengers out to Titanic at Cherbourg, and it now sits in Hamilton Dock next door. Check whether your ticket includes it.
- **Titanic Hotel Belfast:** the former Harland & Wolff Drawing Offices, where the ship was designed. The public bar and lounge let you see inside.
- **Thompson Dock and Pump-House:** the dry dock where Titanic last sat on land before she sailed.
- **Samson and Goliath:** the two yellow gantry cranes that dominate the Belfast skyline.

On your way back into town, the Titanic Memorial in the grounds of City Hall lists the names of those who died. It ends the day on a quieter note.

## Who gets the most from it

The museum suits people who like to know how things were made. The Shipyard and The Launch galleries cover the riveting, the steel and the thousands of Belfast workers, and they make the building of the ship as gripping as its loss. Visitors who only know the film usually find the first half the bigger surprise.

Families do well here too. The car ride and the cabin reconstructions hold children's attention through a visit that's heavy on text. The site is one of the most-reviewed attractions in Northern Ireland, so plan for company whenever you go.
