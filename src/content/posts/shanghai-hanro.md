## Why Hanro Stands Out

Donghu Lu isn't short on polished restaurants, but Hanro is the one Shanghai's food crowd keeps whispering about. It's an upscale Korean BBQ spot from chef Tom Ryu, already a known name in the city's Korean dining scene. What sets it apart isn't just the grill — it's the meat program behind it.

Ryu's team dry-ages and wet-ages imported beef and pork in-house, a technique you rarely see applied to Korean BBQ in China. The result is deeper, funkier, more concentrated flavor than the usual quick-marinated cuts. This isn't a table full of banchan and a hot plate — it's a butchery-driven approach to a format most people think they already know.

## Where It Sits in the City

Donghu Lu is one of former French Concession Shanghai's quieter, tree-lined streets, tucked between the bar-heavy stretches of Julu Lu and the boutique clusters near Middle Huaihai Road. It's the kind of block where a serious restaurant can open without a flashing sign and still fill up on word of mouth.

Expect a walk of a few minutes from the nearest metro regardless of which line you take in — this isn't a station-adjacent spot, it's a destination you plan for. Taxis and ride-hailing apps are the simplest way in, especially at night when the street's low-key restaurants and bars start filling.

## What Makes the Menu Different

The headline draw is the aging program: cuts held to develop texture and flavor you won't find at a standard Korean BBQ counter. Alongside the beef and pork, Hanro has built out a drinks list that's arguably just as distinctive.

The restaurant imports niche Korean soju, cheongju, and makgeolli — small-batch styles that aren't distributed elsewhere in mainland China. For anyone who's only had the mass-market green-bottle soju, this is a genuine education in range: cloudy, lightly sparkling makgeolli, refined cheongju rice wines, soju varieties built for sipping rather than shooting.

## When to Go

Hanro is a dinner destination first — Korean BBQ built around aged meat and paired drinks is not a lunch-counter experience. Given the restaurant's recent buzz and its trending status among Shanghai diners, weekend evenings are likely to be the tightest squeeze.

Because this is a newer, in-demand opening, availability can shift fast and hours weren't independently confirmed for this guide. Call ahead or check Hanro's listing on a reservation platform before you go, and don't assume walk-in seating on a Friday or Saturday night.

## How to Visit Like a Local

Book ahead. In Shanghai's trendier dining scene, a restaurant generating this much word-of-mouth interest fills its evening slots days in advance, especially on weekends — a phone reservation or booking through a Chinese app like Dianping is the standard move, and staff can usually help in English if you call directly.

Come hungry and pace yourself with the drinks list. The soju, cheongju, and makgeolli selection is a big part of what makes Hanro worth the trip, so consider letting staff recommend a pairing rather than defaulting to whatever you already know.

Split the bill the way locals do at Korean BBQ tables — order a spread of cuts to share rather than individual plates, since aged meat is meant to be tasted in comparison, cut against cut.

Tipping isn't customary at Chinese restaurants, including upscale ones, so don't feel obligated to add extra beyond the bill. Payment is almost always by mobile — Alipay or WeChat Pay — so have one set up, or ask if cash or card is accepted before you sit down.

Lastly, don't rush the reservation. This is a restaurant built for a long, unhurried evening of grilling, drinking, and conversation, not a quick refuel — treat it that way and you'll get far more out of the visit.
