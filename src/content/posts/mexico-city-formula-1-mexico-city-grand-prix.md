## Three days, high above sea level

Thin air shapes everything here. Mexico City sits at roughly 2,200 metres, the highest point on the Formula 1 calendar. The cars have less grip and less engine cooling than anywhere else they race, and they reach some of their highest top speeds of the season.

The 2026 Gran Premio de la Ciudad de México runs from Friday, October 30 to Sunday, November 1. The race itself is on Sunday. A typical Formula 1 weekend uses Friday for practice and Saturday for qualifying. Friday crowds are smaller, which gives you room to learn the grounds before the weekend fills up.

## A circuit inside a sports city

The track is named after Ricardo and Pedro Rodríguez. The two brothers were Mexico's first great racing drivers, and both died in racing accidents. The circuit lies inside the Ciudad Deportiva Magdalena Mixiuhca in the Iztacalco borough. The same complex holds baseball diamonds, a velodrome and the concert stadium the cars drive through.

The lap is about 4.3 kilometres and the race runs 71 laps. From the grid, the run down to Turn 1 is over a kilometre long. That makes the first corner one of the most crowded braking zones of the year on the opening lap. After the twisty middle section, the cars slow right down for the stadium.

## The stadium lap

This is the part you won't find at other circuits. On the final sector the cars leave the old Peraltada banking and swing into a baseball and concert stadium, long known as Foro Sol. They thread slowly between tall stands packed on three sides. The podium stands on the stadium floor, so the trophy ceremony happens in front of tens of thousands of seats.

The noise is the point. Stadium seats are where Mexican fans gather in the largest numbers, and the chants carry on long after the chequered flag. In 2026 there is a home driver to cheer again: Sergio "Checo" Pérez is back on the grid with the new Cadillac team.

You get less wheel-to-wheel racing in here than at Turn 1. In return you get the closest look at the cars at low speed and a direct view of the podium.

## A race weekend on the Day of the Dead

The 2026 race falls on November 1, the opening day of Día de Muertos. That turns the weekend into two events in one city. Families build *ofrendas* (home altars) with marigolds, candles, photographs and *pan de muerto*. Markets fill with sugar skulls and orange cempasúchil flowers.

The festival tends to spill into the circuit too. Fans often come in Catrina face paint, and skull imagery appears on merchandise around the grounds.

Many race visitors spend the evenings away from the track:

- **Mercado de Jamaica**, the city's big flower market, is a short ride from the circuit and full of marigolds this week.
- **Zócalo and the Centro Histórico** usually host large altar displays and seasonal installations.
- **San Andrés Mixquic**, in the south-east, is known for candlelit cemetery vigils on the nights of November 1 and 2.

## Getting to Magdalena Mixiuhca

Metro Line 9, the brown line, runs along the northern edge of the sports complex. It has three useful stops:

- **Ciudad Deportiva**: the station most fans use for the main gates.
- **Velódromo**: handy for the western side of the complex.
- **Puebla**: at the eastern end of the grounds.

Line 9 connects with Line 1 at Pantitlán and with Lines 2 and 3 at Chabacano and Centro Médico. You can reach it easily from Roma, Condesa and the Centro Histórico, which is where most visitors stay. Benito Juárez International Airport is only a few kilometres to the north-east. Fans who fly in for the weekend can be at the circuit faster than almost anywhere else on the calendar.

Roads near the track, including Viaducto and Circuito Interior, clog badly on race days. Ride-hail pickups after the race are slow, so many fans walk a few blocks away from the gates before ordering a car.

## Tickets and where to sit

The official Formula 1 website lists the Mexico City Grand Prix with its schedule and ticket links. This guide doesn't quote ticket prices, because none were supplied. Ticket costs appear on the official sale pages for each grandstand.

The seating falls into three broad types:

- **Stadium seats**: the loudest atmosphere and a view of the podium, with slower running.
- **Turn 1 grandstands**: overtaking and the start, at the end of that long straight.
- **General admission areas**: cheaper per seat as a type, but you stand or sit on banks with less shade.

Late October is the start of the dry season, but sunlight is harsh at this altitude even on cool mornings. Grandstand regulars bring a hat and sunscreen. They also pack a layer for the evening, because temperatures drop fast once the sun is low.

## Moving through race day like a chilango

Locals pay small amounts at Metro stations and street stalls with a rechargeable transport card, the *Tarjeta de Movilidad Integrada*. It works on the Metro and Metrobús. Fans usually load it once at the start of the weekend so they don't queue at a ticket machine.

At the circuit, most food and merchandise stands take cards. Vendors outside the gates often work in pesos only.

Expect a security search at every entrance. Gates move more slowly as the race nears, which is why seasoned attendees reach the grounds well before the support races start. Inside, people stay in the area named on their ticket, because the circuit's zones are separate.

A few phrases help with staff and vendors:

- *¿Dónde está la puerta…?*: where is gate…?
- *Tribuna*: grandstand.
- *La salida al Metro*: the exit to the Metro.

When the race ends, thousands of people head for Ciudad Deportiva station at once. Many fans wait 30 to 45 minutes in the stands for the podium and the stadium celebrations. Others walk to Velódromo or Puebla, where the platforms are less crowded.
