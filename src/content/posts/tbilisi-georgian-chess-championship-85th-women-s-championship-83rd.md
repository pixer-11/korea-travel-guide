## Ten days of silence on Rustaveli Avenue

A national title is decided here in near silence. For ten days at the turn of January into February, Georgia's strongest players sit across from each other in Tbilisi. There are two titles: the 85th open championship and the 83rd women's championship, both running at the same time.

The numbering tells you how long this has been going on. The women's event is only two editions younger than the open one. That is unusual anywhere, and it says a lot about Georgian chess.

## Dates, and where the boards go

The 2027 championships run from **January 27 to February 5, 2027**. Both divisions are contested across the same ten days, so one trip can cover the open and the women's games.

The last edition was staged at the **Biltmore Hotel Tbilisi**, on Rustaveli Avenue, and it used the same January 27 to February 5 window. The building is easy to spot. A glass tower rises behind the stone colonnade of the former Institute of Marx-Engels-Lenin, a Stalin-era facade that the hotel kept and built around.

The **Georgian Chess Federation** is the national body that runs the championships. Venue logistics, spectator access and any ticketing all come from the federation, not from the hotel's front desk.

## Getting to the hall

The Biltmore sits on the upper stretch of Rustaveli Avenue, Tbilisi's main boulevard. Most visitors come by metro:

- **Rustaveli station (Line 1)** is the closest stop, a few minutes' walk down the avenue.
- **Liberty Square station (Line 1)** is at the avenue's other end. From there it is a longer but flat walk past the Parliament building and the Opera.
- **Taxis** booked through ride-hailing apps are cheap and common in Tbilisi. "Biltmore" is all a driver needs to hear.

Late January in Tbilisi is cold, and the avenue gets a lot of wind. Spectators who stay for a full round usually wear layers they can take off once they are inside a heated hotel.

## Manners in the playing hall

This is a working tournament, not a show. Players are competing for a national title, and the room behaves accordingly.

- **Phones go fully silent or off.** In serious chess a ringing phone near the boards is a real offence, and arbiters do not treat it lightly.
- **Talking happens outside.** Regulars step into the corridor or lobby to discuss a position, never beside the board.
- **No flash and no leaning in.** Spectators keep a respectful distance from the tables and stay behind whatever barrier is in place.
- **Coming and going is quiet.** People slip in and out between moves and do not hover behind a player who is thinking.

Georgians usually greet each other with "gamarjoba" (hello), and a "madloba" (thank you) to hotel staff is always welcome. Card payment is normal along Rustaveli, so coffee between rounds rarely needs cash.

## Why the women's section matters here

Georgia has a famous place in women's chess. Nona Gaprindashvili and Maia Chiburdanidze, both from Georgia, held the Women's World Championship between them from 1962 to 1991. That's almost thirty years.

That legacy is why the women's championship has its own long numbering and its own following. Followers of the game often watch both divisions with equal attention. Many say the women's title race is the one Tbilisi talks about.

## Between rounds on the avenue

Rounds leave long gaps, and the Biltmore's location makes them easy to fill. Everything below is within walking distance along Rustaveli:

- **Georgian National Museum**, whose treasury holds gold work from ancient Colchis.
- **Tbilisi Opera and Ballet Theatre**, with its striped Moorish-revival facade.
- **Kashveti Church**, a stone church opposite the Parliament building.
- **Freedom Square**, where the avenue meets the old town. From there, streets drop down towards the sulphur bathhouses of Abanotubani.

Winter is low season in Tbilisi. The old town's wine bars and bakeries are calmer than in summer, and a hot khachapuri from a street-corner bakery is how many people warm up after a long game.
