## Why go

You feel the Arc de Triomphe before you really see it — twelve avenues fanning out around it like spokes, a solid wall of traffic circling below, and this 50-metre stone colossus sitting utterly still in the middle of the chaos. Napoleon commissioned it in 1806 to celebrate his military victories, though he never lived to see it finished; it was completed in 1836. Up close, the scale of the carved reliefs is the first surprise — François Rude's "La Marseillaise" on the northeast pillar has a raw, shouting energy that photos never quite capture. Underneath sits the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier, its flame relit every evening at 6:30pm, a small ceremony that most visitors walk right past without noticing.

## Getting there

The arch stands at Place Charles de Gaulle, 75008, at the top of the Champs-Élysées. The nearest metro/RER stop is Charles de Gaulle–Étoile, served by Lines 1, 2, and 6, plus RER A — take the exit signed for the Arc de Triomphe and use the underground pedestrian passage. This part is not optional: the roundabout above is one of the most chaotic traffic circles in Europe, and there is no safe surface crossing. The passage deposits you right at the base, ticket booth included. If you're walking down from the Louvre or Concorde, the Champs-Élysées approach itself is part of the experience — the arch grows steadily larger for the better part of a mile.

## What to see and do

The real reason to visit is the climb: 284 steps up a narrow spiral staircase to the rooftop observation deck. There's no lift for the final stretch, so budget stamina as well as time — most people spend 45 minutes to an hour total, including the ticket line and the climb. At the top, the view lays out the axe historique in both directions: the Champs-Élysées running straight down to the Tuileries and the Louvre, and, looking the other way, the modern Grande Arche de la Défense lining up with almost eerie precision. On the way up, a small museum level covers the monument's construction and history, with models and engravings worth a few minutes. Inside the arch's four pillars, the names of Napoleon's generals and battles are carved into the stone — worth reading even if the volume of names is overwhelming at first glance.

## When to go

The Arc is open every day, with hours running 10am–11pm on Monday, Wednesday through Sunday, and from 11am on Tuesdays. Weekends get genuinely busy from 1pm to 8pm, when tour groups stack up at the ticket counter and the staircase turns into a slow shuffle. Both weekday and weekend mornings and evenings stay markedly calmer — arriving close to opening or after 8pm gives you a much freer run of the staircase and a better shot at photographing the view without a crowd at the railing. Sunset is genuinely worth timing for: the light rakes low across the rooftops and the Champs-Élysées lights up below as dusk settles in.

## How to visit like a local

Skip the surface crossing entirely — always use the underpass from Charles de Gaulle–Étoile station, both for safety and because it's simply the route everyone uses. Book timed-entry tickets online in advance where possible; it doesn't eliminate the line but it moves you past the ticket window faster. Given this is one of the most visited monuments in the city, don't expect a quiet, undiscovered corner of Paris — the crowd is the reality, so work with the quieter hours instead of against them: aim for opening time or the last hour or two before closing. Payment at the ticket counter is by card as much as cash these days, though carrying some cash is never a bad habit in France. The mistake most first-timers make is treating this as a five-minute photo stop from street level and skipping the climb — the ground-level arch is impressive, but the view from the roof is the actual point of coming. If your legs are tired, remember there's no elevator to the top, only to a mid-level landing, so pace yourself on the spiral stairs.
