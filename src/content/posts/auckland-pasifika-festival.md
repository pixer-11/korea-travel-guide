## Two days, one park, a dozen islands

Pasifika Festival runs from March 13 to 14, 2027. It is billed as the world's largest Polynesian festival, and it fills Western Springs Park, the green bowl of lawns and lake beside Great North Road in Auckland's inner west.

The layout is the reason to come. The festival is traditionally divided into island villages, each with its own food stalls, craft tables and performance stage. In past editions these have included:

- Samoa, Tonga, Fiji and the Cook Islands
- Niue, Tokelau and Tuvalu
- Tahiti, Hawai'i, Kiribati and Aotearoa

Walking from one village to the next takes minutes, but the drums, the language and the dishes change completely.

## What fills the plate

Food queues are the backbone of the day. Smoke from umu (earth-oven) cooking and barbecue grills drifts across the paths, and stalls serve dishes you rarely find on a city menu. Look for:

- **Ika mata / oka:** raw fish cured in citrus and coconut cream
- **Sapasui:** Samoan chop suey, glass noodles with meat and soy
- **Palusami / lu pulu:** taro leaves baked in coconut cream
- **Keke pua'a and panikeke:** steamed pork buns and round fried pancakes
- **Otai and fresh coconuts:** drunk straight from the shell

Between meals, the stages run siva, tau'olunga, meke and Cook Islands drumming, plus contemporary Pacific bands and hip hop. Craft stalls sell tapa, woven fans, ula and printed fabric.

## Getting to Western Springs

Western Springs Park sits off Great North Road, between Grey Lynn and Point Chevalier. Auckland Zoo and MOTAT (the Museum of Transport and Technology) are its immediate neighbours, so the area is easy to name to any driver.

Buses along Great North Road stop within a short walk of the park entrances. Kingsland Station on the Western Line is the closest rail stop, though it leaves a longer walk. Parking near the park is tight on festival days, and most attendees use public transport or rideshare drop-offs along the main road.

Plan on a full afternoon at least. Many families treat it as an all-day outing, moving between villages and settling on the grass for the evening sets.

## Moving through the villages

The festival runs on community energy. Many stalls are run by church groups, families and island associations, so service follows its own pace and the best dishes sometimes sell out by late afternoon.

Habits regulars tend to share:

- They carry cash as well as cards, because payment setups vary from stall to stall.
- They bring a picnic blanket or mat and claim lawn near a favourite stage.
- They wear a hat and sunscreen; much of the park is open lawn with limited shade.
- They eat a little in each village rather than one big meal in the first.

Performances carry cultural weight. Audiences usually sit to watch, keep walkways clear in front of the stage, and ask before photographing performers up close. A "talofa", "mālō e lelei" or "kia orana" at the right stall earns a smile.

On tickets: the festival has historically been free to enter. The organiser publishes the dates, venue map and any ticketing details on its official site.
