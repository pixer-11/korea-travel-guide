## Four nights to close the year

The calendar does most of the work here. Sung Si-kyung holds his traditional year-end concert at KSPO Dome from December 31, 2026 to January 3, 2027. That makes four dates that straddle the change of year, with the first one falling on New Year's Eve itself.

He has been a fixture of Korean ballad singing since his debut in 2000. The songs people know him for are slow and built around his voice: "On the Street" (거리에서), "Two People" (두사람), and "Every Moment of You" (너의 모든 순간) from the drama *My Love from the Star*. The set list for this run is not public, so treat those as his catalogue rather than a promise.

## KSPO Dome, the round roof in Olympic Park

The venue is a big indoor arena with a history. KSPO Dome was built as the gymnastics arena for the 1988 Seoul Olympics. Its domed roof sits inside Olympic Park, a wide stretch of lawns, sculptures and walking paths in Songpa-gu, southeast Seoul.

Inside, the arena is laid out in a bowl. Raked seating rings a flat floor, and for concerts the stage takes one end. It is one of the city's standard large venues for solo singers who fill arenas, so the sight lines and the crowd flow are built for exactly this kind of night.

## Riding Lines 5 and 8 to the gate

Two subway stations serve the park:

- **Olympic Park Station (Line 5)**, exit 3, which brings you out on the park's edge closest to the dome.
- **Mongchontoseong Station (Line 8)**, exit 1, which leads in past the Peace Gate on the park's west side.
- **Jamsil Station (Lines 2 and 8)** is a longer walk, or one stop on Line 8, and suits people staying near Lotte World Tower.

From either of the nearest exits the walk takes around 10 minutes on wide paved paths. After the show, thousands of people head for the same two stations at once. Many attendees walk to Bangi Station (Line 5) or Jamsil instead to skip the crush at the platform gates.

## Getting through the gate at a Korean arena show

Korean concerts follow a fairly fixed routine. Reserved tickets at large Seoul shows are usually issued under the booker's name. Staff at the entrance commonly match that name against photo ID, so overseas fans carry their passport rather than a photocopy.

Merchandise booths are set up outside the dome, and the lines for them often form long before the hall opens. Inside, filming and photography during the performance are generally banned at Korean concerts, and ushers enforce it. Fans keep phones down and save their photos for the stage before the lights drop.

Ballad crowds sing along in Korean, often in full. You don't need the language for that, but learning the chorus of "Two People" helps if it comes up.

## Dressing for a Songpa night in deep winter

Late December and early January are the coldest stretch of Seoul's year. Night temperatures sit well below freezing, and Olympic Park is open ground with little to block the wind between the station and the doors.

The arena is heated, which creates the usual dilemma. Locals solve it with layers:

- a long padded coat (the knee-length *rongpaeding* is everywhere in Seoul in winter) that rolls up under a seat
- a thin knit underneath for the hall
- gloves and hand warmers, sold at convenience stores near both stations

## Eating around Olympic Park before and after

The singer runs a popular food channel on YouTube, so a meal fits the occasion. The nearest serious cluster is the Bangi-dong food alley (방이동 먹자골목), next to Bangi Station on Line 5, at the park's southwest corner. The narrow blocks are packed with barbecue, stews and late-opening pubs.

For something calmer, Seokchon Lake and Lotte World Tower in Jamsil sit a stop or two away. Their malls and food halls make an easy dinner before a show. On New Year's Eve the area stays busy late, and the crowds coming out of the dome add to it.

If you have a daylight hour to spare, walk the grassy walls of Mongchontoseong, the earthen fortress inside Olympic Park that dates to the Baekje kingdom. It is a quiet counterpoint to an arena full of people singing.
