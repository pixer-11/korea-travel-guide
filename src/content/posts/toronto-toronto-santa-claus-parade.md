Late November, Bloor Street West is lined several people deep. The kids are in snowsuits and the adults hold thermoses. Folding chairs sit in neat rows along the curb, and a band you can hear before you see it is tuning up somewhere near Christie Pits. On November 22, 2026, the 122nd Original Santa Claus Parade leaves Christie and Bloor and winds into downtown Toronto. It ends, as it always does, with one float everyone has been waiting for.

## A parade older than the skyline

This is one of the longest-running Santa Claus parades in the world. The first one rolled through Toronto in 1905, and the "Original" in its name is a claim the city takes seriously. For most of the 20th century it belonged to Eaton's department store, which used Santa's arrival to open the holiday shopping season.

When Eaton's stepped away in 1982, a group of local businesses took it over rather than let it end. It has run as a community event ever since. Many of the adults on the curb were once the kids in snowsuits, and they come back with their own families.

## Christie and Bloor: where it starts

The parade forms up at the corner of Christie Street and Bloor Street West. That puts the start beside Christie Pits, the sunken park on the north side of Bloor. The parade then heads east toward the downtown core.

The western end tends to feel more like a neighbourhood. Floats are still lining up and performers are finding their places. People who live in the Annex and Koreatown often walk over instead of taking transit. The parade's official website has the full route map, including each turn after Bloor.

## Santa always comes last

The thing to understand about this parade is its order. Santa's sleigh float traditionally closes the procession. Everything before it builds toward that moment:

- themed floats, many sponsored by Toronto businesses
- marching bands from across Ontario and beyond
- clowns and costumed walkers working the crowd at kid height
- characters on foot handing out waves and high-fives

The whole parade takes a while to pass any one point. Families usually treat the early floats as the warm-up and save their loudest cheering for the end. If you leave when the kids get cold, you miss the point of the day. Parents who know this tend to pack snacks and spare mittens so they can stay put for Santa.

## Picking your stretch of curb

Where you stand changes the experience. Bloor Street near the start is wide, with long sightlines. The sections closer to the core pull bigger crowds because they are easiest to reach from more subway stations.

Families with small children usually look for a spot with a building or storefront behind them. It blocks the wind and gives toddlers something to lean on. Regulars set up chairs and blankets well before the first float reaches them, especially along the busiest blocks. A curb spot at the very front is what most kids care about. Adults can usually see fine from behind, and the floats are tall.

## Getting there when the streets close

Roads along the route close on parade day, so driving downtown is slow and parking is scarce. Most people take the TTC. These subway stations sit on or near the route:

- **Christie (Line 2 Bloor–Danforth):** steps from the start at Christie Pits
- **Bathurst, Spadina, St George and Bay (Line 2):** for spots further east along Bloor
- **Museum and Queen's Park (Line 1 Yonge–University):** for the stretch nearer the University of Toronto and Queen's Park

Trains are packed with strollers in the hour before the parade reaches each station, and again once Santa has passed. Many families give themselves extra time on the way home rather than squeeze onto the first train.

## Curbside manners on parade day

There's no ticket booth and no gate. Watching from the street is free, and the parade's official website is where any extras like seating or fundraising are listed. What there is, though, is an unwritten code along the curb.

- **Front row is for kids.** Adults standing at the curb in front of children get asked, politely, to step back.
- **Chairs and blankets mark a spot,** but leaving them empty for hours to save half a block is frowned upon.
- **Don't step into the road** for a photo. Marshals keep the route clear for floats, and the bands march close to the curb.
- **Dress for standing still.** Late November in Toronto usually means near-freezing temperatures. Waiting on concrete feels colder than walking, so locals wear boots and layers rather than fashionable coats.

Torontonians tend to be friendly about sharing space when asked. A quick "mind if we squeeze in?" usually gets your kids to the front.

## Before and after the floats

The area around the route gives you plenty to fill the rest of the day. Near the start, Christie Pits is a natural meeting point. Bloor Street through Koreatown, between Christie and Bathurst, is lined with restaurants for a warm meal afterwards.

Further east, the Royal Ontario Museum stands at Bloor and Queen's Park, beside Museum station. Indoors, it makes a good fallback for frozen fingers. The University of Toronto's St George campus and the Ontario Legislature at Queen's Park are both a short walk from the route. Each has open grounds where families often regroup after Santa has passed.

For many Toronto families, parade day kicks off the holiday season. Afterwards, plenty of them head straight on to downtown's holiday window displays and markets.
