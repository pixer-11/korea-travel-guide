## Where the continent runs out

Cape Byron is where mainland Australia stops. A short timber boardwalk leads you down to a marker at the Most Easterly Point. Past it there is nothing but the Pacific.

The headland is protected as a nature reserve. Coastal rainforest and wind-cut heath cover the slopes, and the white Victorian lighthouse sits on top. Below you, the swell breaks against dark rock. On a clear day you can look inland to the jagged outline of Wollumbin (Mount Warning).

## The lighthouse on the summit

Cape Byron Lighthouse was finished in 1901. It is a squat white tower built from concrete blocks, and it is often described as the most powerful lighthouse in Australia. The old keepers' cottages sit around it, and the head keeper's cottage now holds a small maritime display and visitor information.

You can't miss it. It is the fixed point every other view on the headland is measured against. You'll see it from Wategos Beach, from the Pass, and from the top of the stairs.

Captain James Cook named the cape in 1770, after the navigator John Byron. The land has a much longer story with the Arakwal people of the Bundjalung Nation. Interpretive signs along the track tell some of it.

## Walking the loop

The Cape Byron Walking Track is a loop of roughly 3.7 km. Allow two hours or so with stops. Most people start from the Captain Cook Lookout car park above Clarkes Beach, then head east. The main stops are:

- **The Pass:** a rocky point where surfers paddle out along the headland
- **Wategos Beach:** a small, north-facing cove below the cliffs
- **Fishermans Lookout:** a clifftop platform that is good for spotting dolphins
- **Most Easterly Point:** the boardwalk and marker
- **The lighthouse:** on the summit, with views south over Tallow Beach

The track is a mix of sealed path, boardwalk and many steps, some of them steep. It climbs in full sun in places, so carry water. The shade of the rainforest sections only covers part of the way.

## Whales, dolphins and the water below

Between June and November, humpback whales migrate along this coast. Because the headland sticks so far out, they often pass close enough to see from the lookouts. Look for spouts first, then the slap of a tail or a breach. Pods of bottlenose dolphins are common in the waves off the Pass all year round.

Offshore, the rocky outcrop of Julian Rocks is a marine reserve and a well-known dive site. You'll see it from almost anywhere on the eastern side of the loop.

## Getting up the headland without the parking fight

Walk in if you can. From the Byron Bay town centre, follow the beach path along Main Beach and Clarkes Beach to the Captain Cook Lookout. You'll join the loop without ever moving the car.

If you drive, Lighthouse Road winds up to a small paid car park near the summit. It has only a limited number of spaces. The machines at the car park show the current rates. There is also paid parking near the Captain Cook Lookout.

Plan your visit around the listed hours of 9:30am to 4:30pm, which are the same every day. Give yourself enough of that window to finish the loop without hurrying the stairs.

## Reading the headland like a regular

People who walk this loop often have a few habits worth copying:

- **Go anticlockwise from Clarkes Beach.** The climb comes early and the downhill run to Tallow and back is easier on your legs.
- **Stop at Fishermans Lookout and wait.** Whale spotting is patient work. Scan the water for ten minutes rather than glancing and moving on.
- **Keep to the formed path.** The cliff edges are unstable, and the heath is protected habitat.
- **Pack binoculars in whale season.** They turn a distant spout into a visible animal.
- **Swim at Wategos and check the conditions first.** Swim only where the beach is patrolled and conditions are calm. The water around the Pass has strong currents.

Leave the headland as you found it. It is a conservation area, and dogs are not allowed on the track.
