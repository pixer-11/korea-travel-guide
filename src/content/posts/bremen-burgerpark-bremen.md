## A landscape park behind the station

You walk out of a train station and you're in a park. Leave Bremen Hauptbahnhof by the north side and cross the open square of the Bürgerweide. The tree line of the Bürgerpark starts right there. Within a few minutes the tram noise has dropped away and you're on gravel paths beside wide lawns and still water.

This is an English-style landscape park, laid out from 1866 to designs by Wilhelm Benque. It's built around views, not flowerbeds. Paths curve between clumps of old trees, and then a meadow opens out onto a lake. Together with the Stadtwald to the north, the green belt covers roughly 200 hectares, so you can walk a long way without doubling back.

## A park the citizens still pay for

The name means exactly what it says. Bürgerpark translates as "citizens' park": Bremen residents created it, not the city government. A private association, the Bürgerparkverein, still looks after it, and the upkeep is paid for largely by members and donations.

You'll see its best-known fundraiser in the old town. Every year, ticket booths for the Bürgerpark-Tombola go up in Bremen's city centre. This lottery has been running for decades. If you pass the stands on your way to the Marktplatz, a ticket goes straight to the trees and paths you're about to walk under.

That arrangement shapes the feel of the place. The park is free and open to everyone, and it's kept in good order by people who live nearby and chose to fund it.

## Water, meadows and the Meierei

Most first loops follow the water. The paths join up, so you can link these in any order:

- **Emmasee**: the largest lake, with the café-boathouse Emma am See on its shore. In the warmer months you can usually rent a rowing boat here.
- **Hollersee**: a smaller lake at the park's southern edge, overlooked by the Parkhotel Bremen and its distinctive dome.
- **Meierei**: the park's old dairy building, now a restaurant. It's a natural halfway stop on a long walk.
- **Marcusbrunnen**: a fountain worth a pause between the lakes.
- **Tiergehege**: a small animal enclosure. Families with young children tend to head straight for it.

Between these, the big meadows do most of the work. On fine days they fill with picnic blankets, frisbees and people reading in the shade at the edge of the trees.

## Into the Stadtwald

Keep heading north and the park changes character. The Bürgerpark runs on into the Stadtwald, Bremen's city forest. The landscaping here feels looser: denser trees, narrower paths, fewer lawns.

At the far end, near the university, is the Unisee. This lake is a popular summer swimming spot with locals and students. Walking the whole way from the station to the Unisee and back is a proper half-day outing, so it's best done on a bike if you're short of time.

## Getting there from the Hauptbahnhof

You don't need a car. The park starts just past the station.

- **On foot:** take the north exit of Bremen Hauptbahnhof and cross the Bürgerweide. The park's southern edge is about ten minutes' walk away, with the Hollersee and Parkhotel close by.
- **During Freimarkt:** every October the Bürgerweide fills with the Freimarkt, one of Germany's oldest fairs. You then reach the park through rows of rides and food stalls, which is either a bonus or a detour depending on your mood.
- **By bike:** Bremen is one of Germany's most cycle-friendly cities. A rental bike makes it easy to cover the park and the Stadtwald in one go.
- **From the old town:** the Marktplatz, the Roland statue and the Schnoor quarter are on the other side of the station. Combining the old town and the park makes a full, easy day.

## Paths, bikes and park manners

Bremen cycles a lot, and that sets the rules here. Some paths are shared and some are for walkers only, so follow the signs at the junctions. Keep to the right. A bell behind you means a cyclist wants to pass, not that you're in trouble.

Dogs are a constant presence. Expect owners to keep them on leads across most of the park. On the lawns, picnicking is normal and expected. Pack your rubbish out with you.

One habit is worth copying. Germany runs a bottle deposit system called **Pfand**, and people often stand empty bottles next to a bin rather than inside it. Collectors pick them up to claim the deposit.

At the cafés and the Meierei, cards are increasingly accepted, but keep some euros in cash for smaller kiosks. To tip, round up the bill and tell the server the total you want to pay as you hand over the money. Leaving coins on the table is less usual.

Sundays have a rhythm of their own. Shops in Germany close on Sundays, so families come to the park instead. Weekday mornings are generally more peaceful on the main paths.

## When to go and how long to stay

Every season works here, for different reasons. Spring brings fresh green and lighter crowds. Summer is the time for rowing boats on the Emmasee and swimming at the Unisee. Autumn is arguably the best time: old beeches and oaks turn the long sightlines gold.

Google currently lists the park as open around the clock. The Meierei, Emma am See and other cafés keep their own hours, which change with the season, so check them before planning lunch. After dark, much of the park is unlit woodland. Stick to the main paths if you're walking through late.

For a first visit, allow two to three hours for the lakes, the Meierei and the animal enclosure. Add the Stadtwald and a stop at the Unisee and it becomes a half day.
