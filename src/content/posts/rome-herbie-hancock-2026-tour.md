## One Monday night in Flaminio

The date is Monday, 14 December 2026. Herbie Hancock plays the Sala Santa Cecilia, the largest of the three concert halls inside Rome's Auditorium Parco della Musica. The complex sits in Flaminio, north of Piazza del Popolo, where the city's sports and arts buildings from the last century cluster near the Tiber.

This is a seated concert hall rather than a club. The setting suits the music: Hancock's career runs from Blue Note hard bop to electric funk to Grammy-winning acoustic records. His band in Rome has spent years working the same territory.

## The five musicians on stage

Hancock was born in Chicago in 1940. His history is long enough to fill a concert on its own:

- **Miles Davis's Second Great Quintet** in the 1960s, the group that reshaped modern jazz
- **"Watermelon Man", "Cantaloupe Island" and "Maiden Voyage"**, now standards
- **Head Hunters** (1973), the jazz-funk album that crossed into the pop charts
- **"Rockit"** (1983), the turntable-scratch hit with a famous music video
- **River: The Joni Letters**, which won the Grammy for Album of the Year

The band around him carries real weight too. **Terence Blanchard** is a trumpeter known for his Spike Lee film scores and his opera *Fire Shut Up in My Bones*, staged at the Metropolitan Opera. **Lionel Loueke**, the guitarist from Benin, has worked with Hancock for years and brings his West African rhythms and voice effects.

**James Genus** holds down the bass. Many Americans know him from the *Saturday Night Live* house band. **Jaylen Petinaud** completes the line-up on drums.

## Inside Renzo Piano's three beetles

From above, the Auditorium looks like three giant beetles circling a courtyard. Renzo Piano designed it, and it opened in 2002. Romans have compared the halls to scarabs and to lute bodies ever since. Each one has a curved lead-clad roof, and all three face the open-air Cavea amphitheatre in the middle.

The Sala Santa Cecilia is the biggest. It seats well over two thousand people around a stage lined in warm wood. Panels hung under the ceiling shape the sound. Rome's Accademia Nazionale di Santa Cecilia orchestra plays its symphonic seasons here, so the room was built for unamplified detail. That matters when Hancock moves from a funk groove to a solo piano passage.

Many concertgoers stop at the Roman ruins on the way in. Builders found the foundations of an ancient villa while digging the site, and the complex kept them on display. The remains sit inside the Auditorium grounds with a small archaeological exhibit. It's a very Roman detail: a jazz concert above a farmhouse that is two thousand years older than the music.

## Getting to Viale Pietro de Coubertin

There's no metro station at the door. Most concertgoers use one of these:

- **Tram 2** from Piazzale Flaminio, beside Flaminio station on Metro Line A. Get off at the Apollodoro stop, a short walk from the Auditorium.
- **Bus 910** from Termini station, which runs up to the Auditorium area on Via Pietro de Coubertin.
- **Taxi** from the centre, a short ride up Via Flaminia when traffic is light.

Coming from the historic centre, many people walk to Piazza del Popolo, pass through the Porta del Popolo to Piazzale Flaminio and catch the tram from there. On ATAC trams and buses, you either validate a paper ticket in the machine on board or tap a contactless card where the reader allows it.

## How a concert night works at the Auditorium

Evenings at the Auditorium follow a steady routine. Concertgoers usually arrive with time to cross the Cavea courtyard, find the right hall entrance and use the *guardaroba* (cloakroom). Winter coats are bulky, and seats in a wood-lined hall are close together.

- **Ticket checks** happen at the hall doors. Each of the three halls has its own entrance, so the ticket shows *Sala Santa Cecilia* for a reason.
- **Paying** by contactless card is normal at the box office, the bars and the restaurants on site. Romans still keep a few coins for coffee at the counter.
- **Etiquette** inside is jazz-club rather than symphony. Applause after solos is expected, and Hancock audiences often sing back the riff of "Chameleon" if it comes up.
- **Photos and filming** during the set are generally frowned on in Italian concert halls. Ushers will often ask you to put your phone away.

After the encore, the tram stop and taxi rank fill up quickly. Many people walk a few minutes away from the main exit before looking for a cab. Others use FreeNow, the app Romans use to call licensed taxis.

## Dinner, aperitivo and the walk back

Rome eats late, which helps on concert nights. An *aperitivo*, a drink with small plates, is the usual way to start the evening. The bars and restaurant inside the Auditorium complex are the simplest choice. Via Flaminia and the streets around Piazza Mancini have neighbourhood trattorias and pizzerias aimed at local residents more than visitors.

There's plenty to see nearby by day. These are all within walking distance:

- **MAXXI**, Zaha Hadid's museum of 21st-century art and architecture on Via Guido Reni
- **Palazzetto dello Sport**, Pier Luigi Nervi's ribbed concrete dome from the 1960 Olympics
- **Ponte della Musica**, the pedestrian bridge over the Tiber towards Foro Italico
- **Villa Borghese**, whose northern edge is a short tram ride south
