## Why go

At 632 metres, Shanghai Tower is the tallest building in China and the second-tallest habitable structure on Earth, and you feel that scale the moment you stand at its base and try to find the top with your neck craned all the way back. The twisting glass facade — nine cylindrical zones stacked and rotated as it rises — was engineered specifically to cut wind loads on a megatall tower, and from the street it reads less like a wall of glass than a slow-motion corkscrew of light. Inside, the building holds two observation decks, restaurants, a boutique hotel occupying the upper floors, and a fitted-out atrium that developers call the "world's highest" of its kind. Across the water sits the older Lujiazui skyline — the Oriental Pearl Tower's pink spheres, the bottle-opener-shaped Shanghai World Financial Center — so the view isn't just about height, it's about watching Shanghai's entire skyward ambition laid out in one frame.

## Getting there

The tower sits in Lujiazui, Pudong's financial district, at 501 Yin Cheng Zhong Lu. The easiest approach is Shanghai Metro Line 2 to Lujiazui Station — take the exit signed for Lujiazui/IFC and it's a short, well-marked walk past the mall and underground passages that link the district's towers. Line 14 also stops nearby. If you're coming from Puxi side attractions like the Bund, a river ferry across from Jinling Road landing gives you a low-angle view of the tower growing as you approach, which is worth the extra ten minutes if you're not in a rush.

## What to see and do

Ticketing and security lead you into a queue for the high-speed elevators, which are genuinely one of the highlights — they're among the fastest passenger elevators built, and the pressure change in your ears during the ascent is a small, memorable jolt. The main observation deck spans floors 118–119, enclosed by glass that lets you look almost straight down onto the Huangpu River traffic and the older towers that once defined this skyline. On a clear day you can pick out the Bund's colonial-era waterfront directly across the water and trace the river's curve toward the East China Sea; on a hazy one, the closer towers still loom impressively even if the horizon disappears. Inside the tower, the damper system — a giant gold sphere that stabilizes the building against sway — is visible from an interior viewing point and is worth pausing at, since it's one of the few places where you see the raw engineering rather than just the polished view.

## When to go

The tower opens at 8:30am and closes at 9:30pm every day of the week, so there's no early-morning or late-night workaround — you're working within that 13-hour window. If you want the deck close to yourself, arrive right at opening: 8:30am–9:30am is the measurably quietest stretch on both weekdays and weekends. Avoid showing up between 7pm and 9pm on weekends, when the deck fills up fastest as visitors chase the lit-up night skyline — a genuinely good view, but one you'll be sharing with a crowd. A late-afternoon visit timed to watch the transition from daylight to the city's evening lights is a reasonable middle ground if you can't make the early slot.

## How to visit like a local

Buy timed-entry tickets online ahead of your visit rather than queuing at the counter — this is standard practice here and cuts your wait significantly, since walk-up lines can stretch long during peak hours. Payment inside is almost entirely mobile (Alipay/WeChat Pay) or card; carrying cash isn't necessary. There's no tipping expected anywhere in the building. The single biggest mistake visitors make is treating this like a quick photo stop and rushing straight to the elevator — build in time to walk the lower atrium and check out the damper viewing area, which most rushed visitors skip entirely. Security screening (similar to airport-style checks) adds a real chunk of time, so don't cut your arrival close to a sunset slot if you want a seat by the glass. Since this is one of Shanghai's most visited landmarks rather than a quiet local spot, the practical local wisdom is simply about timing your entry to dodge the crowd rather than expecting to have the place to yourself.
