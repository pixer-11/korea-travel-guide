Most zoos make you queue at a ticket window before you've even decided if you want to be there. This one skips that step entirely: you park along the Route du Semnoz, walk through an open gate, and you're already looking at chamois grazing a slope that was farmland a century ago.

## What Grande Jeanne actually is

This isn't a zoo stocked with imported megafauna. It's a regional wildlife park built into a hillside pasture, focused on animals that genuinely live in the Alps: marmots, ibex, chamois, wild boar, foxes, and a handful of deer species share paddocks that follow the natural slope of the Semnoz rather than a flat, paved circuit.

The paths are unpaved and uneven in places, threading between wooden fences and stands of pine. There's no grand entrance building, no gift shop dominating the view. The whole place reads more like a working alpine smallholding that happens to let you wander through it.

## Getting there and what it costs

The address is 14 Route du Semnoz, on the road that climbs toward the Semnoz summit above Annecy town.

- By car: around 15 minutes from central Annecy, following signs for Semnoz/Crêt de Chatillon; roadside parking near the entrance.
- By bike: a popular stop for cyclists training on the Semnoz climb, a route also known for its Tour de France history.
- Admission: free, with no ticket counter and no set opening or closing time.

Because it's unstaffed and gateless in the way a ticketed attraction would be, there's also no café or facilities cluster at the entrance, so plan food and water before you arrive.

## When to go if you want the animals to yourself

The park logs its quietest stretch on weekend mornings between 7am and 9am, before the day-trippers from Annecy make the drive up.

Weekend crowds build steadily and peak between 11am and 5pm, when family groups and picnicking parties fill the grassy areas near the paddocks. If you're visiting on a Saturday or Sunday, an early arrival genuinely changes the experience: animals are more active in the cool morning air, and you won't be sharing the fence line with a dozen other visitors trying to get a photo of the same ibex.

Weekdays don't have the same measured pattern, but logic still applies: term-time mornings tend to be thinner than a sunny weekend afternoon.

## The picnic ritual on the hillside

Grande Jeanne's open-air layout means the picnic areas aren't an afterthought bolted onto a gift shop, they're woven through the park itself, on grassy terraces between paddocks. Locals treat it as a standard warm-weather outing: pack a baguette, cheese, and something cold, then eat with a view over grazing deer rather than at a table in a car park.

There's no on-site restaurant, so this isn't optional if you want a proper meal; bring your own. Bins are provided, and the informal etiquette is simple: pack out anything the bins can't take, and keep food away from the fences so animals aren't fed by hand.

## Getting around the paddocks

Because the park is open 24 hours with no staffed gate, there's also no guided tour structure and no printed map handed to you at an entrance. Signage along the paths identifies each enclosure, and the loop is walkable in roughly 45 minutes to an hour and a half depending on how long you linger.

- Wear proper shoes: the ground is gravel and packed earth, with a real incline in sections.
- Keep dogs leashed if you bring one; livestock and wild-adjacent species share the fence lines closely.
- Visit in spring or early summer for the most active young animals, since ibex and deer often have offspring visible in the paddocks then.
- Combine it with the Semnoz summit road if you're driving up anyway, since the park sits on the same route as the Crêt de Chatillon viewpoint.

## Why it stays low-key despite being well known

Grande Jeanne has a solid reputation around Annecy and shows up reliably in local recommendations, but it has never been dressed up as a headline tourist attraction. There's no marketing push, no branded merchandise stand, no queue management system, because none of that infrastructure exists here.

That absence is part of the appeal. It functions the way a small municipal park might, quietly maintained, free to enter, used mostly by people who already know the Semnoz road well. Visiting outside the weekend crowd window keeps that low-key feeling intact rather than turning it into another stop on a checklist.

## Before you drive up

Because the park has no fixed hours listed beyond being continuously open, it's worth checking current signage or local listings if you're planning a very early or very late visit, in case of seasonal access changes on the Semnoz road itself, which can affect the route in winter.

Pack for changeable mountain weather even if Annecy town is warm and sunny, since the elevation gain on the drive up is enough to shift temperatures a few degrees cooler.
