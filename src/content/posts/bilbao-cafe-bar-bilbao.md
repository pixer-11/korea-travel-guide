## Why go

Plaza Berria doesn't look like much on a map — a modest square tucked into the grid of Bilbao's Casco Viejo — but Café Bar Bilbao is the reason people detour for it. Push through the door and the room opens up wider than you expect: high ceilings, worn mirrors, tiled floors, wooden fittings gone amber with decades of use.

It's the kind of retro Spanish café-bar that looks like it hasn't changed its bones since the mid-20th century, even as the crowd around you skews younger and louder with every hour. The bar itself runs long, lined with trays of pintxos — the Basque cousin of tapas, small bites skewered with a toothpick so the bartender can tally your bill by counting sticks at the end.

## Getting there

You're in Ibaiondo, right on the edge of the Casco Viejo, Bilbao's old town. The easiest anchor is Casco Viejo metro station (Line 1 and Line 2), a walk of a few minutes through the narrow streets that fan out from the Siete Calles. If you're coming from the Guggenheim or the Abandoibarra riverside, it's an easy 20–25 minute stroll along the Nervión, or a short tram/metro hop back into the old quarter.

Plaza Berria itself is small and easy to miss on a first pass — look for the crowd spilling out with glasses in hand, especially in the evening.

## What to see and eat

This isn't a place with a printed menu you pore over — it's a place where you walk the length of the bar, scan the counter, and point. Expect classic pintxos: tortilla wedges, jamón on baguette, croquetas, anchovy and pepper combinations, alongside the usual short beers (cañas) and coffees. Prices are on the budget-friendly end for what you get, part of why the place fills up.

The room itself is worth lingering in — the retro signage, the din of conversation bouncing off hard surfaces, the sense that this is a working neighborhood bar that happens to be excellent, not a place dressed up for visitors. Grab a stool near the window if you want to watch Plaza Berria's slower daytime rhythm shift into its evening buzz.

## When to go

The rhythm here is worth planning around. On weekdays, the calm window is early — 7am to 9am — when it's really just coffee, pastries, and locals passing through before work. After that, and especially through the weekend, the room fills steadily. Weekends run busy from around midday straight through to closing at 11pm, so there isn't really a lull to wait out — if you want the room to yourself, weekday early morning is your best and really only shot.

If you're coming for the classic pintxos-and-beer scene rather than quiet coffee, lean into the weekend crowd instead of fighting it — it's part of the experience.

## How to visit like a local

Order at the bar rather than waiting to be served if it's busy — this is a stand-and-eat, catch-the-bartender's-eye kind of place, not table service by default. Pintxos are tallied by the toothpicks left on your plate, so don't worry about tracking what you've eaten; settle up at the end. Cash still moves faster here than fumbling with a card for a couple of euros, though card is generally accepted.

Basque bar etiquette leans casual and quick: order, eat standing at the bar or a high table, move on if it's slammed — lingering for hours at the counter during peak times isn't really the local rhythm, even if the room encourages it. The mistake visitors make is treating it like a sit-down restaurant and waiting for a menu; instead, walk the bar, look at what's out, and ask for it by pointing or with a simple "este" (this one).

Given how well-known and consistently busy this spot has become, don't expect a hidden gem — expect a genuinely well-loved neighborhood institution, and time your visit around the 7–9am lull if solitude matters more to you than the crowd.

Give yourself 30–45 minutes for a coffee stop, or an hour-plus if you're grazing several rounds of pintxos with a drink. Either way, it pairs naturally with a wander through the Siete Calles or a pre-Guggenheim breakfast.
