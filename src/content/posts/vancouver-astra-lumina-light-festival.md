Under the cedars and Douglas firs of Stanley Park, the dark comes early from late October. Astra Lumina uses that dark. It is a touring night walk built from light, sound and projection, and in 2026 it takes over a stretch of Vancouver's best-known park from October 22 to December 31.

You walk the route on foot after nightfall. The forest gives the light something to land on: tall trunks, wet ground, the canopy overhead.

## What the walk is

Astra Lumina is an illuminated trail on a celestial theme. It is not a fairground light display. The idea is a slow, guided-by-the-path stroll where each section of the route has its own lighting, soundtrack and effects, so the forest reads like a sequence of scenes.

It travels between cities, and Stanley Park is its Vancouver setting for this run. The route is outdoors and walked at your own pace. Most people take roughly an hour from entrance to exit.

## Dates and tickets for the 2026 run

The festival runs from October 22 to December 31, 2026. That puts it across Halloween, the long November evenings and the whole holiday stretch, finishing on New Year's Eve.

The organiser publishes the nightly schedule, entry times and ticket links on the official Astra Lumina website. Prices aren't printed here; the official ticket page lists them. ## Getting to Stanley Park at night

Stanley Park sits at the northwest tip of the downtown peninsula, between Coal Harbour and English Bay. Getting there takes a few minutes from downtown hotels, but the roads inside the park are one-way loops, which confuses first-time drivers.

Your options:

- **Bus:** TransLink's route 19 runs along West Pender Street to the Stanley Park bus loop.
- **On foot:** the seawall from Coal Harbour or the walk past Lost Lagoon from Denman Street, both easy from the West End.
- **By car:** Park Board pay parking lots inside the park, reached via Georgia Street and the Stanley Park Causeway.
- **Rideshare or taxi:** drop-offs work, though cars bunch up at the park entrance at peak entry times.

Vancouver's late-autumn rain is the main factor. Paths through the trees stay damp for days, and trainers that soak through make a long hour.

## Pacing yourself on the trail

Once you're on the route, nobody hurries you along. Couples and families usually stop at each installation for a few minutes, then move on when the next group comes up behind them.

On a busy night it works best to let the flow carry you between sections and then pause where the path widens. Phones come out everywhere, but people mostly keep their screens low so the person behind still gets a clear view. Flash photography mainly flattens the effects, so most visitors leave it off.

Children generally love it. Strollers that cope with paved and packed paths handle the route better than lightweight ones.

## Dressing for a Vancouver evening

From late October to December, Vancouver evenings are cool, wet and dark early. In December the sun sets well before 5pm, so even the first entry slots are fully night.

What regulars bring:

- A hooded waterproof jacket instead of an umbrella, which gets awkward in moving crowds
- Waterproof shoes with grip for damp, leaf-covered paths
- A warm layer underneath; standing still at installations cools you down quickly
- Gloves, which also make it easier to hold a phone in the cold

## Around the park before and after

Most people pair the walk with something nearby. Denman and Robson Streets in the West End are full of casual restaurants, from ramen to pubs, and they're a short walk from the Lost Lagoon side of the park.

If you come by daylight first, the Brockton Point totem poles, the seawall and the Vancouver Aquarium are all inside the park. Coal Harbour's waterfront path runs back toward Canada Place for a flat walk after dark, with the North Shore lights across the water.
