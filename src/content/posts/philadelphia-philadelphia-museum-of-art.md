## Why this building stops people on the Parkway

The museum occupies a Greek Revival temple of Minnesota dolomite, opened in 1928, with two flanking pavilions and a colonnaded east entrance. It anchors the far end of Benjamin Franklin Parkway, looking back down the Parkway toward City Hall.

Before you even buy a ticket, the building itself is doing work: the scale, the columns, the long approach up the steps are meant to make you feel small and a little reverent. That's on purpose.

## Climbing the steps everyone knows

The 72 steps up to the east entrance are the ones Sylvester Stallone ran in *Rocky*, and a bronze Rocky statue stands at the base for photos. People jog the steps, pose with arms raised, and queue for a turn even if they never set foot inside.

If you only have ten minutes, this is free and doesn't require a ticket. But the collection behind those doors is the real reason to come back.

- The statue and steps are outside the paid area, accessible anytime the plaza is open
- Best light for photos is morning, before the steps fill with tour groups
- The actual museum entrance and ticket desks are at the top, through the columns

## Inside: what actually fills the galleries

Beyond the entry hall, the museum holds around 240,000 objects spanning 2,000 years, arranged loosely by region and period across two floors plus the modern wing. The European collection is the anchor, with strong holdings of Renaissance and Baroque painting alongside a complete 12th-century French cloister reassembled indoors.

Look for the arms and armor hall, a real Japanese teahouse brought over piece by piece, and a Native American gallery that's been substantially reworked in recent years. The modern and contemporary wing, added in 2021, gives Cy Twombly, Marcel Duchamp, and a rotating slate of special exhibitions dedicated space that the old building never had.

Give yourself at least two hours to see highlights without rushing; a full circuit of everything is closer to four.

## Working around the opening hours

The museum keeps an unusual schedule that catches first-timers off guard.

- **Monday, Thursday, Saturday, Sunday:** 10am–5pm
- **Tuesday and Wednesday:** closed all day
- **Friday:** 10am–8:45pm, the only late evening

Because weekends run busy the entire 10am–5pm window, Friday evening after 5pm is the clearest stretch to see the galleries without crowding around the big paintings. A Monday or Thursday morning arrival, right at 10am, is the next best option before tour buses arrive.

## Getting there without the parking headache

The museum sits at the far northwestern end of the Parkway, a stretch not well served by subway. Most visitors take one of a few routes:

- **SEPTA bus 32 or 38** run along the Parkway and stop close to the front steps
- **Phlash purple trolley bus**, a seasonal tourist loop connecting Old City, Rittenhouse, and the museum
- **On foot or bike** from Center City, about a 25 to 35 minute walk or a flat 10 minute ride along the Schuylkill River Trail
- **Driving**, with a pay lot directly behind the museum and metered street parking along the Parkway, though weekend spots go fast

Given the sustained weekend crowds, arriving by trolley or bike on a Saturday will usually beat sitting in the lot's entrance line.

## Making the most of a single visit

Because this is one of the most-visited museums in the country, the smart moves are about sequencing, not secrecy.

Buy timed tickets online in advance, especially for weekend visits, since the ticket counter line can be the slowest part of the day. Start in the east entrance galleries and work counter to the main visitor flow, which tends to move left first.

Coat check and the audio guide desk are just past the main hall; both are worth the minute it takes, especially for the cloister and armor galleries where labels are sparse. If you're only there for a couple of hours, ask the desk staff for that day's "highlights route," since gallery closures for rotating exhibitions do happen.

Café and museum shop are on the ground floor near the west entrance, useful for a break without leaving the building entirely.
