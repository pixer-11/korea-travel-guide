## What's actually inside

The building started life as an orangery for citrus trees, part of the Tuileries palace complex. Monet donated the water lily murals himself, after the 1918 Armistice, on condition they hang in two purpose-built oval rooms with natural light from above.

Eight panels wrap around you in total, each several meters long, no frames breaking the surface. Below, the lower level holds the Walter-Guillaume collection: Cézanne, Renoir, Matisse, Picasso, Modigliani, Soutine. It's a small museum, but dense; most visits run 90 minutes to two hours.

## Layout and what to see first

Enter and you're funneled straight into the oval Monet rooms before the rest of the collection opens up below. Go there first while your legs are fresh; the murals reward slow circling, not a quick lap.

- Room 1: morning-toned panels, willows and reflections
- Room 2: darker, more abstract water surfaces, painted later
- Lower level: Walter-Guillaume collection, arranged by artist

The lower galleries are quieter and easy to rush. Give Soutine's portraits and the small Cézanne still lifes real time; they're often overlooked in the pull toward Monet.

## Timing it around the crowds

This is one of the most visited museums in Paris, and it does not feel empty at any hour. Weekends between 11am and 6pm are the peak stretch; that's when the oval rooms fill with tour groups and the circling gets slow.

Closed Tuesdays, so plan around that if you're stacking it with the Louvre or Musée d'Orsay across the river. On open days, hours run 9am to 6pm without exception across the week, including weekends.

Arriving near opening on a weekday is the surest way to get a few quiet minutes alone with the murals before groups arrive. Late afternoon, an hour or so before closing, is a reasonable second option.

## Buying tickets without the wait

Skip-the-line tickets are sold online in advance and are worth it here; the ticket queue outside can stretch well past the museum's actual capacity on busy afternoons. A combined ticket with the Musée d'Orsay exists and saves money if you're doing both in the same trip.

- Book online ahead, especially for weekends
- Print or screen ticket, no need to redeem in person first
- Bag checks at entry are standard; keep bags small
- Photography is allowed in the Monet rooms without flash

Nearest access is on foot from Place de la Concorde or through the Tuileries gardens; Concorde metro station (lines 1, 8, 12) is a short walk. There's no dedicated parking at the museum itself, so arrive by metro, bus, or on foot from a nearby hotel.
