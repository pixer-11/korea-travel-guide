Cross the Ponte Nuovo from central Verona and the streets of Veronetta feel almost residential, shutters and parked scooters rather than souvenir stalls. Giusti Garden sits behind a plain gate on Via Giardino Giusti, and nothing about the entrance prepares you for the fact that a Medici duke, a Habsburg emperor and Goethe all walked through it before you.

The garden has belonged to the Giusti family since the 16th century and still does, which is part of why it feels lived-in rather than curated for tourism. You enter through a Renaissance palazzo courtyard before the layout opens into the garden proper: a flat lower section of box hedges clipped into geometric knots, giving way to a wooded hill behind.

## The cypress avenue and the maze of hedges

Walk straight ahead from the entrance and you hit the garden's signature view: a dead-straight avenue of cypress trees, some reputedly planted in the 1500s, running toward the hillside. Dante is said to have admired these same trees, though take that claim as garden lore rather than documented fact.

Either side of the avenue, low box hedges form a parterre pattern you can only fully read from above. That's the trick of Giusti Garden: it's designed to be seen twice, once at ground level among the hedges and once from the terrace higher up, where the geometry suddenly makes sense.

## Climbing to the stone-faced grotto

Partway up the hill, a grotto is cut into the rock with a monstrous stone face set into its facade, mouth open in a permanent grimace. It's one of the garden's most photographed features, dating from the same 16th-century mannerist taste for grottoes and artificial ruins that shows up elsewhere in Italian Renaissance gardens.

Behind and above the grotto, gravel paths switchback up through cypress woods and past a few weathered statues. The going gets steeper here; there's no elevator or paved ramp, so wear shoes you don't mind scuffing on loose stone.

## The belvedere and the view over Verona's rooftops

At the top, a small tower and terrace, the belvedere, give a view over the terracotta roofs of Verona toward the Adige and the hills beyond. It's the payoff for the climb: on a clear day you can pick out the bell towers of the old city across the river.

This is also usually the quietest part of the garden, simply because fewer visitors bother with the full ascent. If you only have twenty minutes, skip it; if you have an hour, it's the section that makes the visit memorable.

## When to go if you want the paths to yourself

Giusti Garden is well established on Verona's day-trip circuit, close enough to the Arena and Piazza Bra that coach groups fold it into an afternoon itinerary. Crowd patterns here are consistent: the calmest window on both weekdays and weekends is 9am to 10am, right after opening.

Weekend afternoons, especially 12pm to 6pm, are the busiest stretch, with the lower parterre and grotto area filling up first since they're closest to the entrance. If you're visiting on a Saturday or Sunday, aim to arrive at opening or wait until after 6pm to have the cypress avenue mostly to yourself.

- Quietest: 9am–10am, any day
- Busiest: weekend afternoons, 12pm–6pm
- Open daily: 9:00 AM–7:00 PM

## Reading the garden like the Giusti family did

The layout isn't random; it follows a Renaissance logic where the flat, geometric lower garden represents order and civilisation, while the wilder, wooded upper hill represents untamed nature. Walking from the parterre up to the grotto and belvedere retraces that idea physically, moving from control to wildness to a commanding view over it all.

Knowing this before you go changes how you walk it. Instead of rushing the hedges to get to the "view," slow down in the lower garden first: the box-hedge patterns were meant to be admired as a set piece in their own right, not a warm-up act.

## Getting there and making the most of your visit

Giusti Garden sits on the eastern bank of the Adige, roughly a 15-20 minute walk from Piazza Bra and the Arena, crossing at Ponte Nuovo or Ponte Navi. There's no dedicated car park at the garden itself, so most visitors arrive on foot or by local bus from the centro storico.

Because this is a very popular stop on the Verona sightseeing circuit rather than an overlooked spot, don't expect solitude in the main parterre during midday. The behavioural fix is timing, not searching for a back entrance: come at opening, or treat it as a late-afternoon wind-down after the Arena and Piazza delle Erbe rather than a midday stop.

A few practical notes for the visit itself:

- Budget 60-90 minutes if you want to reach the belvedere
- Wear proper shoes for the gravel switchbacks on the hillside climb
- Bring water in summer; there's little shade once you're on the upper paths
- Photograph the cypress avenue from ground level near the entrance for the classic symmetrical shot

Check current admission prices before you go, since these are adjusted periodically and aren't worth guessing at here. The garden is an easy pairing with Sant'Anastasia or the Roman Theatre, both within walking distance back across the river, if you want to build a half-day loop around Veronetta and the old city.
