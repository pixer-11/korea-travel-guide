## A cottage standing on whale bones

Look down before you look up. Fyffe House rests partly on piles made from whale vertebrae, the leftovers of the trade that built Kaikōura. Some of those bones can still be seen beneath the house.

The cottage is small, timber and painted pink. It faces the bay at the town end of the peninsula. Behind it the Seaward Kaikōura Range rises steeply, so on a clear day you get mountains, sea and colonial carpentry in one frame.

## How a whaling station became a house

The story starts in 1842. Robert Fyffe set up the Waiopuka whaling station on this shore, and the oldest part of the house dates from that era. That makes it the oldest building still standing in Kaikōura.

His cousin George Fyffe later took over and enlarged the cottage. It went through generations of private hands before Heritage New Zealand Pouhere Taonga took on its care. The house was damaged in the 2016 Kaikōura earthquake and reopened after careful repair.

Inside, the scale is what stays with you. Low ceilings, narrow rooms and plain furnishings show how tight colonial coastal life was. Interpretation panels explain the whaling station and the families who lived here.

## Getting out to Avoca Street

The house sits at 62 Avoca Street, where the Esplanade becomes the road out toward Point Kean. From the town centre it's a flat, easy walk along the waterfront. You can also drive and park along the street.

Kaikōura itself is on State Highway 1, around two and a half hours' drive north of Christchurch. The Coastal Pacific train also stops in town on its scenic route between Christchurch and Picton, but check the timetable first because it doesn't run every day.

## Visiting a house on whale bones

Fyffe House is a heritage building, not a big gallery. Visiting takes a little patience.

- **Timing:** It currently opens Wednesday to Sunday, 10am–5pm, and is closed Monday and Tuesday. Hours can change with the season and around holidays, so check the Heritage New Zealand website for this month's times.
- **Avoiding the crowds:** Weekends are busiest for the whole 10am–5pm opening. Come Wednesday to Friday if you want the small rooms mostly to yourself.
- **Admission:** Prices are posted at the entrance and on the Heritage New Zealand site.
- **Pace:** An unhurried visit takes 45 minutes to an hour. Allow longer if you read every panel and walk round the garden.
- **Etiquette:** The floors and fittings are old. Move slowly, keep bags close in the narrow rooms, and ask staff before touching anything.

Staff know the house's story well. Ask them where to look for the whalebone foundations, which are easy to miss.

## Making a morning of the peninsula

The house works well as the first stop on a slow loop around the peninsula. Keep going along the road and you reach Point Kean. New Zealand fur seals often haul out on the rocks there. Keep at least 20 metres away from them and never get between a seal and the sea.

From Point Kean, the Kaikōura Peninsula Walkway climbs onto the clifftops. The track has wide views of the ocean and the ranges, and you can walk as much or as little of it as you like. Check the tide before heading onto the shoreline section.

Back in town, Whale Watch Kaikōura runs from the old railway station. Seeing the whaling-era cottage first changes how a modern whale-watching trip feels.

## Who will get the most from it

This is one of Kaikōura's quieter stops. Most visitors come for the whales and seals and drive straight past the pink cottage on Avoca Street. If you want to understand why the town exists at all, this is the clearest place to start.

It suits history readers, architecture enthusiasts and anyone who has a slack hour between a boat trip and the drive on. Children usually find the bone foundations more memorable than any panel.
