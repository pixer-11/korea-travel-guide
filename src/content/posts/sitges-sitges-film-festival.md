## Eleven days of horror by the sea

Sitges bills itself as the world's leading fantastic film festival. Its full name is the Festival Internacional de Cinema Fantàstic de Catalunya, and it has run since 1968. The 2026 edition was set for eleven days, October 8-18, with horror, fantasy, science fiction and cult cinema filling a whitewashed beach town that is quiet the rest of autumn.

The festival moves between two worlds. There are the polished premieres at the hotel auditorium on the edge of town. There are also the old-town lanes, where fans in genre T-shirts queue outside small cinemas. Between screenings, the seafront promenade (the Passeig Marítim) and the church of Sant Bartomeu i Santa Tecla on its rocky point are a few minutes' walk away.

## Where the screens are

The festival spreads across a handful of venues. These are the ones that keep returning:

- **Auditori, Hotel Melià Sitges**: the flagship hall, home to the big premieres and red-carpet moments. It sits west of the centre, a 20-minute walk from the station.
- **Cinema Retiro**: a historic cinema in the town centre.
- **Cinema Prado**: another central screen, close to the old-town bars.

The festival's official site is sitgesfilmfestival.com. Programmes and the venue list can change between editions.

## Getting to Sitges

The train is the easiest way in. Rodalies line R2 Sud runs from Barcelona Sants and Passeig de Gràcia to Sitges station in roughly 35-45 minutes. Trains run often through the day, but midnight sessions can end after the last return service has left.

From Barcelona-El Prat airport, the drive down the C-32 motorway is short. Some R2 Sud trains also stop at El Prat. Staying in Sitges itself avoids the late-night commute, and festival dates usually fill the town's hotels fast.

## Working the festival like a regular

Regulars tend to follow a few habits:

- **Screenings go early.** Premieres at the Auditori usually sell out first, so seasoned festivalgoers secure the films they care about before anything else.
- **The programme rewards close reading.** Many films are in their original language with subtitles, and the subtitle language differs between sessions.
- **Venues are farther apart than they look.** Walking from the Auditori to the old-town cinemas takes longer than the map suggests, so back-to-back sessions across town are hard to make.
- **The Zombie Walk.** This costumed parade through the streets is a long-running festival tradition.

Local life runs late, which suits a film schedule. Restaurants serve dinner from around 9pm, so a 7pm screening still leaves time to eat afterwards. Cards are accepted almost everywhere. Tipping is modest: rounding up or leaving small change is normal, and nobody expects a percentage.

People here speak Catalan as well as Spanish. A "bon dia" (good morning) or "gràcies" (thank you) at the box office goes down well.
