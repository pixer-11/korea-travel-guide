## A courtyard turned yellow

Jogyesa sits right up against city traffic. One step off Ujeongguk-ro and you're in a stone courtyard. Ahead is Daeungjeon, the main hall, with its heavy tiled roof and eaves painted in the green, red and blue *dancheong* patterns of Korean temples.

For the festival, that courtyard fills with potted chrysanthemums. They are mostly yellow and white, stacked in tiers and banked against the hall's stone base. Some are trained into larger shapes that people walk around and photograph.

This is a working temple. Jogyesa is the head temple of the Jogye Order, the largest school of Korean Buddhism. While you are looking at flowers, monks and lay worshippers are going in and out of the hall to pray. That mix of chanting, incense and visitors with phones is the whole point of coming.

## Dates for the 16th edition

The 2026 festival is the 16th. It opens on October 13 and runs into November.

That timing lines up with Seoul's autumn. Ginkgo trees along Jongno's streets usually turn gold from late October, so the later weeks bring yellow leaves outside the gate to match the flowers inside. Earlier in the run, more of the blooms are still opening.

The festival is held every year at the same temple, so its location doesn't change. The temple runs its own website, and that is where its event notices appear.

## Tickets and getting in

There is no ticket. Jogyesa's grounds open straight onto the street, and the chrysanthemums are set out in that open space. Visitors simply walk in through the gate.

Inside, money is about giving rather than paying. Donation boxes stand near the halls. Next to the courtyard, a row of Buddhist supply shops sells prayer beads, incense and lanterns. Most visitors just look around and leave nothing, and nobody minds.

## Getting to Jogyesa

The temple's address is 55 Ujeongguk-ro, Jongno-gu. Two subway stations are within an easy walk:

- **Anguk Station (Line 3), Exit 6:** walk south along Ujeongguk-ro for about five minutes. The temple is on your right.
- **Jonggak Station (Line 1), Exit 2:** walk north for about seven minutes, past the Bosingak bell pavilion area.
- **From Insadong:** the north end of Insadong-gil is just across the road, a couple of minutes on foot.

Next to the temple stands Ujeongchongguk, a small building that housed Korea's first post office in the 1880s. It's a handy landmark if you come out of the subway facing the wrong way.

## Under the old white pine

The trees are the oldest things in the courtyard, older than any of the halls. Near Daeungjeon stands a white pine, a protected natural monument. It is believed to be around 500 years old, and its pale, mottled bark makes it look almost chalk-white.

A Chinese scholar tree spreads over the courtyard as well. It is thought to be about 450 years old. In the festival weeks, flowers are banked around both trunks. Their dark, twisting branches above all that yellow make one of the better photographs in the grounds.

The hall itself was built in 1938, which is young for a temple this important. Take a minute to look up at the carved panels around its doors. The painted latticework shows scenes from Buddhist stories, and most visitors are too busy photographing flowers to notice.

## Temple manners in a festival crowd

Daeungjeon stays an active prayer hall throughout the festival. A few habits keep you from getting in the way:

- **Shoes off** before stepping into any hall. Leave them on the racks outside.
- **Use the side doors.** The central door of the main hall is traditionally kept for senior monks.
- **No photos of people praying,** and no flash inside. A quick shot from the doorway of the three large Buddha statues is generally tolerated.
- **Keep your voice low** near the hall, even when the courtyard outside is noisy.
- **Leave the pots alone.** Visitors who step between displays for a photo tend to get a firm look from temple staff.

Worshippers may be doing full prostrations inside. Visitors are welcome to sit quietly at the back on a cushion, and many do.

On weekends the temple fills with the Insadong shopping crowd, especially in the afternoon. That follows from where it is, beside a major shopping street. Weekday mornings, when the area is mostly office workers on their way in, give the flowers more breathing room. In the evening the main hall glows from inside and the courtyard thins out.

## Making a half-day of it

The temple takes about an hour, including the flowers, the hall and the trees. With the neighbourhood around it, most people make it a three-hour outing.

- **Insadong-gil:** tea houses, traditional craft shops and the spiral Ssamziegil arcade, directly across the road.
- **Templestay Information Center:** opposite the temple, with information on overnight stays at temples around Korea.
- **Tapgol Park:** a few minutes south, site of the 1919 reading of the March 1st Declaration of Independence.
- **Bukchon Hanok Village:** north of Anguk Station, with streets of tiled-roof houses climbing the hill.
- **Gyeongbokgung Palace:** around 15 minutes' walk northwest, with its own autumn colour in October and November.

Insadong's side lanes also hold vegetarian temple-food restaurants. Their dishes skip garlic and onions in the Buddhist tradition, so you can carry the theme through to lunch.
