## Ten thousand voices that know every word

A Max Pezzali crowd shows up knowing every song already. People who were teenagers in the 1990s bring their own teenagers. The adults usually know more of the lyrics than the kids do. On January 4, 2027, that crowd fills the Unipol Dome, Milan's newest arena, for a night that works more like a group sing than a concert.

The date falls on a Monday. It also falls in the last days of the Italian Christmas holidays, and that matters more than you'd expect. More on that below.

## Why Italy sings along

Pezzali grew up in Pavia, about half an hour south of Milan by train. He became famous in the early 1990s as the voice of 883. The band wrote small-town songs about friendship, scooters, bad crushes and Saturday nights with nothing to do. Their language was plain, and a whole generation adopted it.

These are the songs the arena tends to sing loudest:

- **"Hanno ucciso l'Uomo Ragno"**, the 1992 breakthrough
- **"Nord sud ovest est"**
- **"Come mai"**
- **"Gli anni"**, the nostalgic one, often saved for late in the set
- **"La regola dell'amico"**
- **"Sei un mito"** and **"Tieni il tempo"**

Since 2022 he has played some of the biggest venues in the country, including San Siro and Rome's Circo Massimo. An indoor arena show feels tighter by comparison. The whole room sings together.

## The Unipol Dome in Santa Giulia

The arena was built as the ice hockey venue for the Milano Cortina 2026 Winter Olympics. Before Unipol took the naming rights, it was known as Arena Santa Giulia. The design is by David Chipperfield Architects: a smooth oval shell set into the redevelopment zone between Rogoredo and the Santa Giulia housing blocks.

From the outside it looks plain on purpose, a pale curved wall with no decoration. Inside it seats around 16,000. The bowl is steep, so the upper rings sit closer to the stage than they would in an older palasport. The building is new, so you won't find any of the worn concrete corridors that older Italian arenas are known for.

## Getting to Rogoredo

Nearly everyone arrives through **Milano Rogoredo**, and this station is the venue's biggest advantage.

- **Metro:** M3, the yellow line, to Rogoredo FS. That's a straight ride from Duomo, Centrale and Repubblica.
- **Suburban trains:** S-lines including the S13 to Pavia. That's handy, given where Pezzali comes from.
- **High-speed trains:** some Frecciarossa and Italo services stop at Rogoredo. Fans coming from Bologna, Turin or Rome often skip Centrale altogether.

From the station it's a short walk through the new streets of Santa Giulia to the arena. After the show, the crowd heads back toward Rogoredo in one slow mass. Many fans hang back for a drink so they miss the first rush onto the M3 platforms. Parking around a sold-out arena in a district still being built is limited, which is why so many Milanese leave the car at home.

## The Epiphany-week quirk

The date sits two days before the Epiphany. In Italy, January 6 (Befana) is a public holiday, and many schools and offices stay closed until it passes. So this Monday doesn't behave like a normal work night. Plenty of families are still on holiday, and that's part of why so many parents bring their kids.

The timing also shapes travel. Fans from Lombardy and beyond often turn the show into a short holiday-week trip. Trains into Milan can be busier than on an ordinary January Monday. It's also midwinter: Milan is usually cold and damp at this time of year, and fog is common in the Po valley. Most people bring a coat they don't mind carrying around inside the arena.

## Inside the singalong: how this crowd behaves

The etiquette of an Italian pop arena is simple. The night belongs to the audience as much as to the performer.

- **Singing is expected.** If Pezzali drops the mic out for a chorus, the arena takes it over. Staying quiet is the only real faux pas.
- **Named tickets.** Big Italian concerts have often used *biglietti nominativi* (tickets in the buyer's name). That's why regulars bring photo ID along with the ticket.
- **Buying.** Authorised sellers such as TicketOne handle most large Italian arena shows. Fans who buy on unofficial resale sites are the ones who end up stuck at the gate.
- **Phones.** Expect a sea of phone torches during "Gli anni" and the slower songs. Locals join in.
- **Ordering at the bar.** A beer is *una birra* and water is *un'acqua naturale*. A quick *grazie* goes a long way with arena staff. Tipping isn't expected at concession counters, and card payment is standard in new Milan venues.

Fans usually take their seats well before the lights go down. The pre-show singalong to the house playlist is part of the night.

## Before and after around Santa Giulia

Santa Giulia is a new neighbourhood, not a historic one. Few people come here for dinner unless there's an arena show. Most fans eat somewhere else first and ride the M3 down. Porta Romana and the streets around Corso Lodi are two stops away and offer plenty of trattorias and pizzerias. For an aperitivo, many people stay in the centre and leave enough time for the metro.

If you're making a weekend of it, the things to see are in central Milan, a straight M3 ride north:

- The **Duomo** and its rooftop terraces
- The **Galleria Vittorio Emanuele II** next door
- The **Navigli** canals for an evening drink

Late on concert night, the M3 runs packed with people still singing the encore, and the ride back north is where many fans finish the night.
