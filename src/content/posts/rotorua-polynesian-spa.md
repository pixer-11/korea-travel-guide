## Where the street runs out at the lake

Hinemoa Street ends at the water. Walk east from the city grid and the road stops at Lake Rotorua's shoreline. Polynesian Spa stands there, between the town and the lake, with Government Gardens on one side.

The air carries the eggy tang of hydrogen sulphide. That smell is everywhere in Rotorua, and it gets stronger here. Steam lifts off the pools. Past the rock edges you look across the lake towards Mokoia Island, the island in the legend that gives this street its name.

That legend is short enough to know before you soak. Hinemoa swam across the lake from the shore to reach Tūtānekai on Mokoia, guided by the sound of his flute. Tūtānekai Street, the town's restaurant strip, is a few blocks away.

## Priest and Rachel: two springs, two waters

The spa is built around two different geothermal waters. Most visitors never notice the difference, and it is the most interesting thing about the place.

- **Priest Spring** is acidic. The usual story is that a Catholic priest, Father Mahoney, bathed here in the 1870s and said it eased his arthritis. The spring was named after him.
- **Rachel Spring** is alkaline. People describe the water as silky on the skin, and it has long been sold as the "beauty" water.

The pools are labelled by source and temperature. Work your way from cooler to hotter rather than going straight into the hottest one. Moving between the acidic and alkaline pools is the closest you will get to tasting the difference.

## Picking a pool

There is more than one way in, and the zone you choose shapes your visit. The site groups its bathing into separate areas, each with its own entry:

- **Family area:** where children are allowed. It is the liveliest part of the complex.
- **Adult-only pools:** quieter, and the zone to pick if the lake view is the point.
- **Private pools:** small pools booked for a set block of time, good for couples or anyone who would rather not share.
- **Therapies:** massages and treatments, booked separately from bathing.

Entry types and current prices are on the official Polynesian Spa website and at the front desk. Check there before you go, because the options change. If you are torn, the adult-only lake pools are the experience most visitors come for. Families get more out of the family area.

## Reading the steam clock

Crowds follow a pattern, and on a busy weekend the pools fill fast. Measured foot traffic puts the quietest weekday stretch at 9am–1pm. On weekends the calm window is shorter, 9am–11am. Every day, 10pm–11pm is quiet too.

Try hard not to arrive between 3pm and 8pm on a weekend. That is the peak. Changing rooms get busy and the lake-facing pools fill up.

The late window is the one regulars rate. The pools currently stay open until 11pm. A soak from 10pm, with steam lit up against the dark lake, is a very different place from mid-afternoon. Opening hours can shift on holidays, so confirm on the official site that evening.

## Getting to Hinemoa Street

Rotorua is compact, and the spa is walkable from almost anywhere in the centre. From the Tūtānekai Street restaurant area, head east along Hinemoa Street until it meets the lake. That takes around ten minutes on foot.

Drivers can reach the spa in a few minutes from the city's main roads. Rotorua lies roughly three hours by road from Auckland, so day-trippers often fit a soak in before the drive back. Allow at least two hours for a proper bathing visit. Add more if you have booked a treatment or plan to eat at the café.

## Before you step into the sulphur

Geothermal water has its own rules. A few habits will save you money and protect your belongings.

- **Leave the silver at home.** Sulphur water tarnishes silver and some other metals, often within minutes. Take off rings, chains and earrings before you get in.
- **Keep your head above the water.** In New Zealand, standard advice for natural geothermal pools is not to put your face under or submerge your head. Locals follow it without thinking.
- **Shower first.** Rinsing before you get in is expected in shared pools here.
- **Book the extras.** Private pools and therapies run on time slots, and weekend slots go first. Reserve on the official site, especially if you want a weekend afternoon.
- **Drink water between soaks.** Hot mineral pools dry you out faster than you expect. Short dips with breaks beat one long cook.
- **Bring old swimwear.** The smell can linger in fabric after a sulphur soak.

Payment is by card, as almost everywhere in New Zealand. Tipping is not expected in New Zealand, so you can skip it unless a therapist has gone beyond the usual.

## After the soak: the lakefront walk

You come out right next to Government Gardens. Their lawns and Tudor-style landmarks are the first thing outside the door. The half-timbered building at their centre is the old Bath House, which held Rotorua Museum. It has been closed for earthquake strengthening, so admire it from outside unless the official museum site says it has reopened.

Walk along the lake edge and you pass the steaming ground around Sulphur Bay. The white, mineral-crusted shore shows what feeds the pools you just left.

For dinner, head back up Hinemoa Street to Eat Streat on Tūtānekai Street, the covered restaurant block. Or walk north along the shore to Ōhinemutu. This Māori village sits beside the lake and has St Faith's Church, whose memorial window looks out towards the water. Ōhinemutu is a living community, so walk through quietly and respect any signs asking visitors to keep out.

If you only have one evening in Rotorua, this order works: a walk round the gardens, dinner on Tūtānekai Street, then a late soak once the pools go quiet after 10pm.
