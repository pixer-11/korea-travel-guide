## A palace you reach on foot

The road ends at a gate, and the rest is uphill. A long flight of steps climbs from Hill Palace Rd in Irumpanam to a cluster of whitewashed buildings under steep, tiled Kerala roofs. Trees close in on both sides as you go up. It is a short climb, but it reminds you this was built to stand above the town.

The Rajas of Cochin built the complex in 1865 and ran their administration from here. It is huge: dozens of separate buildings spread over a wide estate, joined by verandas and courtyards. In 1986 the Kerala State Department of Archaeology turned it into a museum. It remains the largest of its kind in the state.

It is not a secret. This is one of the most-reviewed sights around Kochi, and families come out from Ernakulam in large numbers at weekends. Plan around them and you get a much calmer visit.

## What the galleries hold

The museum is spread through the palace rooms, not set out in one modern hall. You walk from building to building over polished floors and under wooden ceilings, and each wing holds a different collection. Expect:

- Royal belongings of the Cochin family, including ceremonial objects and furniture
- Oil paintings and copies of Kerala mural art
- Stone and bronze sculpture
- Palm-leaf manuscripts and old inscriptions
- Coins, weapons and other archaeological finds from the region

The palace itself is worth as much of your attention as the display cases. The low eaves, carved timber and open inner courtyards follow traditional Kerala building styles. They are designed to keep the rooms shaded and cool. Look up at the ceilings as you pass through, and look out through the windows across the grounds.

## The grounds below the galleries

The galleries are only part of the estate. Outside them, the sloping land has been made into landscaped gardens and parks. A deer park is the part children head for first. Paths wind under big shade trees, with benches along the way, and the area feels more like a city park than a museum courtyard.

The grounds need time of their own. Give the galleries around 90 minutes, then add another hour if you want to walk the gardens without rushing. On a warm afternoon, the shaded paths make a useful break between buildings.

## Working around the lunch-hour lock

The timing here catches people out more than anything else. At the moment the palace runs in two sessions: 9:00 AM to 12:30 PM, then 2:00 to 4:30 PM, Tuesday to Sunday. It is currently closed on Mondays. If you arrive at 12:15, you will be heading back down the steps soon after reaching the top.

The simplest plan is to arrive at 9:00 AM and use the whole morning session. You could also come at 2:00 PM and stay until closing. Don't try to fit both sessions around a lunch elsewhere unless you are staying nearby.

Museum hours in Kerala change with the season and for public holidays. Check the Kerala archaeology department's site or call ahead before you travel out.

## Getting out to Thrippunithura

Hill Palace is in Thrippunithura, on the southeastern side of greater Kochi, roughly 10–12 km from central Ernakulam. You have a few options:

- **Auto-rickshaw or taxi** from Ernakulam is the most direct route and drops you at the gate.
- **Train** to Thrippunithura railway station on the Ernakulam–Kottayam line, then a short auto ride to the palace.
- **City buses** run regularly from Ernakulam to Thrippunithura town, and autos cover the last stretch.

If you are staying in Fort Kochi, allow plenty of time. The drive across the city is long and traffic is unpredictable, so make it a half-day trip, not a quick stop.

## Beating the weekend rush up the hill

Crowd data for Hill Palace points to one clear problem: weekends. On Saturdays and Sundays it is busiest from 9am–12pm and 2pm–4pm, which covers almost every hour it is open. Galleries then fill with school groups and families, and the ticket counter at the gate gets a queue.

Go Tuesday to Friday if you can. If a weekend is your only option, the half hour before each session closes (12pm–12:30pm and 4pm–4:30pm) falls outside the busy windows. It is a tight window, though, so pick one area and see it properly.

A few habits make the visit easier:

- Ticket prices are posted at the counter by the gate. Camera fees are charged separately at most Kerala state museums, so read the board before you pay.
- Photography inside the galleries is usually restricted. Look for the signs, and if you're unsure, ask the attendant posted in each room.
- Speak quietly inside. Staff watch the rooms closely, and visitors who touch display cases or lean on the old woodwork get told off.
- Carry water for the climb and the gardens, and cover your shoulders and knees. Thrippunithura is a temple town, and people generally dress modestly.

## Staying on in the old royal town

Thrippunithura grew up around the Cochin court, and the town still feels like it. The Sree Poornathrayeesa Temple, the old royal family temple, sits in the town centre a short auto ride from the palace. Its annual Vrischikolsavam festival in November–December brings elephant processions and traditional percussion. Non-Hindus are generally not allowed inside Kerala's temple sanctums, so plan to watch from outside.

If you go in the morning session, you can be back in Ernakulam by early afternoon. That leaves the rest of the day for Fort Kochi and Mattancherry, which round out the Cochin royal story at the Dutch Palace.
