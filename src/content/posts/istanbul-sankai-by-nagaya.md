## Why Sankai stands out

Bebek is one of those Istanbul neighbourhoods that feels like it's exhaling — a curve of the Bosphorus lined with tea gardens, yachts nosing into the marina, and apartment blocks that have quietly held some of the city's wealthiest addresses for decades. Sankai by Nagaya sits inside this hush, inside the Bebek Hotel by The Stay, and it deliberately turns its back on the waterfront spectacle outside. Where so much of Istanbul's newer dining scene chases the view, Sankai chases silence and precision instead. The name and concept trace to chef Yoshizumi Nagaya, a Michelin-starred figure whose reputation rests on the kind of omakase where every gesture — the angle of a knife, the second a piece of fish leaves the counter — is considered in advance. That's the draw here: not a skyline, but a craft.

## What the experience is actually like

Omakase means "I leave it up to you," and that's the contract at a counter like this — you sit, you trust, and the chef sets the pace. Expect a small room, a handful of seats arranged around a counter rather than a sprawling dining hall, and a rhythm built around courses rather than a printed menu you order from. This is not the place for a quick bite between Bosphorus photos; it's built for unhurried attention, the kind where conversation drops to a murmur and the loudest sound is the soft clatter of chopsticks or the chef's knife meeting the board. Because neither exact pricing nor a fixed course list has been locked in publicly at this stage, treat any number you hear elsewhere as provisional — call or check with the Bebek Hotel directly for what's currently being served and what it costs.

## Getting there

Bebek sits on the European side of the Bosphorus, north of Ortaköy and south of Rumeli Hisarı, and it's best reached by taxi, ride-hail, or the Bosphorus-side bus routes that run along Cevdet Paşa Caddesi — traffic along this stretch can be slow at peak times, so build in extra minutes. If you're coming from the historic peninsula or Beyoğlu, allow at least 30–40 minutes by road depending on traffic. A ferry-and-walk combination is also possible if you're already near a Bosphorus pier, though there's no direct ferry stop at Bebek itself, so you'll still finish on foot or by short taxi. The hotel entrance is easy to spot on the main coastal road, with the restaurant set discreetly inside rather than facing the street.

## Setting and atmosphere

What makes Bebek worth the detour even outside the meal is the contrast it offers to the rest of Istanbul's dining circuit. Ortaköy and the piers near Karaköy trade on spectacle — bridge lights, ferry horns, crowds three-deep at the simit stalls. Bebek, and Sankai within it, trades on restraint. The hotel itself leans quiet and residential-feeling, the kind of place where the Bosphorus is glimpsed rather than performed. Inside, expect the visual language of a serious omakase room: warm wood, muted lighting, a counter positioned so you can watch the kitchen work rather than being seated with your back to it. It's a small-scale, sensory experience built on close proximity to the chef, not a sprawling banquet room.

## How to visit like a local

Book ahead — a counter-style omakase room like this typically runs limited seatings per service, and turning up without a reservation is the single most common mistake visitors make with concept restaurants of this kind. Contact the Bebek Hotel directly to confirm the current reservation process, service times, and any dress expectations, since these details evolve as a new concept beds in. Arrive on time rather than early or late; omakase service is paced by the chef, and latecomers disrupt the rhythm for everyone at the counter. Dress leans smart-casual to elevated — Bebek's crowd skews affluent and understated rather than flashy. Payment is standard card practice in Istanbul's upscale dining scene, though it's worth asking the hotel about service charges and whether tipping is already included. If you want the neighbourhood without the tasting-menu commitment, walk the Bebek waterfront before or after — the tea gardens and park benches facing the water are where the area's actual daily rhythm plays out, away from any tourist crush.
