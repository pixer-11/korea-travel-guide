## Two colonial houses, one long walk through time

From Calle 2 Sur, Museo Amparo looks like the rest of the street. You see a colonial façade, a heavy doorway and a few stone steps. Inside, the old courtyards open into crisp white galleries joined by glass and steel. These came from a renovation led by architect Enrique Norten's firm, finished in 2013.

The museum opened in 1991. Banker and philanthropist Manuel Espinosa Yglesias founded it in memory of his wife, Amparo Rugarcía de Espinosa. That name is the one on the door. The collection is large, with over 3,500 pieces, and it is laid out so you can walk it from beginning to end.

## What hangs on the walls

The pre-Columbian galleries are what most visitors come for. They are arranged by theme rather than by culture, so pieces from different peoples sit side by side. Things to look for:

- Olmec and Maya stone carving, small and large
- West Mexican ceramic figures, many of them dogs and human figures
- Clay vessels and masks from across Mesoamerica

Upstairs the subject changes to the viceregal period and the 19th century. You'll find religious oil paintings, gilded sculpture and furniture from the centuries when Puebla was one of New Spain's richest cities. The contemporary galleries host changing exhibitions. Check what is showing before you go, because some temporary shows are reason enough to make the trip.

Plan on two to three hours if you want to see all three sections without rushing. Guided tours run here too. The front desk can tell you which languages are offered that day.

## Up on the roof

Take the stairs or lift to the top floor before you leave. The museum's rooftop terrace looks north over Centro, and the towers and tiled domes of Puebla Cathedral fill much of the view. A café works up there. Sitting with a coffee and watching the city's church domes is one of the easiest pleasures in Puebla.

## Timing your visit around the crowds

This is one of the city's most visited museums, and it shows on weekends. Try not to arrive between 12pm and 6pm on Saturday or Sunday. Those are its busiest hours, and the pre-Columbian rooms are the ones that fill first.

Weekend visitors do best walking in right at 10am, which gives you a couple of hours before that window starts. The schedule has one trap. Many Mexican museums close on Monday, but Museo Amparo is open Monday from 10am to 6pm and **closed on Tuesday**. That makes Monday a useful day, when other museums in the city are shut.

## Getting in and moving through

Getting in is straightforward, and a few habits make the visit smoother:

- **Admission:** policies on charges and free days change, so check the official Museo Amparo website or the ticket desk at the entrance before you go. Don't rely on an old blog post.
- **Bags:** expect to check larger bags and backpacks at the cloakroom near the entrance, as most Mexican museums ask.
- **Photos:** photography without flash is generally fine in the permanent galleries. Temporary shows sometimes set their own rules, and signs at the gallery door will say so.
- **Language:** labels are in Spanish and English in the main galleries. If you ask for a guided visit, "visita guiada" is the phrase the staff will recognise.
- **Route:** start at the bottom with the pre-Columbian galleries while you're fresh, then work upward and finish on the terrace.

## Around the block

The museum is an easy anchor for a morning in Centro. Walk two blocks north on Calle 2 Sur and you reach the Zócalo and the cathedral you saw from the roof. Next to the cathedral is the Biblioteca Palafoxiana, often called the oldest public library in the Americas.

Head a few blocks east instead and you reach the Callejón de los Sapos and the antique stalls around Calle 6 Sur. North of the Zócalo, the Capilla del Rosario inside the Templo de Santo Domingo is a dense wall of gilded plaster. It pairs well with the viceregal rooms you've just walked through.

If you're coming from Mexico City, buses run to Puebla's CAPU terminal in roughly two hours. From the terminal, a taxi or ride-hail to Centro is the simplest way to finish the trip.
