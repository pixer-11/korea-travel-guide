## Three days of clocks being slapped

Fast chess is loud in a way classical chess never is. You hear the clocks before anything else. In rapid, players have time to think between flurries. In blitz, the hand hits the clock almost before the piece settles, and a whole hall of boards turns into a steady clatter.

The National Rapid and Blitz Chess Championship (Finale) runs from December 12 to 14, 2026, in Chennai. It is the All India Chess Federation's national title event for the two quicker formats. "Finale" in the name marks it as the deciding stage, so the rounds you watch here settle who holds the national rapid and blitz titles.

## Why Chennai is the natural host

Chennai is the city most closely tied to Indian chess. Viswanathan Anand, India's first grandmaster and a five-time world champion, grew up here. So did a later generation that includes R. Praggnanandhaa and D. Gukesh, who became world champion in 2024.

The city has hosted the game at the highest level before. Anand defended his world title against Magnus Carlsen in Chennai in 2013. In 2022, the 44th Chess Olympiad was played at Mamallapuram, about an hour down the coast. Coaching academies across the city keep the pipeline of juniors full, and that local depth shows in who comes to watch: parents, coaches and kids who play themselves.

## Rapid versus blitz: what you are actually watching

The two formats feel like different sports. Knowing which one is on changes where you look.

- **Rapid:** each player gets a few tens of minutes for the game, with a small increment per move. Openings are deep and recognisable, and the drama usually comes late, when one player's clock runs low.
- **Blitz:** each side gets only a few minutes. Games last about as long as a cup of filter coffee. Blunders, swindles and flag falls decide plenty of results.
- **The scoresheet difference:** in rapid you often see players notating; in blitz, nobody has time.

Rapid days tend to suit newcomers, because you can follow a single game from start to finish. Blitz rewards watching several boards at once and catching the endings.

## Watching from the rail

Chess halls run on quiet, and the etiquette is stricter than first-time visitors tend to expect. Spectators are usually kept behind a rope or barrier, a few metres from the top boards. What regulars do:

- **Phones silent and out of sight.** Electronic devices near the playing area are a serious anti-cheating issue at rated events, and arbiters act on them.
- **No talking near the boards.** Analysis happens outside the hall, in corridors or at the tea counter.
- **No pointing or reacting.** A gasp at a blunder can disturb a player mid-calculation.
- **Watch the screens.** Where live boards are projected, most spectators follow the top games there and walk the rows quietly for the rest.

The best moments often come between rounds. Players step out, juniors cluster around familiar names for a photo, and coaches replay key positions on pocket sets. Approaching a player is usually fine once their game is over, never while their clock is running.

## Evenings in the December Music Season

Mid-December in Chennai belongs to Carnatic music. The December Music Season builds through the month in the sabhas (concert halls) of Mylapore, T. Nagar and Alwarpet. Afternoon and evening concerts follow one another back to back, so a day of chess can end with a recital.

The sabha canteens are a ritual of their own. Visitors queue for South Indian tiffin served on banana leaves or steel plates: dosai, pongal, vadai and filter coffee poured between tumbler and dabarah. Many fans of the season come as much for the canteens as for the concerts.

Around Mylapore, the Kapaleeshwarar Temple and its surrounding streets of flower sellers and brass shops make an easy walk between sessions. Marina Beach is a short ride east.

## Getting around Chennai in mid-December

December is among the more comfortable months in Chennai. The heat eases, though the northeast monsoon can still bring heavy showers in the first half of the month. Visitors usually carry a compact umbrella and allow extra time on the roads after rain.

Chennai International Airport sits in the city's south, and the Chennai Metro links it to the centre on the Blue Line. Beyond the metro, app-based cabs and auto-rickshaws cover most journeys; agreeing the fare or using the meter app before an auto sets off is standard practice.

Where to stay depends on the venue the AICF lists. Visitors who want the music-season evenings tend to base themselves around Mylapore or Alwarpet. Those who also want the coast, or a day trip to the Shore Temple at Mamallapuram, often choose the southern side of the city along East Coast Road.
