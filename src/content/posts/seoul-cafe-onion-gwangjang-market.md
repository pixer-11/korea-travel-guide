Gwangjang Market has sold silk, hanbok fabric and bindaetteok since 1905, and it's the last place you'd expect to find a café built out of raw poured concrete. That contrast is the whole point of Café Onion's newest branch.

## What makes this branch different

Onion already runs outlets in Seongsu, Anguk and Cheongdam, each known for turning industrial spaces into minimalist coffee halls. This fourth branch drops that same aesthetic straight into a century-old traditional market.

The design is open-air, with exposed concrete walls, ceilings and furniture standing in deliberate contrast to the market's tin roofs, hanging fabric and packed food stalls around it. You get shelter and a place to sit, but the boundary between café and market stays porous. Market noise, smells from the food alleys and the concrete hush of the café all sit in the same room.

## Finding it inside the market

Gwangjang Market sits in Jongno-gu, on the north bank of the Cheonggyecheon stream, and is best reached via Jongno 5(o)-ga Station on Line 1, exit 7 or 8, an easy few minutes' walk.

The market itself is a warren of narrow lanes organized loosely by trade: fabric on one side, the famous food alleys with bindaetteok and mayak gimbap stalls on another. Café Onion doesn't have a street-facing storefront in the usual sense; it's built into the market fabric itself, so the approach is part of the experience.

- Enter from any of the market's main gates and follow signage or ask a stallholder; "Onion" is a recognizable name locally now.
- Expect to walk past food vendors and fabric stalls right up to the café's edge.
- The concrete structure is visually distinct enough that it stands out once you're in the right lane.

## Why it's worth the detour

This isn't a quiet, secluded find. Onion is a well-known chain in Seoul, and pairing its signature look with one of the city's most visited traditional markets was always going to draw attention. Expect a mix of market regulars, coffee-chain fans and visitors doing both in one stop.

What makes it worth seeking out isn't solitude, it's the friction: a design language associated with quiet, photogenic neighborhoods like Seongsu, transplanted into a working market that still smells like frying batter and cut fabric. Few places in Seoul let you sit with a coffee while a century-old market keeps operating at full volume around you.

## Timing your visit around the market

Gwangjang Market's food alleys get heaviest around traditional lunch and dinner hours, when both tourists and office workers converge on the bindaetteok stalls. A café built into that same market will likely feel that overflow too.

Arriving in the late morning, before lunch service peaks, is a reasonable way to beat the crush based on how the rest of the market typically runs. Since this is a newly opened branch, don't assume the café keeps the same hours as the market stalls around it.

## Before you go

Because this location only recently opened, treat any hours or reservation details you find online as provisional.

- Confirm current opening hours directly with Onion's official channels or the café itself before making a special trip.
- If you want a guaranteed seat, check whether reservations are being taken, since demand at other Onion branches has been high enough to require them.
- Don't expect a printed or fixed menu to match other branches exactly; new locations sometimes adjust offerings early on.
- Pair the visit with the rest of Gwangjang Market: the food alleys and fabric section are both within a couple of minutes' walk.

## Getting the most from one stop

Treat this as a single stop that does double duty: market browsing and a coffee break in one loop. Grab a bindaetteok or mayak gimbap from a nearby stall first, then walk over for coffee, since the raw concrete interior is a genuine change of pace from the market's texture and noise. Keep expectations flexible on hours and seating until you've checked current details, and you'll get the best of both worlds without a wasted trip.
