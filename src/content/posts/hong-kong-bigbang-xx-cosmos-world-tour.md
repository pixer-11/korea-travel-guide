Twenty years is a long run for any pop group, and BIGBANG spent a chunk of the last decade broken up, solo, or in and out of the news for reasons that had nothing to do with music. The XX: Cosmos tour was billed as the reunion show, and Hong Kong was given three dates instead of the usual one-night stop, which said something about how this leg of the tour was being treated.

## What XX: Cosmos actually is

This wasn't a new album rollout with a supporting arena run. It was billed as a 20th-anniversary victory lap, and the "XX" in the title did double duty as both the Roman numeral for 20 and a nod to the group's old logo aesthetic. The setlist was expected to draw on career-spanning hits rather than a single record's tracklist.

The Hong Kong leg was set for three consecutive nights (November 13, 14, and 15, 2026) at one venue. That is unusual for a K-pop act at this stage, and it suggested either strong demand or a scaled-down number of Asian tour stops this cycle, possibly both. Either way, Hong Kong fans had more chances at a ticket than a typical single-night stop would offer.

## Kai Tak Stadium, the venue itself

Kai Tak Stadium sits on the site of Hong Kong's old international airport in Kowloon, and it's one of the city's newest large-scale venues, built as the centerpiece of the wider Kai Tak Sports Park development. It has a retractable roof and a bowl design meant for both sport and concerts, with capacity well into the tens of thousands.

Because it's new, the surrounding infrastructure is still maturing. Signage, crowd flow, and food and transport options around the stadium don't yet have the muscle memory that older Hong Kong venues like the Hong Kong Coliseum or AsiaWorld-Expo have built up over decades.

- Nearest access points connect via Kowloon Bay and Sung Wong Toi MTR stations, with shuttle and feeder bus services typically laid on for large events
- Roads around the old runway area can back up fast when tens of thousands of people leave at once
- Large-event logistics here, including gate and transport arrangements, are still being refined show by show

## The three-night ritual fans are planning around

A three-night stand at one venue creates its own micro-culture. Longtime BIGBANG fans, many of whom are now in their 30s, treat multi-night runs as a chance to catch different setlist quirks, camera angles, or member ad-libs across nights rather than seeing them as three identical shows.

Fan-organized meetups, light-stick trading, and merchandise queues outside the stadium in the hours before doors are a pattern that has followed this fandom through Seoul and Tokyo. Among fans attending only one night, night two often carries a reputation for looser, more relaxed performances once the opening-night jitters are out of the way, though this is fan lore rather than anything guaranteed.

## Where to base yourself in Kowloon

Kai Tak sits between the older, denser neighborhoods of Kowloon City and the newer waterfront stretch near Kwun Tong. Kowloon City still has some of Hong Kong's better Thai and Chiu Chow food streets, a legacy of its history as a low-rise district that escaped the airport height restrictions until Kai Tak closed.

- Mong Kok and Tsim Sha Tsui are a short MTR ride from the stadium, with far more hotel and dining options
- Kowloon City itself has fewer hotels but is walkable to the venue on a clear night if crowds allow
- After a stadium show, the walk to a working MTR platform can take 45 minutes to an hour

## Eating before or after the show

Kowloon City's Nga Tsin Wai Road and the streets around it are known locally for Thai food, a holdover from the neighborhood's long-standing Thai community. A quick bowl of boat noodles or a plate of green curry is a common, efficient meal ahead of a three-hour concert.

Kitchens near the stadium get slammed when a crowd this size lets out. Smaller Kowloon City spots away from the immediate stadium footprint tend to clear out faster than anything directly adjacent to the venue.
