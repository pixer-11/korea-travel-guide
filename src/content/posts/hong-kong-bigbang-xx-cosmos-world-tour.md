Twenty years is a long run for any pop group, and BIGBANG spent a chunk of the last decade broken up, solo, or in and out of the news for reasons that had nothing to do with music. The XX: Cosmos tour is billed as the reunion show, and Hong Kong got three dates instead of the usual one-night stop, which tells you something about how this leg of the tour is being treated.

## What XX: Cosmos actually is

This isn't a new album rollout with a supporting arena run. It's a 20th-anniversary victory lap, and the "XX" in the title is doing double duty as both the Roman numeral for 20 and a nod to the group's old logo aesthetic. Expect a setlist built around career-spanning hits rather than a single record's tracklist.

Three consecutive nights (November 13, 14, and 15, 2026) at one venue is unusual for a K-pop act at this stage; it suggests either strong demand or a scaled-down number of Asian tour stops this cycle, possibly both. Either way, Hong Kong fans get more chances at a ticket than a typical single-night stop would offer.

## Kai Tak Stadium, the venue itself

Kai Tak Stadium sits on the site of Hong Kong's old international airport in Kowloon, and it's one of the city's newest large-scale venues, built as the centerpiece of the wider Kai Tak Sports Park development. It has a retractable roof and a bowl design meant for both sport and concerts, with capacity well into the tens of thousands.

Because it's new, the surrounding infrastructure is still maturing. Signage, crowd flow, and food and transport options around the stadium don't yet have the muscle memory that older Hong Kong venues like the Hong Kong Coliseum or AsiaWorld-Expo have built up over decades.

- Nearest access points connect via Kowloon Bay and Sung Wong Toi MTR stations, with shuttle and feeder bus services typically laid on for large events
- Roads around the old runway area can back up fast when tens of thousands of people leave at once
- Confirm the exact gate and transport arrangements on the official Kai Tak Sports Park site or the tour's own site closer to your visit, since large-event logistics here are still being refined show by show

## Getting tickets without losing your shirt

For a three-night reunion run by an act with this much built-in nostalgia, expect heavy demand across all three dates, with weekend-adjacent nights likely to move faster than the others. Official ticketing for concerts at Kai Tak Sports Park typically runs through established Hong Kong platforms tied to the venue and promoter.

- Buy only through the tour's official site or the verified local ticketing partner it links to
- Check whether the show uses registered, ID-matched ticketing, which several recent large K-pop shows in Hong Kong have adopted to curb scalping
- Resale prices for BIGBANG shows have historically spiked hard; if buying secondhand, verify seat numbers and transfer method before paying

Confirm timing, pricing, and on-sale mechanics on the official tour site rather than relying on secondhand fan forums, which often carry outdated or region-mismatched information.

## The three-night ritual fans are planning around

A three-night stand at one venue creates its own micro-culture. Longtime BIGBANG fans, many of whom are now in their 30s, treat multi-night runs as a chance to catch different setlist quirks, camera angles, or member ad-libs across nights rather than seeing it as three identical shows.

Expect a visible cluster of fan-organized meetups, light-stick trading, and merchandise queues outside the stadium in the hours before doors, a pattern that's followed this fandom through Seoul, Tokyo, and now Kai Tak. If you're only attending one night, night two often carries a reputation among fans for looser, more relaxed performances once the opening-night jitters are out of the way, though this is fan lore rather than anything guaranteed.

## Where to base yourself in Kowloon

Kai Tak sits between the older, denser neighborhoods of Kowloon City and the newer waterfront stretch near Kwun Tong. Kowloon City still has some of Hong Kong's better Thai and Chiu Chow food streets, a legacy of its history as a low-rise district that escaped the airport height restrictions until Kai Tak closed.

- Staying in Mong Kok or Tsim Sha Tsui puts you a short MTR ride from the stadium with far more hotel and dining options
- Kowloon City itself has fewer hotels but is walkable to the venue on a clear night if crowds allow
- Whichever base you pick, build in 45 minutes to an hour post-show just for the walk to a working MTR platform

## Eating before or after the show

Kowloon City's Nga Tsin Wai Road and the streets around it are known locally for Thai food, a holdover from the neighborhood's long-standing Thai community. Grabbing a quick bowl of boat noodles or a plate of green curry before a three-hour concert is a reasonably efficient plan if you arrive with enough buffer time.

If you'd rather eat after, be aware that kitchens near the stadium will be slammed by a crowd this size; smaller Kowloon City spots away from the immediate stadium footprint tend to clear out faster than anything directly adjacent to the venue.

## Practical notes before you book

Hong Kong in mid-November sits in the city's most comfortable stretch of weather, dry and mild, which matters for a stadium with a retractable roof and a lot of outdoor queuing. Pack a light jacket for the evening rather than anything heavy.

Confirm gate times, bag policies, and any ID-matching ticket rules on the official tour site, since large indoor-outdoor stadiums like Kai Tak tend to adjust entry procedures as they host more events. Don't assume rules from another city's BIGBANG show will carry over here.
