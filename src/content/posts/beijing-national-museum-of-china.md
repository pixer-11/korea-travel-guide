## Why go

This is one of the largest museums on Earth, and somehow it still feels under-visited compared to the square outside it. Over a million objects live here, from Neolithic pottery to Qing dynasty court robes, spread across galleries so vast your footsteps echo on the marble.

You come for "Ancient China," the museum's permanent centerpiece, which walks you chronologically from Peking Man through the last emperor. Bronze ritual vessels sit under warm spotlights, their surfaces crusted green with age. Oracle bones, some of the earliest Chinese writing, are etched in a script you can almost trace with your eyes.

## Getting there

The museum sits at 16 Dong Chang An Jie, forming the eastern wall of Tiananmen Square. Take subway Line 1 to Tiananmen East Station and walk south a few minutes. Security here is airport-grade, matching the square itself, so leave pocketknives and large bags behind.

You'll pass through a bag scan and metal detector before reaching the ticket check. Budget extra time for this on busy mornings, even though the crowds are lighter than you'd expect for a landmark this central.

## What to see

The building itself is a Soviet-influenced 1959 structure, later gutted and rebuilt with a soaring atrium that makes the crowds feel small. Start upstairs with Ancient China before your legs tire, since it demands the most time.

Don't miss:

- The Simuwu ding, a massive Shang dynasty bronze cauldron, among the heaviest ancient bronzes ever cast
- Silk Road-era ceramics and Tang dynasty tomb figurines, glazed in amber and green
- Rotating art wings showing contemporary Chinese painting and calligraphy, a good contrast to the ancient halls
- Porcelain galleries tracing Ming and Qing kiln techniques

Give yourself at least two hours, three if you want to properly read the English placards, which are thorough but dense.

## When to go

The museum is closed Mondays entirely, so don't build it into a Monday itinerary. It opens 9am to 4:30pm Tuesday through Sunday, with no evening hours.

Arrive right at 9am if you can. Staff let visitors filter in steadily rather than all at once, so the first hour is noticeably calmer than midday, when tour groups clustered around the Tiananmen Square sights start filing in after visiting the flag-raising or the Chairman Mao Memorial Hall next door.

Because entry is free, there's little financial reason for anyone to skip it, which is exactly why the timed-ticket system exists. Book a slot for the day you want and stick to it.

## How to visit like a local

Reserve your free ticket online in advance through the museum's official booking channel or a linked platform — you cannot simply walk up and enter. You'll need to enter passport details matching whoever is attending, and bring the physical passport for the security check.

Payment barely factors in here since entry costs nothing, but special exhibitions occasionally carry a separate ticketed fee, so check signage near the entrance before assuming everything inside is included.

Tourists' most common mistake is treating this as a quick add-on to Tiananmen Square and rushing through in under an hour. Locals and regulars know Ancient China alone deserves ninety unhurried minutes.

Skip the ground-floor gift shop crowds at midday and browse it right before closing instead. And because this remains lightly visited relative to its size, weekday afternoons around 2pm to 3:30pm are your best shot at wandering a bronze-age gallery in near silence.
