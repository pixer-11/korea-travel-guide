Four days, one convention centre, and a programme built around three things: exhibitions, interactive displays and lectures. The Malaysia Muslim Festival returns to the World Trade Centre Kuala Lumpur from **January 28 to 31, 2027**. It's an annual Islamic cultural festival in a city where the call to prayer carries over the traffic five times a day, so the subject matter belongs here.

This guide covers what the festival is and how to reach the hall. It also explains the venue's two names and how to move through a busy Islamic event with ease.

## What fills the halls

The festival has three strands. Exhibitions make up the core, and interactive displays sit alongside them for visitors who'd rather do something than read a panel. Lectures run through the four days, and they're where the festival slows down.

That mix shapes the day. Exhibitions you can walk at your own pace, stopping where something catches you. Lectures are fixed points, so visitors who come for a particular talk tend to build the rest of their visit around it.

Nothing about the specific speakers or exhibitors is listed here, because we haven't verified them. Ticketing and programme details come from the festival organiser, not from the venue.

## The building everyone still calls PWTC

The World Trade Centre Kuala Lumpur used to be the Putra World Trade Centre. Plenty of locals, Grab drivers and older signs still use **PWTC**, and the LRT station next door kept that name. If someone looks blank at "WTC KL", say "PWTC" and they'll know it.

It's a large convention complex on **Jalan Tun Ismail**, at the northern edge of the city centre. Its best-known space is **Dewan Merdeka**, the big hall used for decades of national political gatherings and trade fairs. Inside you'll find what you'd expect from a centre this size:

- long carpeted concourses between halls
- escalators linking the levels
- strong air-conditioning, which feels sharp after the heat outside

Sunway Putra Mall stands across the road. On a long festival day it's handy for a proper meal, an ATM or just a break from the crowds.

## Getting to Jalan Tun Ismail

The train is the simplest way to get here. **PWTC LRT station**, on the Ampang and Sri Petaling lines, sits beside the complex and a covered walkway runs to the venue. From Masjid Jamek, where these lines meet the Kelana Jaya line, it's only a few stops north.

Other options:

- **Sultan Ismail station** (KL Monorail, plus the Ampang/Sri Petaling lines) is a short walk away, though you'll cross busy roads.
- **Grab** is the standard ride-hailing app in KL. Typing "PWTC" or "WTC Kuala Lumpur" usually pins the right entrance.
- **Driving** means using the complex's own car parks. On convention weekends they fill up, and Jalan Tun Ismail backs up with them.

KL Sentral is a short ride away, so it's easy to come straight from the airport rail link with your luggage.

## A festival on the doorstep of Ramadan

This is the part of the timing that's specific to this festival. The 2027 edition runs Thursday 28 January to Sunday 31 January. That's the last stretch of weeks before Ramadan, which in 2027 is expected to start in early February.

That gives the festival a feel of preparation. For many Muslim visitors it's a chance to listen and learn in the run-up to the fasting month, and the lectures matter more because of it. Chinese New Year also falls in early February 2027, so the city around you is busy getting ready for two big seasons at once.

The days themselves feel different. Thursday and the morning of Friday are working days for most of KL, while Saturday and Sunday draw families. If you want quieter aisles, the weekday end is the better bet, simply because offices and schools are open.

## Moving through an Islamic event with ease

Most of this is common sense, but a few habits make a visit smoother. This applies especially if you're not Muslim, and you're welcome to attend.

**Dress.** Visitors usually wear loose clothing that covers shoulders and knees. Women aren't expected to cover their hair at a cultural exhibition, but many attendees will, and modest dress is the norm in the halls.

**Friday prayers.** On Friday 29 January, Muslim men leave for Jumaat prayers around midday. Many Malaysian events slow down or pause through that window. Visitors who aren't attending prayers usually spend it in the exhibition halls or have an early lunch across the road.

**Prayer space.** Venues of this size in Malaysia have a *surau* (prayer room), and you'll see attendees heading there at prayer times. Let people pass, and don't photograph anyone praying.

**Lectures.** Attendees tend to take their seats before a talk starts and stay put until it ends. Phones go on silent. If you need to leave, slip out at the back.

**Greetings.** *Assalamualaikum* is the standard Muslim greeting; the reply is *waalaikumsalam*. A plain "terima kasih" (thank you) works with anyone.

**Paying.** Malaysia leans heavily toward cashless payment. DuitNow QR codes and the Touch 'n Go eWallet are everywhere, and cards are widely accepted. A few ringgit in notes still helps at small stalls.

## Food and walks around the venue

The festival makes a good base for exploring this end of the city. A few places to try:

- **Chow Kit**: the wet market and the Bazaar Baru Chow Kit stalls are a short walk or one Monorail stop away. They're best visited in the morning, when the produce is fresh.
- **Kampung Baru**: a Malay village of wooden houses that has survived against the Petronas Towers skyline. It's known for nasi lemak and Malay rice stalls, and it's a short Grab ride east.
- **Masjid Jamek**: one of KL's oldest mosques, built in 1909 where the Klang and Gombak rivers meet. It's a few LRT stops south.
- **Masjid Negara and the Islamic Arts Museum Malaysia**: the National Mosque and the museum sit together near the Lake Gardens. Together they make a natural follow-on day after the festival's exhibitions.

If you're short on time, Sunway Putra Mall's food court across the road is the fastest option.

## How long to give it

Half a day covers the exhibitions and interactive displays at a comfortable pace. Adding a lecture or two turns it into a full day. Many visitors pair a morning at the festival with an afternoon at Masjid Jamek or Kampung Baru.
