## Why Monza matters

Monza isn't just another stop on the calendar. It's the "Temple of Speed," a circuit carved through a royal park north of Milan since 1922, and it's Ferrari's home race.

Walk the paddock fence on race weekend and you'll understand instantly why. The tifosi don't just watch — they roar, they wave flags the size of bedsheets, and when a red car crosses the line first, the grandstands genuinely shake.

## Getting there

The track sits inside Parco di Monza, about 15km northeast of central Milan. Most visitors base themselves in Milan and take the train to Monza station, then a shuttle bus laid on for race weekend straight to the circuit gates.

Driving is possible but painful — parking fills early and roads around the park clog for hours before and after sessions. If you're staying in Monza town itself, several gates are within a 20–30 minute walk through the park, which is a pleasant option on a warm September morning.

## What to expect on track

Friday brings two practice sessions, a chance to wander the circuit at a relaxed pace and catch cars flat-out through the Curva Grande. Saturday is qualifying, when the tension in the grandstands visibly tightens as drivers chase pole around one of the fastest laps on the calendar.

Sunday is race day — the reason everyone's there. The start into the first chicane is chaotic and loud, and the low-downforce setup means cars top 350 km/h down the pit straight, a genuine physical jolt when they scream past.

Beyond the racing, expect:

- Support races and F1 support series on Friday/Saturday
- Fan zones and merchandise stands near the main entrance
- Historic Monza banking (the old oval) visible on a walk around the park — a crumbling, photogenic relic of 1950s speed

## Tickets and where to sit

Grandstands range from the Rettifilo Tribuna near the start/finish straight to Curva Grande and Ascari, each offering a different character of racing. Rettifilo puts you closest to the podium action; Ascari and Lesmo grandstands give you tight, technical cornering to watch instead of pure speed.

General admission inside the park is usually the most affordable way in, letting you roam between viewing hills and screens, though you'll be standing or picnicking on grass rather than a fixed seat. Always confirm exact prices, grandstand maps, and on-sale dates on the official Formula1.com or Monza circuit website, since allocations and costs shift year to year and this is Ferrari's home race — demand is intense.

## When to go

September in Lombardy is usually warm and dry, comfortable for a full day outdoors, though afternoon sun on open grandstands can be intense — a hat and sunscreen earn their keep. Arrive at the circuit gates early on race day; queues build fast once shuttle buses start dropping thousands of fans at once.

## How to visit like a local

Italian fans call it "Monza" or simply "il Gran Premio d'Italia," and most arrive by train rather than car — follow that lead. Buy grandstand tickets the moment they go on sale; locals know Ferrari's home race is the one weekend Monza genuinely sells out.

Cash and card are both widely accepted at food stalls and merchandise stands inside the park, though a little cash speeds up small vendor queues. Bring your own water bottle to refill — queues at drink stands get long by midday.

The mistake tourists make is leaving the park the second the chequered flag drops. Traffic and shuttle queues are brutal right after the race; locals linger over a coffee-stand espresso, let the crowd thin for 30–45 minutes, then head out. Applauding the podium ceremony from the grandstand, even if you can't see it directly, is part of the ritual — most of the crowd stays for it.
