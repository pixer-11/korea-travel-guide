## Standing on the city's first square

Modern Mendoza was not built here. This plaza was the colonial Plaza Mayor, laid out after Pedro del Castillo founded the city in 1561. It stayed the civic centre for three centuries, with the Cabildo, churches and main houses facing it.

That ended on the night of 20 March 1861. The earthquake and the fires that followed destroyed the old city. Mendoza was rebuilt to the southwest around today's Plaza Independencia, and the old core became the barrio people still call the Área Fundacional, or Ciudad Vieja.

So when you cross this square, you are walking over the original grid. The streets around it are lower-rise and quieter than the microcentro, and the plaza itself is the focal point of a small heritage district rather than a shopping zone.

## What you'll find around the plaza

The square is paved and open, built for walking through rather than lounging on a lawn. A statue of the founder, Pedro del Castillo, marks it. The best-known sight is next door.

- **Museo del Área Fundacional:** built over the foundations of the colonial Cabildo. Excavated remains sit below floor level and you look down on them through glass panels.
- **Archaeological displays:** finds from the old city and material on the 1861 earthquake, which is the main story here.
- **Ruinas de San Francisco:** a short walk away. These are the roofless brick walls of the Jesuit church, later run by the Franciscans, left standing after the quake.

Together they explain why Mendoza's modern centre is so wide and so low. Rebuilding after 1861 meant broad avenues and open plazas that people could escape to in another tremor.

## Getting to the Área Fundacional

From Plaza Independencia it is roughly a 20-minute walk northeast. The streets are flat and set out on a grid, so it is easy to find. The route takes you out of the café-lined microcentro into plainer residential blocks.

A taxi or ride-hailing car is quick if the afternoon heat is strong, which it often is in a Mendoza summer. Ask for "el Área Fundacional" or "Plaza Pedro del Castillo"; drivers know both names. City buses run nearby too, but walking lets you see the city change from new to old.

## Timing around the posted hours

The schedule Google lists right now runs Tuesday to Saturday from 9am to 7pm. Sundays are afternoon only, 3pm to 7pm, and Monday is closed. These are the hours for the museum side of the site, and heritage sites like this often change them through the year, so check the official municipal page for this month's times.

Don't come on a Monday expecting to get inside. A Sunday visit means arriving after 3pm. For the whole circuit with the ruins included, a weekday morning gives you the most time.

## Visiting the way Mendocinos do

Mendoza still keeps its siesta. Many shops and smaller businesses in the area close for part of the afternoon. Eat before you come, or plan to head back to the centre for lunch.

- **Speak a little Spanish:** museum staff may not speak English. A "buen día" when you walk in goes a long way.
- **Ask about guided visits:** the museum has run guided tours, and these bring the underground ruins to life. Ask at the desk whether one is on that day.
- **Respect the glass:** you can walk over some of the viewing panels, but treat the excavations as a site, not a photo prop.
- **Carry water:** Mendoza is dry desert, and the plaza has little shade in the middle of the day.

The square is well known and gets a steady stream of school groups and visitors. If you want the museum quieter, arriving right at the 9am weekday opening is your best chance, since groups tend to come later in the morning.

## Pairing it with the rest of the day

After the old city, go and see the city that replaced it. Head back southwest to Plaza Independencia and the four smaller squares around it, including Plaza España with its tiled benches. That trip shows you the post-1861 plan in full.

You can also spend the afternoon in Parque General San Martín on the western edge of the centre. Many visitors use a city day like this to rest between winery trips to Luján de Cuyo or Maipú, and the Área Fundacional fits easily into an unhurried morning.
