## A November race on the Costa del Sol

Málaga in November suits a long run. Summer heat has gone by then, and daytime temperatures on this coast usually sit in the mild mid-teens Celsius. That is close to ideal for running 42.195 kilometres.

The Generali Málaga Marathon is an annual road race with two distances: the full marathon and the half. In 2026 it runs across the weekend of November 7-8. That is a Saturday and a Sunday, so you can fly in on Friday and head home on Monday.

The city is low-lying along its seafront, between the Mediterranean and the Montes de Málaga behind it. You see sea on one side and the old centre on the other. The Alcazaba fortress and Gibralfaro castle sit on the hill above.

## Dates, distances and getting a bib

There are no tickets for this event in the concert sense. If you are running, you register for a bib. If you are watching, you just stand by the road.

- **Dates:** November 7-8, 2026 (Saturday and Sunday)
- **Distances:** marathon and half marathon
- **Registration:** through the official race website, where entry fees and any cut-off dates are posted
- **Schedule:** check the official site for which distance runs on which day, plus start times

Confirm timing, the route and registration on the official site before you book anything. Big-city marathons often hand out bibs at a pre-race expo, so leave room in your plans for collection. The official site lists where and when that happens.

## Flying in and finding the start

Most international visitors land at Málaga-Costa del Sol Airport (AGP), southwest of the city. The Cercanías C1 commuter train goes from the airport to the centre. Get off at Málaga Centro-Alameda for the old town, or María Zambrano for the main rail hub.

From Madrid, the AVE high-speed train arrives at María Zambrano. The trip takes roughly two and a half to three hours. Coming from Seville or Córdoba, the train is usually the easiest option too.

The race's start and finish venue is published on the official site. Check it before you choose where to stay. The historic centre is compact, and from the area around Calle Larios you can reach most of the waterfront on foot. Expect road closures on race day, so walking is often quicker than a taxi.

## Breakfast before the gun: ordering coffee the Málaga way

Málaga has its own coffee vocabulary, which is useful to know at 6am. Order a "café con leche" here and you may get a puzzled look, because locals name their coffee by how much milk goes in. The system is credited to Café Central on Plaza de la Constitución, where a tiled chart on the wall shows every ratio.

- **Solo:** straight espresso
- **Largo:** espresso with a little milk
- **Semilargo:** a bit more milk than a largo
- **Mitad:** half coffee, half milk
- **Entrecorto:** a bit more coffee than milk
- **Corto:** more milk than coffee
- **Sombra:** mostly milk with a shadow of coffee
- **Nube:** a cloud of milk with barely any coffee

For a carb load that doesn't upset your stomach, order a **pitufo**. It's a small soft roll, usually served as **tostada con tomate y aceite**: toasted, rubbed with grated tomato and drizzled with olive oil. Most bars along the old-town streets serve this from early morning.

## Cheering, eating and paying in race-week Málaga

Spain eats late, and that catches runners out. Restaurants often don't fill up for dinner until well after 9pm, so book an early table for your pre-race pasta. Or eat a substantial lunch, which is the main meal here anyway.

At bars you usually pay at the end, not per round. Cards and contactless work almost everywhere, though small coins help at kiosks. Nobody expects a tip. Rounding up or leaving a little change for good table service is normal, and anything more is unusual.

On the roadside, Spanish crowds shout **"¡Ánimo!"** (keep going) and **"¡Vamos!"** (let's go). Runners often have their first name printed on the bib, and spectators will use it. If you're watching, follow the marshals' instructions at crossing points. Course barriers are there to keep runners moving, not to stop you seeing.

## Where spectators gather

The waterfront is the natural place to watch. The Paseo del Parque is a palm-lined promenade beside the port, and Muelle Uno is the redeveloped pier below the Alcazaba. Both give wide pavements and easy walking between viewing points.

Check the published route map before you choose a spot. On a looped city course, you can sometimes see a runner twice by cutting across the centre on foot. The Malagueta beach promenade, east of the port, is also within walking distance of the old town.

## The day after: Málaga on tired legs

Recovery here can be gentle. The Mercado Central de Atarazanas is the main food market, inside an iron-and-glass hall behind a Moorish-era gateway. Go for olives, fried fish and fresh fruit without walking far.

Things to do once your legs work again:

- **Málaga Cathedral:** known locally as "La Manquita" (the one-armed lady) because its second tower was never finished
- **Alcazaba:** the Moorish fortress above the Roman Theatre, reached by sloping paths and steps
- **Museo Picasso Málaga:** in the city where the artist was born, a short walk from the cathedral
- **Malagueta beach:** for standing knee-deep in the Mediterranean, the cheapest ice bath around

Skip the climb up to Gibralfaro on Monday unless your quads say yes. The path is steep, and the bus is a fair alternative.

## Planning the weekend

Book accommodation early. A big road race fills the old town's hotels and apartments quickly. Districts like Soho, La Malagueta and the area around the Alameda Principal keep you within walking distance of the centre.

Pack for mild days and cooler mornings. Bring a throwaway layer for the start corral. Most importantly, confirm timing, venue and registration details on the official race site, because that is the only source the organisers stand behind.
