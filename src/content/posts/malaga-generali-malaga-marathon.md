## A November race on the Costa del Sol

Málaga in November suits a long run. Summer heat has gone by then, and daytime temperatures on this coast usually sit in the mild mid-teens Celsius. That is close to ideal for running 42.195 kilometres.

The Generali Málaga Marathon is an annual road race with two distances: the full marathon and the half. The 2026 edition was set for the weekend of November 7-8, a Saturday and a Sunday.

The city is low-lying along its seafront, between the Mediterranean and the Montes de Málaga behind it. You see sea on one side and the old centre on the other. The Alcazaba fortress and Gibralfaro castle sit on the hill above.

## Dates, distances and getting a bib

There were no tickets for this event in the concert sense. Runners registered for a bib. Spectators simply stood by the road.

- **Dates:** November 7-8, 2026 (Saturday and Sunday)
- **Distances:** marathon and half marathon
- **Registration:** through the official race website

Big-city marathons often hand out bibs at a pre-race expo in the days before the race. Collection usually means a visit in person with identification and the registration confirmation.

## Flying in and finding the start

Most international visitors land at Málaga-Costa del Sol Airport (AGP), southwest of the city. The Cercanías C1 commuter train goes from the airport to the centre. Málaga Centro-Alameda is the stop for the old town, and María Zambrano is the main rail hub.

From Madrid, the AVE high-speed train arrives at María Zambrano. The trip takes roughly two and a half to three hours. From Seville or Córdoba, the train is usually the easiest option too.

The historic centre is compact, and from the area around Calle Larios most of the waterfront is within walking distance. Road closures are normal on race day, so walking is often quicker than a taxi.

## Breakfast before the gun: ordering coffee the Málaga way

Málaga has its own coffee vocabulary, which is useful to know at 6am. Order a "café con leche" here and you may get a puzzled look, because locals name their coffee by how much milk goes in. The system is credited to Café Central on Plaza de la Constitución, where a tiled chart on the wall shows every ratio.

- **Solo:** straight espresso
- **Largo:** espresso with a little milk
- **Semilargo:** a bit more milk than a largo
- **Mitad:** half coffee, half milk
- **Entrecorto:** a bit more coffee than milk
- **Corto:** more milk than coffee
- **Sombra:** mostly milk with a shadow of coffee
- **Nube:** a cloud of milk with barely any coffee

A gentle carb load is the **pitufo**. It's a small soft roll, usually served as **tostada con tomate y aceite**: toasted, rubbed with grated tomato and drizzled with olive oil. Most bars along the old-town streets serve it from early morning.

## Cheering, eating and paying in race-week Málaga

Spain eats late, and that catches runners out. Restaurants often don't fill up for dinner until well after 9pm, so runners tend to take an early table for pre-race pasta. Others eat a substantial lunch, which is the main meal here anyway.

At bars you usually pay at the end, not per round. Cards and contactless work almost everywhere, though small coins help at kiosks. Nobody expects a tip. Rounding up or leaving a little change for good table service is normal, and anything more is unusual.

On the roadside, Spanish crowds shout **"¡Ánimo!"** (keep going) and **"¡Vamos!"** (let's go). Runners often have their first name printed on the bib, and spectators use it. Marshals manage the crossing points. Course barriers are there to keep runners moving, not to block the view.

## Where spectators gather

The waterfront is the natural place to watch. The Paseo del Parque is a palm-lined promenade beside the port, and Muelle Uno is the redeveloped pier below the Alcazaba. Both have wide pavements and easy walking between viewing points.

On a looped city course, spectators can sometimes see a runner twice by cutting across the centre on foot. The Malagueta beach promenade, east of the port, is also within walking distance of the old town.

## The day after: Málaga on tired legs

Recovery here can be gentle. The Mercado Central de Atarazanas is the main food market, inside an iron-and-glass hall behind a Moorish-era gateway. It has olives, fried fish and fresh fruit without a long walk.

Things to do once your legs work again:

- **Málaga Cathedral:** known locally as "La Manquita" (the one-armed lady) because its second tower was never finished
- **Alcazaba:** the Moorish fortress above the Roman Theatre, reached by sloping paths and steps
- **Museo Picasso Málaga:** in the city where the artist was born, a short walk from the cathedral
- **Malagueta beach:** for standing knee-deep in the Mediterranean, the cheapest ice bath around

The climb up to Gibralfaro is hard on tired quads. The path is steep, and the bus is a fair alternative.

## Planning the weekend

Big road races tend to fill the old town's hotels and apartments quickly. Districts like Soho, La Malagueta and the area around the Alameda Principal sit within walking distance of the centre.

November brings mild days and cooler mornings. A throwaway layer is standard kit in the start corral.
