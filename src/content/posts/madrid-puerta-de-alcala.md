## Granite, limestone and a roundabout

Traffic moves around you in a slow circle. In the middle, on a fenced island of lawn and clipped hedges, stands a pale stone gate with five openings. The three central openings are round-arched, with the middle one tallest. The two outer ones are square-headed, sized for people on foot rather than carriages.

This is the Puerta de Alcalá. Francesco Sabatini designed it for King Carlos III, and it was completed in 1778. It replaced an older gate on the road out to Alcalá de Henares, which is where the name comes from. The date and the king's name are carved across the top in Latin: REGE CAROLO III ANNO MDCCLXXVIII.

## Reading the stone

Go close and look up. It is worth the effort. The lower body is grey granite and the upper sections are lighter limestone, so the gate reads as two tones even at a distance. The top is crowded with carved detail:

- **Lion heads** set into the keystones of the arches
- **Cherubs and trophies of arms** crowning the central section
- **Coats of arms** held aloft above the inscription
- **Columns and capitals** framing each opening

The sculptures were carved by Roberto Michel and Francisco Gutiérrez. The two faces of the gate are not identical, so walk the full circuit of the roundabout. What you see from Calle de Alcalá is a different composition from what you see on the Retiro side.

## Getting to Plaza de la Independencia

The nearest metro stop is **Retiro (Line 2)**. It sits on Calle de Alcalá, a few minutes' walk east of the gate. **Banco de España (Line 2)** puts you at the Cibeles fountain. From there, the gate is a short, gently uphill walk along Calle de Alcalá, and the arch stays in view ahead of you the whole way.

The address is Pl. de la Independencia, s/n, 28001 Madrid. That "s/n" means *sin número*: the monument has no street number because it takes up the whole square. Several city bus routes cross the plaza, but walking is usually the easier way to arrive.

## The song everyone hums

Mention the gate to a Spaniard of a certain age and you may hear a chorus. "La Puerta de Alcalá", recorded in 1986 by Ana Belén and Víctor Manuel, runs through two centuries of Madrid history as the gate "watches the time go by". Its refrain, *"ahí está, ahí está, la Puerta de Alcalá"*, is one of the best-known lines in Spanish pop.

Playing it through your headphones as you cross toward the island is a small ritual plenty of visitors keep. It also explains why the gate turns up so often on souvenirs, murals and fridge magnets across the city. For Madrileños, it stands for the city itself.

## Timing your photos around the crowds

The gate never closes, and early morning is the clear winner. It's calmest between **7am and 9am on weekdays** and **7am and 10am at weekends**. At that hour the island is mostly empty, and low sun from the east lands on the Retiro-facing side.

Try not to come between **1pm and 11pm at weekends**. That is when the plaza is at its busiest. The Retiro crowds spill across the square, tour groups stop here, and every crossing point has people waiting with phones raised. After dark the arch is floodlit. A weekday evening gives you the lit stone without the weekend crush.

## Crossing, framing and staying out of the traffic

This is not a place where people linger on café terraces. Madrileños mostly see it through a bus window or on the walk into the park. A few habits will make your visit easier:

- **Use the zebra crossings.** The roundabout carries heavy traffic, and stepping into the road for a better angle is the one real danger here.
- **Pick your side.** The pavement by the Retiro's Puerta de la Independencia gate gives a clean frontal view. The Calle de Alcalá approach from Cibeles frames the arch at the end of a long avenue.
- **Respect the fence.** The planted island is for looking at, not climbing into.
- **Keep an eye on bags.** Busy photo spots in central Madrid draw pickpockets, especially at weekend peak times.

You won't need cash or a booking, because there is no entry point and nothing to pay. Fifteen to thirty minutes covers a slow circuit and your photos.

## Where to walk next

The gate works best as a hinge between neighbourhoods. Across the square, the **Puerta de la Independencia** leads into **Parque del Buen Retiro**. Head for the boating lake at the Estanque Grande and the Crystal Palace beyond it.

Downhill to the west, the **Plaza de Cibeles** and its fountain sit in front of the Palacio de Cibeles, Madrid's city hall. Beyond that, the Paseo del Prado runs past the Prado museum. To the north, **Calle de Serrano** begins at the plaza and leads into the shopping streets of the Salamanca district.
