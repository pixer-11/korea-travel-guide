Step out of Tamsui MRT Station and the river is right there. It is wide and brown, with Guanyin Mountain rising on the far bank. The Old Street runs along it, a few minutes' walk to the left. You don't come here for a single sight. You come for a long, slow graze past fried snacks, grilled squid, temple incense and buskers.

## Why Tamsui pulls a crowd

Tamsui was once northern Taiwan's main port. Its layers still show. Spanish and Dutch traders built the fort that became Fort San Domingo. A Canadian missionary, George Leslie Mackay, arrived in 1872 and founded churches, schools and a clinic. Fishermen and merchants filled the lanes in between.

What survives today is a busy street that can feel more like a snack festival than a museum. That's the appeal. This is one of the most-reviewed attractions in greater Taipei and consistently well rated, and it is not a quiet find. Go expecting company.

## Getting there on the red line

The trip is easy, which is part of why it's so busy.

- **MRT:** Take the Tamsui-Xinyi Line (red, R) to its northern terminus, Tamsui Station (R28). From Taipei Main Station it's roughly 40 minutes, no transfers.
- **Walk:** Leave the station toward the river and follow the waterfront promenade or Zhongzheng Road. The Old Street begins within a few minutes.
- **Payment:** An EasyCard or iPASS covers the MRT, buses and the local ferries, so top one up before you leave the city.

## Walking the three streets

The address lists three roads, and each one feels different.

**Zhongzheng Road** is the main drag, and it's where most of the food lives. The shopfronts are open to the street, and the pavement narrows where queues spill out. Partway along you'll find Fuyou Temple, a Mazu temple from the 18th century and one of the oldest in Tamsui. A bust of Mackay stands at the junction near the eastern end.

**Chongjian Street** climbs uphill behind the main road. It's steep, stepped and much older in feel, with low brick houses and fewer stalls. Take it for a break from the crush.

**Qingshui Street** runs inland toward the traditional market area. The shops there serve daily life more than day-trippers.

Between them sit the street sculptures and buskers the street is known for. Waterfront performers usually draw a ring of onlookers once the afternoon cools.

## A-gei, fish balls and iron eggs

Tamsui has its own snack canon, and you should eat through it.

- **A-gei (阿給):** A block of fried tofu, hollowed out and stuffed with glass noodles. It's sealed with fish paste, steamed, and served in a sweet-savoury red sauce. You eat it with a spoon, often with a bowl of soup on the side.
- **Fish ball soup (魚丸湯):** Tamsui fish balls are made from fish paste with a pork filling. They come floating in clear broth with celery.
- **Iron eggs (鐵蛋):** Small eggs braised and air-dried over and over until they turn black, dense and chewy. They're sold in vacuum packs and make a good gift for home.
- **Fish crackers and fish rolls:** Crisp local snacks sold by the bag at speciality shops.

Beyond those staples you'll find grilled squid, oversized ice-cream cones and fried seafood. Share portions so you can try more.

## Sunset behind Guanyin Mountain

Tamsui is known as one of the best places near Taipei to watch the sun set. The river opens to the west, and the sun drops toward Guanyin Mountain and the Taiwan Strait.

Time your visit around it. Eat your way along the street in the late afternoon, then find a spot on the promenade railings or at a riverside café before the light turns. If you want more, take the ferry or a bus to Fisherman's Wharf. Lover's Bridge, a white span over the harbour, is the classic sunset spot there.

## Snacking without the shuffle

The street works best if you know how it runs.

- **Carry cash.** Most snack stalls take small notes and coins. Some shops accept cards or mobile payment, but don't count on it at the counters.
- **Order by pointing.** Menus are often in Chinese only. Say the dish name ("a-gei", "yu-wan tang") or point at the photo or the tray. Tipping isn't expected.
- **Join the right line.** Famous stalls have queues, and some hand out numbers. Stand where everyone else is standing, and don't hover at the counter.
- **Keep your rubbish.** Public bins are rare in Taiwan. Stalls will often take back their own cups and skewers. Otherwise, keep a bag for litter until you pass a convenience store.
- **Pick your day.** Weekends and holidays pack Zhongzheng Road shoulder to shoulder, as you'd expect near a city of millions. A weekday afternoon gives you room to stop and look.
- **Step aside to eat.** The main road is narrow. Move to the riverside or into a side lane to finish your food.

## Beyond the snack stalls

Give the Old Street itself one to two hours. Budget half a day if you add the sights nearby.

- **Fort San Domingo (紅毛城):** A red-brick fort on the hill, first built by the Spanish, then rebuilt by the Dutch. The British consular residence stands next to it. It's a steady uphill walk from the Old Street.
- **Tamsui Church:** Mackay's Presbyterian church, a brick building with a square bell tower, just up from Mazu Street.
- **Oxford College at Aletheia University:** Mackay's 1882 school building, on the same hill as the fort.
- **Bali (八里):** Ferries cross from the Tamsui pier to Bali on the opposite bank. It has a flat riverside cycle path and more snack stalls.
- **Fisherman's Wharf:** Reached by ferry, bus or the Danhai Light Rail. It has Lover's Bridge and boardwalk seating over the water.

Opening hours for the fort, church and ferries change with the season, so check them before you plan your day around one.
