Seventy point three is the number to start with. That is the race distance in miles: a 1.9 km swim, a 90 km bike leg and a 21.1 km half-marathon, all in one morning. In mid-November 2026, Puerto Princesa City puts that distance on Palawan's roads and coastline, and athletes from around the region fly in for it.

The swim water is the tropical sea of Palawan, not a cold northern lake. The run comes late in the morning, under equatorial sun. That combination shapes almost everything about how this race goes.

## What the 70.3 format means here

Every Ironman 70.3 follows the same three legs, done back to back with the clock running throughout. Between legs you change gear in transition areas, where bikes hang on long metal racks in numbered rows.

- **Swim:** 1.9 km in open water
- **Bike:** 90 km on closed or controlled roads
- **Run:** 21.1 km, the half-marathon that finishes the day

The finish is the same at every Ironman race: a carpeted chute, an announcer reading out names, and a medal at the end. The exact swim start, transition sites and finish line belong to the organiser's course maps, which are published on the official Ironman event page for Puerto Princesa.

## Racing in Palawan heat

November falls in the shift between Palawan's wet and dry seasons. You can expect heat and high humidity, plus the chance of a passing shower. For a field mostly used to cooler races, the run is where the day is decided.

Athletes who come from cooler climates usually arrive several days early to adjust. At Ironman races in Southeast Asia, aid stations tend to hand out ice, water and sponges, and you will see runners pushing ice down their tri-suits between stations. Spectators deal with the same sun. Umbrellas, hats and a refillable bottle are what you will see along the barriers.

## Tricycles, closures and race-morning roads

Puerto Princesa runs on tricycles. These are motorbikes with covered sidecars, and they are the usual way to get around town for short hops. On race morning, closures for the bike and run courses change which streets they can use.

Ironman 70.3 races generally start around dawn. Athletes therefore usually stay close to the start or transition so they can walk or take a short ride before the roads shut. Spectators tend to choose one stretch of the run course and stay there. The run often uses loops, so a single spot lets you see the same athlete more than once.

## Watching like a regular at the barriers

Spectating at a triathlon is mostly free and informal. People stand on public roadsides, and the run course is the easiest place to watch. Here is what experienced supporters usually do:

- **Track the athlete:** the official Ironman Tracker app shows live split times, so you know when your runner is coming
- **Cheer by name:** race numbers and often first names are printed on the bibs, and Filipino crowds use them freely
- **Stay off the course:** running alongside an athlete or handing them food can get them penalised under Ironman rules
- **Head to the finish late:** the chute is liveliest while the middle of the field comes through, not when the leaders arrive

Supporters in Puerto Princesa usually pay cash for tricycle rides and at small eateries, so carrying small peso notes saves time. Larger hotels and restaurants take cards. Tipping isn't required, but rounding up for good service is common.

## Getting to Puerto Princesa

Most athletes fly into Puerto Princesa International Airport (PPS), which has frequent domestic flights from Manila and Cebu. The airport sits close to the city centre, and tricycles and vans wait outside.

Bike boxes are what make this trip complicated. Triathletes usually check their airline's sports-equipment allowance, since domestic carriers handle oversized bags differently. Racers often rebuild their bikes at the hotel and do a short spin on local roads before racking.

Most visitors stay in the city proper, around Rizal Avenue and the Baywalk along Puerto Princesa Bay. This keeps everyone close to the race logistics and to restaurants.

## Before and after race week

Many athletes add recovery days to the trip, because Palawan is the reason this race attracts travellers in the first place.

- **Puerto Princesa Subterranean River National Park:** a UNESCO World Heritage Site, roughly two hours north by road at Sabang. Entry permits are handled through the city's tourism system, and day tours include transport.
- **Honda Bay:** island-hopping boats leave from a wharf north of the city, stopping at sandbars and snorkelling spots.
- **Plaza Cuartel and the Immaculate Conception Cathedral:** a short walk apart in the old centre, and easy for an afternoon after the race.
- **Baywalk:** the waterfront promenade where locals gather in the evening, with grilled-food stalls nearby.

A long boat day straight after 113 km of racing is hard on tired legs. Athletes usually keep the day after the race light and save the Underground River trip for a day later.
