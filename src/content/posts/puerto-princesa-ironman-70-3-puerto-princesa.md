## What the 70.3 format means here

Every Ironman 70.3 follows the same three legs, done back to back with the clock running throughout. Between legs, athletes change gear in transition areas, where bikes hang on long metal racks in numbered rows.

- **Swim:** 1.9 km in open water
- **Bike:** 90 km on closed or controlled roads
- **Run:** 21.1 km, the half-marathon that finishes the day

The finish is the same at every Ironman race: a carpeted chute, an announcer reading out names, and a medal at the end.

November falls in the shift between Palawan's wet and dry seasons. Heat and high humidity are normal, and passing showers are common. In these conditions, the run is usually where the day is decided.

Athletes from cooler climates usually spend several days acclimatising. ## Tricycles, closures and race-morning roads

Puerto Princesa runs on tricycles. These are motorbikes with covered sidecars, and they are the usual way to get around town for short hops. On race mornings, closures for the bike and run courses change which streets they can use.

Ironman 70.3 races generally start around dawn. Athletes therefore usually stay near the start or transition so they can walk or take a short ride before the roads shut. Spectators tend to pick one stretch of the run course and stay there. The run often uses loops, so a single spot shows the same athlete more than once.

## Watching like a regular at the barriers

Spectating at a triathlon is mostly free and informal. People stand on public roadsides, and the run course is the easiest place to watch. Experienced supporters usually do the following:

- **Track the athlete:** the official Ironman Tracker app shows live split times, so supporters know when their runner is coming
- **Cheer by name:** race numbers and often first names are printed on the bibs, and Filipino crowds use them freely
- **Stay off the course:** running alongside an athlete or handing them food can get them penalised under Ironman rules
- **Finish-line timing:** the chute is liveliest while the middle of the field comes through, not when the leaders arrive

In Puerto Princesa, tricycle rides and small eateries are usually paid in cash, and small peso notes are the norm. Larger hotels and restaurants take cards. Tipping isn't required, but rounding up for good service is common.

## Getting to Puerto Princesa

Most athletes fly into Puerto Princesa International Airport (PPS), which has frequent domestic flights from Manila and Cebu. The airport sits close to the city centre, and tricycles and vans wait outside.

Bike boxes are what make this trip complicated. Domestic carriers handle oversized bags differently, and sports-equipment allowances vary by airline. Racers often rebuild their bikes at the hotel and do a short spin on local roads before racking.

Most visitors stay in the city proper, around Rizal Avenue and the Baywalk along Puerto Princesa Bay. This keeps them near restaurants and the waterfront.

## Before and after race week

Many athletes add recovery days to the trip, because Palawan is the reason this race attracts travellers in the first place.

- **Puerto Princesa Subterranean River National Park:** a UNESCO World Heritage Site, roughly two hours north by road at Sabang. Entry permits are handled through the city's tourism system, and day tours include transport.
- **Honda Bay:** island-hopping boats leave from a wharf north of the city, stopping at sandbars and snorkelling spots.
- **Plaza Cuartel and the Immaculate Conception Cathedral:** a short walk apart in the old centre, and an easy afternoon after a race.
- **Baywalk:** the waterfront promenade where locals gather in the evening, with grilled-food stalls nearby.

A long boat day straight after 113 km of racing is hard on tired legs. Athletes usually keep the day after a race light and save the Underground River trip for a day later.
