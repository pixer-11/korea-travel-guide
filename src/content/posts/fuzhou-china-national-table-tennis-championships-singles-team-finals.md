## Eight days of paddles in Fuzhou

China's strongest players spend a week playing each other. The 2026 China National Table Tennis Championships hold their singles and team finals in Fuzhou, the capital of Fujian, from December 16 to 23, 2026.

International events give you China against the world. The nationals give you something rarer: an all-Chinese draw. Opponents here train in the same system, often in the same halls, and know each other's serves well. Over the week the programme moves between team ties and singles brackets.

## The paddlers on the bill

The field is built from the players who run the world game. Ma Long and Wang Chuqin are among them. Ma Long is the most decorated men's player of his era, a multiple Olympic and world champion whose forehand loop is copied in academies everywhere.

Wang Chuqin is a left-hander with a fast, aggressive backhand, and he carries much of the next generation's weight.

At the nationals, players turn out for their provinces and clubs rather than for Team China. That shifts the mood in the team finals. Teammates from the national squad end up on opposite sides of the net, and regional pride counts for as much as world ranking.

## Tickets, venue and where the details sit

The Chinese Table Tennis Association is the governing body behind the championships. This guide does not name a hall, session times or prices. Those details belong to the organiser and its ticketing partner, and anything printed here would be a guess.

Here is how buying usually works in China:

- **Real-name tickets:** major sporting events usually tie each seat to an ID document. Foreign visitors register with a passport number.
- **Same passport at the gate:** fans carry the passport they registered with, because staff can match it at entry.
- **Phone tickets:** e-tickets inside Alipay or WeChat are the norm. Paper tickets are uncommon.

## Getting into and around Fuzhou

Most international visitors land at Fuzhou Changle International Airport, out on the coast southeast of the city. Taxis and airport coaches cover the run into town.

By rail, Fuzhou sits on the high-speed coastal line, with frequent trains from Xiamen and from Shanghai to the north. Fuzhou has two main stations, Fuzhou Station and Fuzhou South. Metro Line 1 links them and runs north to south through the centre.

Didi, China's ride-hailing app, accepts many international cards. Most visitors rely on it for short trips across town.

## In the stands: how a table tennis crowd behaves

Chinese crowds know this sport well, and the etiquette is clear. A packed hall goes quiet the moment a player tosses the ball to serve. The noise comes between points.

- **Silence during rallies.** Talking or calling out while the ball is in play draws glares.
- **Cheer after the point.** The shout you'll hear is *jiāyóu* (加油), roughly "come on!"
- **Move between points.** Stewards usually hold people in the aisles until a game or rally ends.
- **No flash.** Flash photography distracts the players, and fans keep their phones dim.
- **Pay by phone.** Alipay and WeChat Pay cover snacks and drinks at the hall and around town. Both apps let foreign users link an international card.

Fans of a particular player often bring a small banner or wear their province's colours. During the team finals, some seating blocks fill with supporters of a single province.

## Fuzhou between sessions

December in Fuzhou is far milder than in Beijing, and a light jacket usually covers the evenings. Locals call Fuzhou *Róngchéng*, "Banyan City", and the old banyan trees along its streets make the nickname easy to understand.

Three Lanes and Seven Alleys (Sanfang Qixiang) is the restored old quarter: whitewashed walls, dark-tiled roofs and grand Ming and Qing courtyard houses. It lies a short walk from Dongjiekou Station on Metro Line 1. To the east, Gushan (Drum Mountain) rises over the city, with Yongquan Temple on its slopes.

Two dishes are worth knowing by name:

- **Fuzhou fish balls (鱼丸):** springy fish paste around a pork filling, served in clear broth.
- **Buddha Jumps Over the Wall (佛跳墙):** a slow-braised soup of seafood and meats that started in Fuzhou. Restaurants usually serve it in a lidded clay jar.

Fuzhou also sits over natural hot springs, and bathhouses across the city make good use of a free evening.
