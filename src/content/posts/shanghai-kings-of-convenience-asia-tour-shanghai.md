## Erlend and Eirik

Kings of Convenience are Erlend Øye and Eirik Glambek Bøe, two friends from Bergen on Norway's west coast. Their 2001 album *Quiet Is the New Loud* gave a whole strand of soft, acoustic indie its slogan.

The catalogue is short and well loved:

- *Quiet Is the New Loud* (2001), with "Toxic Girl" and "Failure"
- *Riot on an Empty Street* (2004), home of "Misread", "Cayman Islands" and "Know-How" with Feist
- *Declaration of Dependence* (2009), with "Boat Behind" and "Mrs. Cold"
- *Peace or Love* (2021), their return after a twelve-year gap, featuring "Rocky Trail"

The two sound alike enough that new listeners often can't tell which voice is which. Live, the difference is easier to see. Eirik tends to sit still, and Erlend, with his thick-rimmed glasses, is the one who moves.

## The hush in the room

This music rewards silence, and Kings of Convenience audiences usually give it. There's no loud backing track for chatter to hide under. Two guitars and two voices carry the whole hall, so a conversation three rows back is audible to everyone.

What fans describe from past tours is a show that shifts between that stillness and moments of release. Erlend has a long habit of dancing, leading claps or nudging the crowd into singing a chorus. Songs like "I'd Rather Dance with You" tend to be where the seated politeness breaks. Regulars know the quiet songs and the loud ones get very different crowd behaviour, and they follow along.

## Getting in and getting home in Shanghai

A Shanghai concert night has its own routine. Most of it comes down to phones, IDs and the metro.

**Paying for everything.** Daily life in Shanghai runs on Alipay and WeChat Pay, from the metro gate to the bottle of water outside the hall. Many visitors link an international card to Alipay before they land. Cash still works, but staff at smaller counters often reach for the QR code first.

**At the door.** Security at Chinese concert venues usually includes a bag check, and real-name tickets mean an ID check too. Foreign fans carry the same passport they registered with. A ticket in one name and a passport in another is the snag that slows people down at the gate.

**Phones out, briefly.** Phone recording is a familiar sight at shows everywhere, Shanghai included. At a show this quiet, though, a lit screen held high is more noticeable than at a rock gig, and many fans keep it to one song.

**After the encore.** The Shanghai Metro is the easiest way across the city. Its last trains leave in the late evening, so attendees heading far across town usually keep an eye on the time near the end. Taxis and the DiDi ride-hailing app cover the rest, and DiDi has an English interface.

## Late November in Shanghai

The city in late November is cool and often damp. Daytime temperatures usually sit in the low to mid teens Celsius, and evenings drop lower. A jacket that works indoors and out suits a night of queueing outside and sitting under stage lights.
