## Under the Glass Nave

Paris Photo fills one of the city's great iron halls. The Grand Palais was built for the 1900 Exposition Universelle, and its nave sits under a vaulted roof of glass and riveted steel. On fair days that space is divided into gallery stands: white partition walls, framed prints at eye level, and dealers sitting at small desks near the aisle.

The 2026 edition was set for November 12 to 15. Paris Photo is the leading international fair dedicated to photography, which in practice means galleries from many countries in one building. Vintage silver prints and recent large-format colour work hang within a few metres of each other.

Most of the work is for sale. Buying is not the point for everyone, though. Plenty of visitors treat the fair as the biggest photography exhibition of the year, one that just happens to have price lists.

## Getting to the Grand Palais

The building faces Avenue Winston Churchill, the short avenue that runs from the Champs-Élysées down to the Pont Alexandre III. Two Métro stations are close:

- **Champs-Élysées–Clemenceau** (Lines 1 and 13): the closest, a short walk along the avenue.
- **Franklin D. Roosevelt** (Lines 1 and 9): a little further up the Champs-Élysées, and handy when coming from the west.
- **Invalides** (Lines 8 and 13, plus RER C): across the Pont Alexandre III on the Left Bank, a scenic ten-minute walk over the river.

Taxis and ride-shares can drop off on the surrounding avenues. Traffic around the Champs-Élysées is slow in the late afternoon, so the Métro is usually quicker. November in Paris is cold and often wet, and it gets dark early. The walk over the bridge looks best in the last of the daylight.

## Tickets, Dates and What Is Posted Where

Tickets for the 2026 edition were sold through the fair's official website. The fair also announced daily opening times, an exhibitor list, a floor plan and a programme of talks and signings. Prices and schedules change from year to year.

A Paris Photo ticket covers entry for one fair day. Many people find one visit is enough. Collectors and photography students often come back on a second day, once they've spotted which stands they want to see again.

## Working the Aisles Like a Regular

The hall is big. Regular visitors usually skim the floor plan at the entrance or on their phone, pick a handful of galleries they don't want to miss, and wander between them. Otherwise it's easy to spend two hours in the first third of the hall.

How it works at the stands:

- **"Bonjour" on stepping in.** It's ordinary French courtesy, and dealers notice when it's skipped.
- **The price list.** Prices are rarely printed beside the work. Staff keep a list at the desk and will usually show it to anyone who asks.
- **Photographing the work:** many visitors take phone pictures, but some galleries prefer they don't. A quick question at the desk settles it.
- **Paying:** cards are standard in Paris for anything from a catalogue to a print. Galleries arrange larger purchases directly with the buyer, including shipping.

Bag screening at the entrance is normal for major Paris venues, so a small bag gets through faster. Very few people manage the hall in under three hours. Benches are scarce, and a café break in the middle helps.

## The Photobook Aisles

Paris Photo has a dedicated area for photobook publishers and booksellers, and for many visitors it's the best part of the fair. Tables are stacked with new titles, limited editions and out-of-print volumes. Unlike at the gallery stands, most things can be picked up and leafed through.

Book signings are a regular part of the programme. Photographers sit at publishers' tables, sign copies and talk to readers. The schedule of signings is usually printed in the fair's programme. A signed photobook is also the most affordable way to take something home from Paris Photo, and that's why the queues at the popular tables are often longer than at any gallery stand.

## Photo Days: The Fair Spills Into the City

The 2026 edition was scheduled to overlap with Photo Days, a festival that spreads photography exhibitions across galleries, institutions and less obvious spaces around Paris. In November, the fair sits at the centre of a much larger photography month, and many visitors build a few days around it.

The city's permanent photography venues fit easily into that kind of trip:

- **Jeu de Paume**, in the Tuileries Garden at Place de la Concorde: a twenty-minute walk east from the Grand Palais.
- **Maison Européenne de la Photographie (MEP)**, in the Marais: one of the city's main photography museums.
- **Fondation Henri Cartier-Bresson**, also in the Marais, on Rue des Archives.

Galleries in the Marais and around Saint-Germain-des-Prés often hang their strongest photography shows during fair week. Walking from one to the next makes a good afternoon away from the busy nave.

## Around the Avenue Winston Churchill

There's plenty close to the fair. The **Petit Palais**, also built for the 1900 exhibition, stands directly across the avenue. It houses the City of Paris fine arts museum, and its courtyard garden is a calm place to sit after a few hours under the glass roof.

To the south, the **Pont Alexandre III** crosses the Seine towards Les Invalides, with gilded statues on its pillars. To the north lies the Champs-Élysées, with the nearest cafés and restaurants. They tend to be busy in fair week. For a quieter, cheaper meal, many visitors take Line 1 a few stops east, or cross the river into the 7th.
