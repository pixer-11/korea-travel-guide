Namdaemun Market has been trading since 1964, though merchants set up stalls near this gate long before that. Today it sprawls across dozens of narrow lanes just south of Seoul Station, a grid so dense that first-time visitors often give up trying to map it and just follow the flow of shopping carts instead.

Each alley tends to specialize. One stretch is stacked with imported ginseng and dried anchovies, another with hiking gear and thermal underwear, another with plastic housewares piled to head height. There's a corner known for handmade eyeglasses and another for children's clothing sold by the armful. The market's own signage sorts it into numbered zones, but the honest way to navigate is to pick a gate, walk, and let one stall's smell or color pull you into the next.

## Finding your way from Seoul Station

The market sits at 21 Namdaemunsijang 4-gil in Jung District, a ten-minute walk from Seoul Station's Line 1 or Line 4 exits, or a short walk from Hoehyeon Station (Line 4, Exit 5), which drops you almost directly into the market's south side. Namdaemun Gate itself, the stone-and-timber city gate the market is named for, stands at the market's northern edge on a traffic island, worth five minutes even if you don't go inside.

## Reading the market's rhythm

This is one of Seoul's most-visited markets, not a quiet find, so timing matters more than which entrance you use. Measured foot traffic shows the calmest hours are 9am to 10am, both on weekdays and weekends, right as stalls are rolling up their shutters.

Weekend afternoons are the opposite: crowds build steadily from 11am through closing at 5pm, and the narrow alleys near the food stalls bottleneck fast. If you want photos of the stalls without a wall of shoulders in frame, get there at opening.

Remember the market is closed entirely on Sundays. A Saturday morning visit followed by lunch is a safer bet than assuming it runs seven days.

## What to eat between the stalls

Namdaemun's food alleys are as much a reason to visit as the shopping. Look for:

- **Kalguksu** (hand-cut knife noodles) served from steaming vats at open-air counters
- **Hotteok**, sweet syrup-filled pancakes fried to order, especially in cooler months
- **Galchi jorim**, braised cutlassfish, at the stalls clustered near the market's food-focused lanes
- Grilled mackerel and other seafood plated simply on plastic tables

Most of these stalls have a handful of stools and a single specialty; watch what the stall ahead of you orders if the menu is only in Korean.

## Bargaining and paying like it's expected

Haggling is normal here, especially for clothing, accessories and souvenirs bought in any quantity, but it's a soft negotiation, not a confrontation. Vendors expect a counter-offer; a polite "can you do a little better?" gesture goes further than a hard number.

Cash still moves faster than cards at many of the smaller stalls, though larger shops take cards without issue. Carrying smaller bills speeds up the small purchases that make up most of a market visit, like a bag of roasted chestnuts or a pair of socks.

Few stalls speak English fluently, but pointing, calculators, and hand-signed numbers get transactions done quickly; nobody expects a long conversation over a scarf.

## Making the most of a short visit

Two to three hours covers a decent loop: one pass through the clothing and household lanes, a stop at the food alley, and a walk to the gate. Wear shoes you don't mind standing in for a while, since the stalls are packed tight and there's little seating beyond the food counters.

If you're also headed to Myeongdong's shopping streets or Seoul Station's transit hub, Namdaemun slots neatly into either route, sitting almost exactly between them.
