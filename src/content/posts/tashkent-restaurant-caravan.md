Tashkent has no shortage of plov houses, but Restaurant Caravan built its name on doing two versions of the dish under one roof rather than picking a side. That alone makes it worth a special trip if you're trying to understand why Uzbeks argue about plov the way other countries argue about barbecue.

## What sets Caravan apart

The restaurant spans multiple floors, which gives it more room to maneuver than the typical single-hall plov spot. Downstairs and upstairs dining areas mean groups can be seated together even when the place fills up, and the layout separates the quieter tables from the ones near the entrance and kitchen traffic.

The signature draw is the pairing of two plov styles on one menu. Uzbek plov varies significantly by region and household, from the rice-forward Tashkent style to heavier, meat-and-carrot versions from Samarkand or the Fergana Valley, so a restaurant confident enough to serve two versions side by side is making a statement about its kitchen.

## The room itself

Inside, Caravan leans into what's often described as Eastern elegance: dark wood paneling, carved detailing, and furniture that feels closer to a formal dining room than a canteen. It's a deliberate contrast to the plastic-chair plov stalls that dominate much of the city.

In warmer months, the summer terraces open up as an alternative to the indoor rooms. Outdoor seating in Tashkent restaurants typically runs from late spring through early autumn, weather permitting, so a terrace table is a seasonal option rather than a year-round guarantee.

## A spot with a reputation

Restaurant Caravan has built a name locally as a favorite among celebrities, which shapes the overall experience: expect a more polished service style and a dining room that skews toward special occasions rather than a fast weekday lunch. That reputation also means demand can spike unpredictably, especially on weekend evenings.

If you're the kind of traveler who likes people-watching alongside the meal, this is one of the more reliable places in the city for it. Just don't expect the casual, in-and-out pace of a street-side chaikhana.

## Booking a table

Given the multi-floor layout and its popularity, calling ahead or booking through whatever reservation channel the restaurant currently offers is the safer move, particularly for dinner or weekend visits. Walk-ins are possible but you risk a long wait or a table far from the terrace if the room is already busy.

Because this is a well-regarded, frequently visited restaurant rather than a quiet neighborhood find, treat it as a place where showing up with a plan pays off. Arriving with a group of four or more, in particular, is worth flagging when you book so staff can prepare the right table.

## Ordering the way locals do

Plov here isn't a side dish, it's the reason to come, so build the meal around it. Ask your server to walk you through the two styles on offer if the menu doesn't spell out the difference clearly enough for a first-time visitor.

Uzbek dining culture leans communal: shared plates, tea poured continuously throughout the meal, and dishes arriving in a loose sequence rather than strict courses. Don't rush the table; a proper Uzbek meal, especially one built around plov, is meant to stretch over an hour or more.

Card payment is standard at restaurants of this caliber in Tashkent, but it's sensible to carry some cash in Uzbekistani som as a backup. Tipping isn't as rigidly expected as in some countries, but rounding up or leaving a modest tip for attentive service is common practice at sit-down restaurants in the capital.

Before you go, confirm current opening hours, terrace availability, and whether reservations are required or simply recommended. Details like these shift restaurant to restaurant and season to season in Tashkent, and a quick call or check of the restaurant's own listings will save you a wasted trip.
