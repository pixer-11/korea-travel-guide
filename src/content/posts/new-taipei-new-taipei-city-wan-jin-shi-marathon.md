## Three districts, one coastline

The name is a map. Wan Jin Shi (萬金石) joins the first characters of Wanli (萬里), Jinshan (金山) and Shimen (石門). These are the three districts strung along New Taipei's northern shore, where the island faces the East China Sea.

That coastline is the whole point of the race. Provincial Highway 2 hugs the sea here, with rocky headlands, small fishing harbours and green hills dropping almost straight to the water. Runners spend much of the course with the ocean on one side and the slopes of the Yangmingshan volcanic range on the other.

## A Gold Label field

The 2027 edition takes place in January. It holds a World Athletics Gold Label, one of the top road-race ratings, and that status shows in who lines up at the front. Elite international runners have won here before, including Ethiopia's Fozya Jemal.

Behind the elite pack, the field is mostly amateurs from Taiwan and abroad. For many Taiwanese runners this is a fixture of the winter calendar. For visitors, it is a rare chance to run a labelled marathon through landscape rather than city blocks.

## Entries, dates and the official site

For a marathon, "tickets" means race entries. The race's official site carries the exact date, the start and finish venue, the distances on offer and the registration link. No entry fee is printed here; the current figure is on that site.

Spectating needs no ticket. The course runs along public roads, so anyone can stand at the roadside, within whatever barriers marshals set up.

## North coast weather in January

January on this coast belongs to the northeast monsoon. The wind comes off the sea, the sky is often grey and drizzle is common. Temperatures are cool rather than cold, which suits distance running, though headwinds on exposed stretches can cost time.

Runners usually bring:

- A throwaway layer or rain poncho for the start corral
- Anti-chafe balm, since damp wind makes chafing worse
- A dry change of clothes in the bag drop
- Sunglasses, because conditions can flip to bright between showers

## Race morning on the coast

The rhythm of race day is very Taiwanese. Convenience stores along Highway 2, mostly 7-Eleven and FamilyMart, become runners' breakfast stops for rice balls (飯糰), tea eggs and bananas. Most accept an EasyCard (悠遊卡) as well as cash, which speeds up the pre-race queue.

Spectators cheer with "jiayou!" (加油), literally "add oil". Shouting it back to a crowd, or to a struggling runner beside you, is completely normal. Volunteers at aid stations are polite and quick; a short "xièxie" (thank you) is the standard reply.

Visitors usually sleep on the coast itself rather than in central Taipei, as early starts and coastal roads make a long dawn transfer stressful. Jinshan has the most beds, many in hot-spring hotels. Public baths here are typically nude and gender-separated, and you shower fully before getting in.

## Getting there and staying on

The coast sits roughly an hour from central Taipei by road. Kuo-Kuang intercity buses run from Taipei to Jinshan, and local buses link Keelung and Tamsui along Highway 2. Road closures on race day reshape these routes, and the official site lays out race-day transport.

Few runners leave straight after crossing the line. Nearby stops worth the extra hours:

- **Jinshan Old Street**, for the braised goose-and-duck stalls the town is known for
- **Jinshan's sulphur hot springs**, the obvious post-race cure for tight legs
- **Yeliu Geopark** in Wanli, with its mushroom-shaped sandstone rocks and the Queen's Head formation
- **Shimen Cave**, a sea-carved arch right beside the highway
- **Fuji Fishing Harbor** in Shimen, where seafood restaurants cook the catch you choose from tanks

A lot of finishers spend the next day soaking, eating and walking slowly along the seawall, which is the best recovery plan this coastline offers.
