Every few years Barcelona produces a restaurant that the local food press can't stop mentioning, and for 2026 that restaurant is Melós. It sits in Dreta de l'Eixample, the grid of wide sidewalks and Modernista facades east of Passeig de Gràcia, in a neighborhood better known for design shops and quiet residential blocks than headline openings.

## Who's cooking

Melós is chef Miquel Pardo's first restaurant under his own name. That detail matters more than it might sound: a chef's debut solo project tends to carry a point of view that a group restaurant, built by committee, rarely has.

The food is Catalan at its core but treated with some freedom, built around a tasting menu format rather than an à la carte list. Expect the kind of cooking that reworks familiar regional ingredients into something less predictable than the traditional versions you'd find in an old-town taverna.

## What a meal here looks like

Melós runs on tasting menus rather than plates ordered piecemeal. Evening menus sit on the pricier side for the city, with the exact figure depending on the menu length and any wine pairing you add.

That puts it solidly in special-occasion territory rather than a casual weeknight stop. Because tasting menus depend on a kitchen's daily sourcing, the exact dishes change with the season, so don't go in expecting to look up tonight's plates in advance.

- Format: multi-course tasting menu, evening service
- Price range: on the pricier side for the city
- Style: contemporary Catalan, chef-driven
- Setting: counter-style dining typical of newer tasting-menu openings

## Finding it in Dreta de l'Eixample

Dreta de l'Eixample is the eastern half of the Eixample grid, the district of straight avenues and chamfered corner buildings that Ildefons Cerdà designed in the 1800s. It's a short walk from Passeig de Gràcia and its Gaudí buildings, and well served by the L2, L3, and L4 metro lines depending on which corner you're approaching from.

Because Melós is a recent opening, exact street address details and hours are best confirmed the day you book rather than assumed from an old listing. Restaurant hours and even opening days can still shift in a place's first year.

## Booking a table

New tasting-counter restaurants in Barcelona tend to fill up fast once word gets around, and Melós has already generated enough buzz to make walk-ins a gamble. Book online or by phone as far ahead as the restaurant allows, especially for Friday and Saturday evenings.

If you're flexible, ask about weeknight availability when you call. Newer places sometimes hold back some tables for online booking platforms and others for direct phone reservations, so trying both routes can turn up a table that a single search missed.

## Eating like it's a regular Tuesday, not a tourist stop

Tasting-menu restaurants in Barcelona generally expect diners to commit to the full arc of the meal rather than rushing through courses. Plan for two to three hours at the table, and don't book Melós as a quick pre-theatre dinner.

- Arrive on time; tasting menus are often paced from the kitchen and a late arrival can throw off timing for other diners
- Mention any allergies or dietary restrictions when you book, not when you sit down
- Tipping in Spain is modest; rounding up or leaving a small amount in cash is normal, and it's never expected the way it is in the US
- Dress code is generally smart-casual at this tier of restaurant, without being formal

Because Melós is still new, ask when booking whether pairing wines are included in the quoted price or charged separately, since that detail can change how the final bill compares to the headline menu price.
