## A week of open front doors

Buenos Aires keeps its finest rooms behind heavy doors. Much of the city's grand architecture belongs to private clubs, government bodies and institutions, and on an ordinary day only the façade is visible.

Semana del Patrimonio, Heritage Week, opens those doors for seven days. The 2026 edition was set for November 2 to 8, with historic clubs, palaces and institutional buildings across the city opening their interiors to the public at no charge. These are staircases, ceilings and halls that most porteños never see from the inside either.

## Dates, tickets and how the week runs

The 2026 edition was scheduled from Monday, November 2 to Sunday, November 8. That made five working days and one full weekend, and the two have a different character.

On weekdays, many institutional buildings are still in daily use, and visits fit around that. The weekend usually draws families and local sightseers, so the city feels more like it is on an outing.

Access to the interiors was free. Registration and ticketing arrangements were set building by building. Some heritage visits in Buenos Aires work on a walk-up basis, while others cap numbers for guided groups.

## Where the venues cluster

There is no single venue. The week happens wherever the participating buildings are, and Buenos Aires' heritage stock is concentrated in a few neighbourhoods that are easy to cover on foot.

- **Monserrat and Avenida de Mayo:** the civic spine between Plaza de Mayo and the Congress, lined with Belle Époque and Art Nouveau buildings such as Palacio Barolo.
- **San Telmo:** the oldest residential quarter, with cobbled streets, colonial-era houses and former mansions.
- **Retiro:** around Plaza San Martín, home to aristocratic palaces from the early twentieth century, including Palacio San Martín.
- **Recoleta:** the city's grandest residential district, built in the French style.

Not every building in these areas takes part in a given edition. Participation changes from year to year.

## Moving between buildings by Subte

The Subte, Buenos Aires' metro, links most of the heritage districts. Fares are paid with a rechargeable SUBE card, which also works on city buses.

- **Line A** runs directly beneath Avenida de Mayo. Its stations, including Perú, Piedras and Lima, make it easy to work along the avenue building by building.
- **Line C** connects Retiro with Avenida de Mayo and Constitución, passing near San Telmo.
- **Line E** starts at Bolívar beside Plaza de Mayo and heads south-west.
- **Line D** starts at Catedral, also on Plaza de Mayo, and runs north-west towards Recoleta.

Plaza de Mayo is where Lines A, D and E meet, which makes it a natural base. Between San Telmo and Monserrat, walking is usually quicker than any train.

## Jacaranda season on the street outside

Early November is late spring in Buenos Aires, and the jacarandas are in flower. The city planted these trees widely along its avenues and in its plazas, and in this season the canopies turn violet. Fallen blossom gathers on the pavements.

That gives Heritage Week a second layer. The queue outside a palace often stands under a purple tree, and the walk between two buildings is part of the outing. Plaza San Martín in Retiro and the avenues of Recoleta and Palermo have some of the densest plantings.

Days are generally warm and long at this time of year. Showers are not unusual, though, and a light layer and something for rain cover the range.

## Standing in line for a palace

Queueing works the Buenos Aires way: an orderly line on the pavement, often longer than it first looks.

- **Greetings:** a simple "buenas" or "buen día" at the door is normal courtesy here and opens conversations.
- **Identification:** government and institutional buildings in the city commonly ask visitors for a passport or ID at security.
- **Language:** guided visits in public buildings are usually led in Spanish. Visitors who don't speak it often carry a short written history of the building on their phone.
- **Bags:** security at official buildings often means bag checks, and a small bag gets through faster.
- **Photography:** rules vary by room. Some rooms permit it and others don't, and attendants enforce this.

Locals usually pick two or three buildings for a day rather than chasing a long list. The lines at the best-known palaces tend to build through the afternoon and on the weekend. Visitors who get the most out of the week usually start with the building they most want to see, then fill in with smaller ones nearby.

## Building a day around it

Heritage Week pairs easily with the rest of central Buenos Aires. Plaza de Mayo puts the Casa Rosada, the Metropolitan Cathedral and the Cabildo within one square. Café Tortoni, a historic café, stands on Avenida de Mayo for a mid-route stop.

The closing day, November 8, fell on a Sunday, when San Telmo's street fair runs along Calle Defensa, a short walk south of Plaza de Mayo. In Recoleta, the Recoleta Cemetery and the Basílica del Pilar sit next to each other, so a day of interiors can end among some of the city's most elaborate funerary architecture.

Two full days covers a good handful of interiors. The seven-day schedule left room for a slower pace, one neighbourhood at a time.
