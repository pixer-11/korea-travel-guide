## Ramparts after dark

This is a haunted tour inside an 18th-century fortress. The Fortaleza del Real Felipe went up between 1747 and 1774, after the 1746 earthquake and tsunami flattened colonial Callao. It was named for Philip V of Spain. The fort is a pentagon of thick stone walls, with five bastions around a large central parade ground.

Two round towers anchor the design: the Torreón del Rey and the Torreón de la Reina. The fortress was the last Spanish royalist stronghold in South America. Brigadier José Ramón Rodil held it under siege until January 1826, and that history of hunger and disease inside the walls is where the ghost stories start.

## Dates and the setting

The Tour del Miedo Nocturno runs from **December 7, 2026 to January 30, 2027**. That puts it in Lima's summer, when evenings are mild and the breeze comes in off the Pacific. Session times and ticket sales sit with the organiser, not with the fortress's daytime museum ticket desk.

By day the fort holds the Museo del Ejército, which the Peruvian Army runs, so the tour uses real military ground and not a built set. What you walk through:

- Stone casemates and vaulted passages inside the curtain walls
- The open parade ground, which is dark and exposed at night
- Bastion ramparts that look out toward the port and the sea

## Reaching Plaza Independencia at night

The fortress stands on Plaza Independencia in Callao, close to the La Punta peninsula. From Miraflores the ride usually takes 30–45 minutes, depending on traffic on Avenida Costanera or Avenida La Marina. Jorge Chávez International Airport is also in Callao, so you could combine a late arrival or a last night in Lima with the tour.

Most attendees use Uber, Cabify or InDrive and go door to door. Parts of central Callao are not places to wander on foot after dark, and visitors generally avoid walking between the fortress and other districts at night. Once the tour ends, people usually request the return ride from the plaza itself.

## Moving through the fort with a group

Haunted tours in Peru are generally led in Spanish, with actors who use the fortress's real corridors to stage their scares. If you don't speak Spanish, the atmosphere still lands, but the historical storytelling between scenes will be harder to follow. Many visitors read up on the Rodil siege beforehand for context.

A few things attendees usually do:

- **Wear closed shoes.** The parade ground and passages are uneven stone and packed earth.
- **Keep phones at low brightness.** Screen glare breaks the darkness the actors rely on, and guides at haunted events commonly ask for flash to be turned off.
- **Stay with the group.** The casemates and bastions connect in ways that are easy to lose track of in the dark.
- **Carry a digital wallet or a card for snacks.** Peruvians widely pay with Yape and Plin, and vendors near plazas often take them.

Daytime visitors often pair the fortress with La Punta's seafront promenade or the painted streets of Callao Monumental. After dark, groups generally treat the tour as a single outing with a taxi at each end.
