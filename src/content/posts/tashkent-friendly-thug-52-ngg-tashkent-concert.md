## The 52 in the name

Russian rap tags often carry numbers, and they usually point to a place. In Russia, 52 is the vehicle registration code for Nizhny Novgorod Oblast, the region on the Volga that the act claims as home. NGG belongs to the same crew identity, and fans chant the letters as readily as the name.

That matters in Tashkent. Russian is still the everyday second language of the capital, so Russian-language rap draws a home crowd here rather than an expat one. Expect an audience that knows the words. Many are students and people in their twenties from across the city.

## Yunusabad after dark

Yunusabad was built mostly in the late Soviet decades. Its streets are long, its blocks are wide, and its avenues run broad between apartment towers. It is not a nightlife quarter. It does hold some of the city's biggest modern venues, though, including Humo Arena, the ice hockey arena that also hosts large concerts.

A few landmarks help you get your bearings on the way in:

- **Minor Mosque**, the white marble mosque on the bank of the Ankhor canal, near Minor metro station
- **Tashkent TV Tower**, the steel lattice tower on the district's southern edge, visible from most of the north side
- **Amir Temur Square**, the central roundabout, a short metro ride south and the usual meeting point before heading up

## Moving north on the blue line

The Yunusobod line is the metro route into the district. It runs north from the centre through stations including Minor, Abdulla Qodiriy, Bodomzor, Shahriston and Turkiston, and ends at Yunusobod. Tashkent's stations are worth a look on their own: Soviet-era halls with marble, chandeliers and themed mosaics on the older lines.

Most concertgoers combine the metro with a ride-hailing car for the final stretch. **Yandex Go** is the dominant app in Tashkent. It shows the fare up front and works in English, which spares you any negotiating with street taxis.

After the show, demand for cars spikes all at once. Plenty of fans walk a few blocks away from the venue before ordering, where pickup points are less jammed. Others ride back on the metro, so keep in mind that it stops running at night.

## December cold, and what people wear

Early December in Tashkent is properly cold. Daytime temperatures hover just above freezing and nights drop below it. Snow sometimes falls, and the dry continental air bites harder than the number suggests.

Venues have cloakrooms, and Tashkent crowds use them; locals call it the *garderob*. A heavy coat is normal on the street and gets checked in at the door. Inside a packed hall the temperature climbs quickly, so a thin layer underneath is what most people dance in.

**Paying.** The currency is the Uzbek som. Local cards run on the **Uzcard** and **Humo** networks, and Visa and Mastercard are now widely accepted in central Tashkent. Small kiosks and some bar counters still prefer cash, so most visitors carry a few notes.

**Language.** Russian gets you furthest at a show like this; Uzbek is the state language and always welcome. "Spasibo" (Russian) and "rahmat" (Uzbek) both mean thank you, and both land well with security staff and bar crews.

**Security.** Door checks at large Tashkent venues usually include a bag search and metal detector. Bulky bags, outside drinks and professional cameras are the items typically turned away. Travelling light gets you through the line faster.

**Tipping.** Tipping is not expected at concert bars. In sit-down restaurants a service charge is often already added to the bill.

## Around the show: food and a longer stay

Yunusabad has the plain, filling food of a residential district. That means **plov** centres, **somsa** ovens and shashlik grills. Plov is traditionally a lunchtime dish in Tashkent, and the big plov houses often sell out of the day's cauldron by afternoon. An evening meal before a show is more likely to be shashlik, **lagman** noodles or somsa.
