## Why go

You step off the ferry and the noise of the mainland just falls away. Nami Island is shaped like a half-moon dropped into the Han River, and its whole reputation rests on a few long, straight avenues of trees — the metasequoia row and the ginkgo lane — planted decades ago and now grown into a cathedral of trunks. This is the island that launched a thousand honeymoon photos after the Korean drama "Winter Sonata" filmed here in 2002, and it still draws couples, school groups, and camera-toting visitors from across Asia in numbers that make it one of Gangwon-do's most-visited attractions. It isn't a hidden find — it's a genuine crowd magnet — but the reason it keeps its pull is simple: the tree tunnels really are that photogenic, in every season.

## Getting there

Nami Island sits administratively closer to Gapyeong, Gyeonggi-do, than to Chuncheon city itself, but it's almost always bundled into Chuncheon-area itineraries since it's roughly midway between the two and often paired with a same-day Chuncheon visit for dakgalbi (spicy stir-fried chicken). From Seoul, take the ITX-Cheongchun or regular commuter train on the Gyeongchun Line to Gapyeong Station, then a short taxi or shuttle bus to Gapyeong Wharf (Namiseom Ferry Terminal). The island itself has no bridge for pedestrians — you arrive either by a five-minute ferry crossing or by the zip-line (Nami Island Zipwire) that swoops in from the Gapyeong side and drops you near the entrance. Round-trip ferry tickets typically double as your island entry fee; confirm current pricing at the terminal since it's adjusted periodically.

## What to see and do

Once inside, the island unfolds as a loop you can walk in under two hours, though most people take longer. The Metasequoia Lane and the Ginkgo Tree Road are the postcard shots, especially with morning side-light filtering through the leaves — golden in late October and November, bare and frost-rimmed in deep winter, fresh green in May. Scattered through the grounds are small galleries, an outdoor sculpture garden, a UNICEF handprint pond, and stages that host seasonal festivals — a fire-and-light show in winter, craft markets in warmer months. Ostriches, squirrels, and peacocks wander semi-freely near the central lawns, which surprises first-time visitors who expect a purely manicured park. Bike rentals let you cover the perimeter faster, and there are enough cafés and a bakery for a slow lunch by the water. Give yourself three to four hours minimum; a full day is easy if you linger for photos.

## When to go

Each season gives the island a different personality. Snow blankets the tree lanes from late December through February and is the single most-photographed version of Nami Island, but it's also the coldest and busiest holiday stretch. Cherry blossoms arrive briefly in early April, and the metasequoia and ginkgo trees peak for autumn color from mid-October to mid-November — that autumn window is when crowds are heaviest and weekend ferries can mean real queuing. Summer is greener and quieter in comparison, with river breezes making the walk more comfortable than the exposed heat suggests.

## How to visit like a local

Because this is a very-popular, well-trafficked attraction rather than a quiet corner of Gangwon-do, the smartest strategy is timing, not secrecy. Arrive on the first ferry of the day on a weekday if you can — tour buses from Seoul tend to land mid-morning, and by early afternoon the main tree avenues are shoulder-to-shoulder with visitors angling for the same shot. Weekends and the peak autumn-foliage weeks are worst; if you must go then, plan to arrive before the gates properly wake up. Payment on the island runs mostly on card and mobile pay these days, though it's worth carrying some cash for small vendor stalls during festivals. There's no formal reservation system for entry — it's pay-and-board at the wharf — but zip-line slots and some seasonal events do sell out, so book those ahead online if you have your heart set on the aerial approach. The most common visitor mistake is treating Nami Island as a quick photo-stop: rushing the loop in under an hour means missing the quieter side paths away from the main avenue, where the crowds thin out even at peak times.
