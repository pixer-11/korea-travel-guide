## Nine days on the riverbank

The festival has no single gate. The 2026 fall edition runs from Saturday, October 17 to Sunday, October 25, spread along the Hangang River parks that line both banks of the river through Seoul. Most of its programmes are free to enter. That changes how you approach it: you pick a stretch of riverbank and walk into it, and nobody scans a ticket.

The parks themselves are wide strips of lawn, cycle path and concrete promenade set between the water and the elevated expressways. Bridges cross overhead every kilometre or so. On festival days the parks fill with stages, activity tents and picnic mats. The rest of the riverbank carries on as usual, with cyclists, runners and families who may not know a festival is on.

## Picking your park by subway exit

Seoul's riverside parks have names, and each sits close to a station. Since the festival is spread out, most people choose one park and stay there. These are the ones most visitors reach on foot from the subway:

- **Yeouido Hangang Park**: Yeouinaru Station, Line 5. The biggest lawns, beside the Yeouido office towers.
- **Ttukseom Hangang Park**: Jayang Station, Line 7. Exits 2 and 3 open straight onto the riverbank, so it's the easiest park to reach in bad weather.
- **Banpo Hangang Park**: walkable from Express Bus Terminal Station (Lines 3, 7 and 9). It sits under Banpo Bridge and the Moonlight Rainbow Fountain.
- **Mangwon Hangang Park**: Mangwon Station, Line 6. It's a short walk through the Mangwon-dong neighbourhood, past its traditional market.
- **Nanji Hangang Park**: near World Cup Stadium in Mapo, and quieter and greener than Yeouido.

The organiser, the Seoul Metropolitan Government, publishes the festival programme on its Hangang park channels, and the programme says which activities happen at which park. A T-money card covers the subway, buses and the city's Ttareungyi rental bikes. Docks for those bikes stand at the entrances to every park.

## October light on the water

Late October is one of the best times of year to sit by the Han. The summer humidity has gone, and the zelkova and ginkgo trees along the embankments are turning. Days are usually dry and clear. After dark, though, the riverbank is exposed to the wind, and the temperature drops fast.

The sun sets around six in late October, so the evening comes earlier than many visitors expect. Seoulites usually bring an extra layer and a blanket along with the picnic mat, even when the afternoon felt warm. Once the bridges light up, the river turns into a band of reflected colour between Yeouido and Banpo. Many people come for this part of the evening.

The festival runs over two weekends, so the Saturdays and Sundays are when families spread across the lawns. On the weekday days in between, the parks belong more to office workers on an after-work walk.

## Ramyeon from the riverside machine

The Han has its own food ritual. The convenience stores inside the parks have instant-noodle cooking machines. You buy a packet of ramyeon and a paper bowl, put the noodles in, and set the bowl under the machine. It adds hot water and boils everything for a few minutes. Then you carry the bowl outside and eat it beside the water. Korean dramas have made this a pilgrimage for visitors.

The other riverbank habit is chimaek, fried chicken with beer. Couriers bring orders to designated delivery points at the park edges, marked with signs. People order through Korean delivery apps, which can be hard to use without a local phone number. If you can't use them, the convenience store is the easy option: it sells chicken, gimbap, snacks and cans, and it has a seating area.

## How the lawn works on festival days

Hangang picnics come with a few unwritten rules and some posted ones. Here's what people usually bring:

- A picnic mat, sold in the park convenience stores and often rented from riverside vendors
- A light blanket or padded jacket for after sunset
- A bag for rubbish, because bins fill up fast on busy evenings
- A T-money card, which also pays at most park stores

Pop-up shade tents are only allowed in the marked zones. The park rules say at least two sides must stay open, and enforcement officers walk the lawns. People sort their rubbish into the recycling, general waste and food waste bins when they leave. Leaving chicken boxes on the grass gets you frowned at.

When a performance draws a crowd, people sit down on mats rather than standing in front of others. Saving space with an empty mat is common. Spreading out across a walking path is not. Card and mobile payment work almost everywhere along the river, though some small festival stalls prefer cash.

## Getting between the parks

The festival covers kilometres of riverbank, so moving between sites is part of the visit. The riverside cycle path runs unbroken along both banks. A Ttareungyi bike gets you from Yeouido to Banpo in well under an hour of easy riding. Bikes go back to any dock.

The Hangang Bus ferry is another way to travel along the water between the main parks. The subway is faster when the parks are on opposite banks. Crossing a bridge on foot is slow and noisy. The Han is wide and the bridges are long.

## Beyond the festival grounds

Each park opens onto a different part of Seoul. From Yeouido, the 63 Building and the Yeouido Saetgang Ecological Park are close by, and The Hyundai Seoul department store is a short walk inland. From Banpo, you can cross the lower deck of Jamsu Bridge on foot towards Yongsan.

Mangwon Hangang Park sits next to Mangwon-dong's cafés and its market. From there it's a short subway ride to Hapjeong and Hongdae for the evening. Ttukseom is a single stop from Seoul Forest and the converted warehouses of Seongsu-dong, which many visitors combine with an afternoon at the river.
