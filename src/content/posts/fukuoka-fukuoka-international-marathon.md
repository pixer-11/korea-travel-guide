## An elite race with a long record

Fukuoka is one of road running's oldest proving grounds. The race began in 1947, and its IAAF Gold Label status puts it among the top-ranked marathons in the world. It is a men's race for qualified elite runners. Strict qualifying times keep the field small and fast.

The record books explain why Japanese running fans treat it with near-reverence:

- Derek Clayton ran a world best here in 1967, the first marathon under 2:10.
- Frank Shorter won four straight editions from 1971 to 1974.
- Toshihiko Seko won it four times, and Rob de Castella set a world best here in 1981.

The 2026 edition falls on December 6. This is a different event from the Fukuoka Marathon, the mass-participation race the city holds in November. Visitors confuse the two all the time.

## Where the race happens

The Fukuoka International Marathon has traditionally started and finished at Heiwadai Athletic Stadium in Chuo Ward. The stadium sits between Ohori Park and the stone walls of the Fukuoka Castle ruins. From there the route heads northeast through the city and out along Hakata Bay before turning back.

For a fixed viewing spot, most spectators use the Fukuoka City Subway Kuko Line. Akasaka and Ohorikoen stations are both a short walk from the stadium. Tenjin, one stop further, has the most hotels and food near the course. The bayside stretches are flat and open, so runners can be seen coming from a long way off.

## Watching from the kerb

Roadside viewing costs nothing and needs no ticket. That is how nearly everyone sees the race. Spectators line the pavements two or three deep near the stadium, and the crowd thins out further along the bay. Many fans watch the first pass in the city, then take the subway to see the runners return.

Japanese television carries the race live, which suits the long middle section. Fans in cafés and hotel rooms follow the pack on screen and walk out for the final kilometres. Early December mornings in Fukuoka are cool, and spectators usually dress for standing still, not for walking.

## Kerbside manners in Fukuoka

The crowd here follows clear unwritten rules. People stay behind the barriers or the kerb line. They cross the road only at points where marshals in hi-vis vests hold back traffic and let people across in groups. Stepping into the road while the lead pack approaches is the one mistake that draws real disapproval.

What locals typically do:

- Cheer "ganbare!" (keep going) for every runner, Japanese or not, and keep clapping long after the leaders pass.
- Keep their voices down near the start and save the noise for the finish.
- Take their rubbish home, because Fukuoka streets have very few public bins.
- Eat afterwards at the yatai food stalls in Nakasu and Tenjin, which open in the evening.

Most people pay by IC card (Hayakaken, Suica and others work on the subway) and use cash at the yatai. Tipping is not part of Japanese custom anywhere along the course.
