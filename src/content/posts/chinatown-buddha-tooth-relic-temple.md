Four storeys of dark timber and red lacquer rise above the shophouses on South Bridge Road, built in the style of Tang dynasty temples even though the building itself is only from 2007. It's easy to spot: the roofline alone, tiered and heavy with carved eaves, stands out from the low, pastel-painted shopfronts around it in the heart of Chinatown.

Inside, the ground floor holds the main hall, where a large seated Buddha Maitreya presides over a room thick with the smell of sandalwood incense. Devotees move along the sides with joss sticks, bowing at stations set into the floor plan; visitors are welcome to walk the perimeter and watch without joining in.

## The relic and the rooftop

The temple's namesake relic, said to be a tooth of the historical Buddha, sits in a stupa on the top floor, cast in nearly 320 kilograms of gold and kept behind glass in a room visitors can enter but not photograph.

One floor down is a quieter payoff: a rooftop garden with a large prayer wheel, bonsai trees and a viewing deck looking over Chinatown's roofs. It's one of the few genuinely peaceful spots in this part of the district, and worth the extra flight of stairs.

## The museum floors in between

The second and third floors house a Buddhist culture museum, with galleries of statues, manuscripts and ritual objects from across Asia. None of it feels rushed or crammed in; the rooms are laid out gallery-style, with English signage explaining what you're looking at.

Give yourself time here rather than treating it as a corridor between the ground floor and the roof. Most visitors spend 45 minutes to an hour total in the building; add 15 minutes if the museum floors interest you.

## Getting there and getting in

The temple is at 288 South Bridge Road, a five to seven minute walk from Chinatown MRT station (exit A). It's also walkable from Maxwell Food Centre and the Sri Mariamman Temple, making it easy to fold into a wider Chinatown loop.

- Open daily, 7am to 5pm
- Entry is free; a donation box sits near the entrance for those who wish to contribute
- Modest dress is required: shoulders and knees covered, and shawls are usually available to borrow at the door if you arrive underdressed
- Shoes stay on for the main hall but come off before entering some upper rooms; follow the signage at each threshold

## Avoiding the crowds

This is one of the most-visited religious sites in Chinatown, and it shows between late morning and mid-afternoon. Weekends get busiest from 11am to 4pm, when tour groups and day-trippers stack up on the stairwells and in front of the relic room.

Both weekdays and weekends are quietest from 7am to 10am, right after opening. Arrive then and you'll often have the rooftop garden nearly to yourself, with the prayer wheel and bonsai terraces free of the queues that build later.

## Temple etiquette worth knowing

Speak quietly throughout; this is an active place of worship, not just a museum, and prayer continues on the ground floor all day. Photography is generally fine in the main hall and museum galleries but is restricted around the relic itself, so watch for posted signs rather than assuming.

If you want to light incense or make an offering, watch how regulars do it near the urns before joining in; there's no fixed script, but following the flow avoids disrupting anyone mid-prayer. Cash is the simplest way to donate, since there's no expectation of card payment inside a temple setting.

Combine the visit with a walk down Pagoda Street or a stop at nearby Chinatown Complex Market for lunch afterward. It sits comfortably alongside a couple of other Chinatown stops in a single morning, especially if you start here before the heat and crowds of midday build up.
