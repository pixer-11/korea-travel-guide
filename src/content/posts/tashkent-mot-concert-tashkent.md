## Getting to Humo Arena

Most concert-goers arrive by taxi. Yandex Go is the standard ride-hailing app in Tashkent. It shows the fare upfront, accepts the destination in Latin or Cyrillic script, and saves explaining the route in Russian or Uzbek. Searching "Humo Arena" brings up the venue directly.

The Tashkent Metro is famous for its ornate Soviet-era stations and worth riding for its own sake. For the arena itself, though, a car to the door is simpler, especially for a late finish. After a big show, thousands of people request rides at once. Many fans walk a few streets away from the entrance before opening the app, so their driver can actually reach them.

October is a pleasant month in Tashkent. Days are usually warm and dry, but evenings cool down noticeably. That matters when stepping out of a heated arena late at night, so visitors generally carry a light layer.

## Plov first, concert second

Plov keeps its own hours. Uzbekistan's national rice dish of carrots, lamb, chickpeas and cumin is traditionally a midday meal. Big plov houses cook it in enormous cauldrons in the morning and serve until it runs out, usually by mid-afternoon.

The best-known is the Central Asian Plov Centre, often called Besh Qozon, near the Tashkent TV Tower. Rows of wide kazan pots steam outside and the dining halls fill at lunch. Lunch there before an evening show is a long-standing Tashkent habit, because by evening the cauldrons are empty.

By dinnertime, the city's other dishes take over:

- **Shashlik**: skewers grilled over charcoal, sold everywhere from street stalls to sit-down restaurants
- **Samsa**: flaky pastries filled with meat or pumpkin, often baked in clay tandyr ovens
- **Lagman**: hand-pulled noodles in a rich broth, a filling pre-show option

## Filling the hours before doors

A night concert leaves the day free for the city. These are the classic daytime stops:

- **Chorsu Bazaar**: the domed market in the old town, with stalls of dried fruit, spices, bread and fresh produce
- **Hazrati Imam complex**: a religious ensemble of madrasas and mosques, known for housing an early Quran manuscript
- **Amir Timur Square**: the leafy centre of the modern city, with the equestrian statue of Timur
- **Tashkent TV Tower**: the tallest structure in Central Asia, close to the plov centre

The bazaar and Hazrati Imam pair well in the morning. From there it is a ride across town for plov, which still leaves the afternoon free before an evening at the arena.

The rhythm is familiar to anyone who has been to an arena show. A few local details stand out.

**Security and entry.** Large venues in Tashkent run bag and body checks at the doors. Fans usually carry a small bag and leave behind anything bulky, like big power banks or professional cameras. Queues at the entrances build as doors approach, and the people who walk in calmly tend to be the ones who got there with time to spare.

**Language.** MOT's stage banter is in Russian. Staff and stewards speak Russian and Uzbek. A few Russian words go a long way: *spasibo* (thank you), *gde vkhod?* (where is the entrance?), *moyo mesto* (my seat).

**Paying.** Uzbekistan runs on the som. Local cards on the Humo and Uzcard networks dominate, and international Visa and Mastercard work in bigger venues but not everywhere. Visitors usually carry some som in cash for taxis, kiosks and the bazaar.

**The phone-light moment.** At arena shows here, thousands of phone torches go up across the tiered stands for the slower songs. The view from the upper rows is often better than from the floor.

**Leaving.** Locals tend to linger for a few minutes after the lights come up rather than joining the first surge for the exits. The taxi rush fades fast, and the walk out is easier once it does.
