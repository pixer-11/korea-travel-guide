## A grass slope under a white turbine

The landmark gives the place its name. A tall white wind turbine stands on the headland above Ya Nui, and the hillside below it drops away towards the Andaman Sea. There's no ticket gate or boardwalk. You get a roadside pull-off, a kiosk, and grass sloping towards a wide stretch of water.

Most people come for the hour around sunset, and you can see why within a minute of parking. The sun goes down over open sea, and the coastline curves away on both sides. Google currently lists the site as open 24 hours. In practice it's an unfenced lookout beside a public road, not a venue with doors.

## Reading the view

Face the water and get your bearings. Here's what's in front of you:

- **Ya Nui Beach**, the small cove directly below, popular for snorkelling and kayaking
- **Koh Man**, the small island just offshore from Ya Nui
- **Promthep Cape (Laem Phromthep)**, the long headland reaching south towards the open sea
- **Nai Harn Bay**, curving away to the north behind you

The breeze is steady here, which is why someone built a turbine. It also keeps the slope cooler than the beaches below in the late afternoon. Bring a layer if you plan to stay after dark. On a clear evening the light catches the islands first and the water last, and the colours keep changing for a while after the sun disappears.

## The sunset question: here or Promthep Cape?

Most visitors to the south coast face this choice. Promthep Cape, a few minutes' drive further along the same road, is Phuket's most famous sunset point. It has a lighthouse, a shrine and a large car park, and it draws tour buses on most evenings.

Windmill Viewpoint is busy too, so don't expect solitude. Still, it's mostly a grass slope with a road behind it rather than a built-up terrace, so it feels looser. People sit on the grass and spread out along the ridge. You also get Promthep Cape itself in the frame as a silhouette, which you can't do while standing on it.

You can do both if you have your own wheels. Watch the sun go down from one, then drive to the other for the afterglow. The two are close enough that the light is still worth seeing when you arrive.

## Getting to the Ya Nui ridge

There's no rail or metro in Phuket, so you'll need road transport. The viewpoint sits on the coastal road (Route 4233) that loops between Nai Harn and Promthep Cape in Rawai, at the far south of the island. From Phuket Town it's roughly a 30–40 minute drive. From the airport, allow well over an hour.

Your options:

- **Scooter or rental car**: the most flexible choice, and there's roadside parking at the lookout. The road up is steep and winding, so take it slowly if you're new to riding.
- **Grab or Bolt**: easy to book there. Getting a ride back after sunset can take longer, because many people leave at once.
- **Songthaew**: the blue open-sided buses from Phuket Town run towards Rawai and Nai Harn. They don't go all the way up, and they stop running in the evening, so check the return before relying on one.
- **Taxi with a wait**: agree on the waiting time and fare with the driver before you set off.

Type "Windmill Viewpoint" into Google Maps or Grab and you'll land at the right pull-off. Its plus code is Q894+VG9.

## Claiming your spot before the sun drops

This is a popular spot, so a little planning helps. Sunset in Phuket falls roughly between 6pm and 7pm through the year. Aim to arrive about an hour before it to get a good patch of grass and room to park. Arrive at the last minute and you'll be standing behind phones near the roadside.

A few habits that help here:

- **Sit on the slope rather than at the road edge.** People settle on the grass, and the view opens up as you move down.
- **Bring your own drink and snacks, or buy from the kiosk.** Small purchases are normally paid in Thai baht cash. The kiosk's offerings and prices are on display there.
- **Park neatly along the road.** Scooters bunch together near the kiosk. Leave room for cars getting through, because the road stays in use.
- **Take your rubbish with you.** There's little in the way of bins on an open hillside, and it's a public space.
- **Wait for the afterglow.** Many visitors leave as soon as the sun touches the water, which jams the road. The 15 minutes after sunset often bring the strongest colour.

For a quieter visit, come in daylight. Late morning or early afternoon gives you the same view with clearer water colours and far fewer people. It's also hotter, with little shade.

## When the sky cooperates

The season decides how good your sunset is. Phuket's dry season runs roughly from November to April. That's when you're most likely to get a clean horizon and a clear view out over the Andaman Sea.

From about May to October the southwest monsoon brings cloud, rain squalls and a rougher sea. Evenings are hit and miss. Sometimes a storm cloud breaks up and the sunset beats any in the dry season, but often the sun just disappears into grey. Check the cloud cover on the day before you make a trip south. Bring rain protection for the scooter ride home.

Plan for 45 minutes to an hour and a half. That's enough to arrive, settle in, watch the sunset and leave before the road gets busy.

## Before or after: the Rawai loop

The viewpoint fits neatly into an afternoon on the island's southern tip. One route that works:

- **Ya Nui Beach**: swim or snorkel in the cove below in the afternoon, then drive up the hill.
- **Nai Harn Beach and Nai Harn Lake**: a wide bay a short ride north. Locals and long-stay residents walk and jog around the lake.
- **Promthep Cape**: the lighthouse and headland, just south along the same road.
- **Rawai seafront**: the seafood market and restaurants along the Rawai waterfront are an easy stop for dinner on the way back.

If you're coming from Patong or Kata, the coast road south passes the Karon and Kata Noi lookouts on the way. You get a run of sea views before you reach the turbine.
