## Who you are watching

Lolita Milyavskaya first became famous in the late 1980s and 1990s as half of Kabare-duet Akademiya, the comic cabaret duo she formed with Alexander Tsekalo. After the duo split she built a solo career. Her stage persona is big-voiced, theatrical and openly self-mocking.

Her solo hits include "Na Titanike" ("On the Titanic"), the dance-pop single that brought her to a younger audience in the late 2000s. Her shows sit between a pop concert and a cabaret evening. Expect long stretches of talk between songs, delivered in Russian, with the jokes landing on the audience as often as on herself.

- **Language on stage:** Russian, throughout
- **Who comes:** mostly adults who grew up with her songs, plus friends they bring along
- **The mood:** sing-along choruses, applause mid-song, standing for the encore

## Bouquets at the stage edge

This tradition defines a Russian-language pop concert in Tashkent. Fans bring flowers, and they deliver them in person. During the slower songs, people walk down the aisles, often several at once, and hand bouquets up to the singer at the front of the stage.

Performers like Lolita expect this and usually pause, bend down and take each one. Sometimes there is a quick kiss on the cheek or a word into the microphone. By the encore, the stage edge is often lined with wrapped roses and chrysanthemums.

Flower kiosks around Tashkent's metro stations and markets sell bouquets wrapped in cellophane. These are what most concertgoers carry in. Odd numbers of stems are the custom for celebrations across the former Soviet world. Even numbers are for funerals, and locals notice.

## December in the Uzbek capital

Early December in Tashkent is properly cold after dark, often close to freezing, though days can be clear and bright. The plane trees along Amir Temur Square and the boulevards around it are bare by then. The city's New Year decorations usually start appearing in public squares during the month.

For visitors flying in for the concert, a day either side gives time for the city's sights. Chorsu Bazaar, under its turquoise dome, is busiest in the morning, with dried fruit, nuts and fresh non bread stacked high. The Hazrati Imam complex in the old town is a short taxi ride from there.

The Tashkent Metro is worth riding for its own sake, especially the Soviet-era stations decorated with chandeliers, mosaics and carved stone. Kosmonavtlar station on the Uzbekistan line, with its blue cosmonaut medallions, is the one most people photograph.

## Where visitors usually stay

Most concert visitors base themselves in central Tashkent, around Amir Temur Square and the streets towards the Mustaqillik metro area. Hotels there sit close to several metro lines, which keeps the journey to any of the city's concert halls short.

Yunusabad, to the north, has more newer business hotels and apartments. Chilonzor, to the south-west, is residential and quieter, with plenty of rental flats. From any of these, a taxi across the city after a show is a normal and inexpensive way home by local standards.
