## Ten days, one long room of clocks

Picture rows of tables and identical boards, each with a digital clock beside it. That is what the MICE Center in Quezon City holds from November 17 to 26, 2026. The AQ Prime International Open Chess Championship is a FIDE-rated open. Every result goes onto the players' international ratings, and the strongest performances can count toward a title.

An open draws a wide field. Titled players sit a few rows away from ambitious club players and juniors chasing their first international result. The top boards get most of the attention. The real stories of the event are often further back in the hall.

## The norm hunt

This tournament offers norm chances for three titles: Grandmaster (GM), International Master (IM) and Woman International Master (WIM). A norm is one qualifying performance toward a FIDE title. A player usually needs several of them, plus a minimum rating, before the title is awarded.

Norms are hard to get. FIDE requires a minimum number of rounds and enough titled opponents. It also requires opponents from several different national federations. Only an international field like this one can supply all of that. Without such events, a player has to fly abroad to chase norms.

That changes how the final rounds feel. A player on a norm path can need one exact result, a win or sometimes just a draw, in the last game. The arbiters and other players know who is in contention. In the closing days, watch the boards where a crowd quietly gathers.

## Why Quezon City suits a chess open

The Philippines has a long chess tradition. Eugene Torre became Asia's first grandmaster in 1974, and his name still comes up whenever Filipino chess is discussed. Wesley So, born in Bacoor, Cavite, grew up in this scene before he reached the world elite.

Quezon City is the most populous city in Metro Manila. It is also a university town, home to the University of the Philippines Diliman and Ateneo de Manila. Campus clubs, school teams and park players feed local tournaments. For a home player, an international open within commuting distance is a rare chance at a norm without the cost of travelling abroad.

## Getting to the MICE Center without losing a round

Traffic decides your day here. EDSA, the highway along Quezon City's western edge, is one of the most congested roads in Southeast Asia. A cross-city ride that takes twenty minutes at night can take over an hour at rush hour.

Rail is the usual way to avoid it:

- **MRT-3** runs along EDSA, with stops including Cubao, Quezon Avenue and North Avenue.
- **LRT-2** runs east–west along Aurora Boulevard, crossing MRT-3 at Araneta Center–Cubao.
- **Grab** is the main ride-hailing app for the last stretch from a station to the venue door.

Most international visitors land at Ninoy Aquino International Airport in Pasay, south of the city. The transfer north is a serious journey, not a quick hop. Players usually give themselves a buffer day before round one, and many stay near the venue rather than in Makati or Manila Bay.

## Watching in silence: playing-hall manners

A chess hall has strict unwritten rules, and FIDE events add written ones. Spectators who know them are welcome. Those who don't get noticed quickly.

- **Phones off, not just silent.** Anti-cheating rules are strict. Players can forfeit if their own phone makes a sound, and arbiters don't like seeing devices near the boards.
- **No talking near the boards.** Analysis belongs outside the playing area, usually in a corridor or the skittles room where players go over games afterwards.
- **Keep your distance.** Stand back from the tables, never lean over a board, and never point at a move.
- **No flash.** Organisers decide whether photos are allowed at all, and often only in the first minutes of a round.

Filipinos are generally warm and patient with visitors, and a quiet "po" adds politeness when speaking Tagalog to someone older. For food and transport money, most locals pay with GCash or Maya. Small canteens and tricycle drivers often take only cash, so carry some peso notes for small purchases.

## Between rounds: food and air nearby

Games can last for hours, and the area around the venue is worth exploring. Quezon City has some of Metro Manila's best casual food at fair prices.

- **Maginhawa Street** in Teachers Village is lined with small restaurants, coffee shops and dessert places. Students and young professionals eat here.
- **Quezon Memorial Circle** is a large park around the Quezon Memorial Shrine, with shaded paths and food stalls. It's a good place to clear your head after a loss.
- **Araneta City in Cubao** has malls, the Smart Araneta Coliseum and easy MRT-3 and LRT-2 connections.
- **Tomas Morato and Scout area streets** fill up in the evening with grill restaurants and bars, popular for a post-round dinner.

Try silog for breakfast: garlic rice, fried egg and a meat such as tapa or longganisa. It fills you up and keeps you going through a long morning game.
