## Two walls, five centuries apart from the plaza

Step off the Plaza de Armas beside the Jesuit church of La Compañía and the noise drops away. Calle Loreto narrows into a pedestrian corridor of grey stone. On either side rise walls the Incas built, and the colonial city was later stacked on top of them.

This is one of the best places in Cusco to see imperial stonework at close range without a ticket desk in sight. The blocks are fitted without mortar. The walls lean slightly inward as they climb, a classic Inca technique for earthquake resistance.

## Reading the stones

Each wall belonged to a different Inca compound. Knowing which is which turns a short walk into a lesson in how Cusco was rebuilt.

- **West side:** the wall of Amarucancha, the palace complex associated with the Inca ruler Huayna Capac. The Jesuits later raised La Compañía church on this ground.
- **East side:** the wall of the Acllahuasi, the "House of the Chosen Women," who served the state religion. Today the Convent of Santa Catalina occupies the site.
- **The name:** in Quechua the lane is Intik'ijllu, usually translated as "the alley of the sun."

Run your eye along the Acllahuasi side. The courses are long, even rectangles, laid with a precision that colonial walls above them never match. Where Spanish plaster or adobe sits on Inca foundations, the seam between the two eras is plain to see.

## Timing your walk

The lane itself takes 15–30 minutes, longer if you stop to photograph the joints. Google currently shows it open from 8am on weekdays, from 8am until 1pm on Saturdays, and closed on Sundays. Treat these as this week's listing and check again before you go.

Morning light suits the stone. Weekday mornings after 8am also line up neatly with visits to nearby sites, so you can build a compact walking loop. Avoid planning a Saturday afternoon visit, since the listing currently ends at 1pm.

## Walking Loreto without rushing it

The street is a working shortcut, not a roped-off monument. Behaving like part of the traffic makes it more pleasant for everyone.

- **Keep to one side** when you stop for photos. The lane is narrow and people use it to get between the plaza and the streets toward Avenida El Sol.
- **Look, don't climb or lean.** These are protected walls, and touching worn edges speeds their erosion.
- **Expect vendors and photo offers.** Women in traditional dress, sometimes with llamas or alpacas, often pose for pictures nearby. A photo is a paid service, so ask first and agree on the amount before you raise the camera.
- **Say "permiso"** to squeeze past someone. It is the polite Spanish for "excuse me" in tight spaces.
- **Carry small notes and coins** if you plan to buy crafts or take portraits. Street sellers rarely take cards.

## Pace yourself at altitude

Cusco sits at roughly 3,400 metres. Even a gentle cobbled lane can leave new arrivals short of breath.

The paving is uneven stone that turns slick after rain, so shoes with grip matter more here than in most cities. Walk slowly, drink water, and save Loreto for a day when you are acclimatising. It is flat enough to be a good first outing.

## Where Loreto leads next

The lane works best as a link between bigger sights. Pair it with these, all within a short walk:

- **Plaza de Armas** and **Cusco Cathedral**, at the lane's northern end.
- **La Compañía de Jesús**, the baroque church built on Amarucancha.
- **Convent of Santa Catalina**, built over the Acllahuasi, which houses a museum of religious art.
- **Qorikancha**, the Inca sun temple under the Santo Domingo convent, a few blocks southeast.
- **Calle Hatun Rumiyoc**, home to the famous twelve-angled stone, a short walk northeast of the plaza.

Start at the plaza, walk Loreto, then continue on to Qorikancha. Every stretch of Inca wall you pass on that route is easier to read once you have seen Loreto's two walls side by side.
