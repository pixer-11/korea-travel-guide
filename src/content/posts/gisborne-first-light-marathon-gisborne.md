## A race named for the sunrise

Gisborne gets the day before almost anywhere else. The city sits on Poverty Bay on the North Island's east coast, close enough to the International Date Line to claim the title of first city in the world to see the sun rise. The First Light Marathon takes its name from that claim.

This edition takes place on Saturday, January 23, 2027. That puts it in the middle of the southern summer, a few weeks after the New Year beach-festival crowds have left the East Coast.

## Gisborne in high summer

Late January is about as warm and bright as Gisborne gets. The city is small and flat, and its main beaches start right at the edge of town. Waikanae Beach runs west from the river mouth, and Midway Beach continues around the bay beyond it.

The town is built where the Taruheru and Waimatā rivers meet to form the Tūranganui River, which runs to the sea past the port. Above the port, Titirangi (Kaiti Hill) has a lookout across the whole bay. At its foot is the site where James Cook came ashore in 1769, the first meeting on land between Māori and Europeans.

The Māori name for the area is Tūranganui-a-Kiwa. Around the region you will see the wider name, Tairāwhiti, on signs, buses and shopfronts.

## Getting to the East Coast

Gisborne is remote by New Zealand standards, and that shapes how race visitors travel. There is no passenger train, so it is a flight or a long drive.

- **By air:** Gisborne Airport sits a short drive west of the city centre, with Air New Zealand flights from Auckland and Wellington.
- **From the south:** State Highway 2 runs north from Napier through Wairoa, roughly three hours of rural driving.
- **From the north:** State Highway 2 also comes in from Ōpōtiki in the Bay of Plenty through the Waioeka Gorge. It is a winding road with long stretches between fuel stops.

Many runners from Auckland fly in. Visitors who want the scenery usually drive one way, often taking in the gorge.

## Entry, course and start line

Entry for the First Light Marathon runs through the race's own official site. That is where the distances, course map, start location and race-day timetable live. This guide doesn't name a start line or give fees, because none was supplied to us and a guess could send you to the wrong place.

Because the race is named for first light, people tend to assume it starts at dawn. Treat that as a guess until you have seen the official timetable. Runners who travel in usually stay near the beachfront or the city centre, both flat and walkable from most of town.

## Race-day manners in Tairāwhiti

A few habits make the weekend go smoothly. Gisborne is a relaxed country town with a strong Māori identity. Ngāti Porou, Rongowhakaata, Ngāi Tāmanuhiri and Te Aitanga-a-Māhaki are the main iwi of the wider region.

- **Greetings:** "Kia ora" is the everyday hello, and locals use it with everyone.
- **Paying:** EFTPOS and contactless cards work almost everywhere, including small cafés and dairies (corner shops).
- **Tipping:** It isn't expected in New Zealand. Service staff are paid a wage that doesn't depend on tips.
- **Sun:** UV levels in a New Zealand summer are high even on cloudy mornings. Runners and supporters usually wear sunscreen, a hat and sunglasses.
- **Spectating:** Supporters stand behind course markings, cheer runners by name or bib, and cross the route only at marshal points.

Shops and cafés in a town this size follow regular hours. Recovery meals the evening after a race tend to cluster along the main streets close to the beach.

## The day after the finish line

Gisborne rewards a spare day. Tired legs usually head for the water first: Waikanae Beach is close enough to the centre to walk to, and the surf there is gentler than on the open coast.

- **Titirangi (Kaiti Hill):** A road and walking tracks lead up to the lookout over the port and bay.
- **Tairāwhiti Museum:** The regional museum by the river, with Māori taonga and East Coast history.
- **Gisborne wineries:** The area is known for its Chardonnay, and cellar doors sit on the flat land around the city.
- **Eastwood Hill Arboretum:** A large collection of trees from the Northern Hemisphere, inland from the city.
- **Rere Rockslide:** A natural sloping rock in the Wharekōpae River northwest of town. People ride it on boogie boards and inner tubes in summer.

A single day after the race covers the beach, the hill and a winery. The rockslide and arboretum are worth a separate trip with a car.
