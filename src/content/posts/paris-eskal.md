## Why Eskal stands out

Paris opens dozens of ambitious tables a year, but Eskal has attracted attention quickly for a specific reason: it pairs restaurateur Ken Nanaumi with chef Alexandre Chambat, whose cooking leans on slow, deliberate techniques — maturation, curing, drying and fermentation — rather than pure seasonal flash. The result is contemporary French food built with the patience of a charcuterie or fermentation cellar behind it, which is a different rhythm from the typical bistro-to-tasting-menu pipeline that dominates the city's newer openings. The room itself reinforces that philosophy: it's intimate, centred on a broad kitchen counter, so diners — solo travelers especially — can watch plates assembled a few feet away rather than sit at a distance from the action.

## Where it is and how to get there

Eskal sits in the 8th arrondissement, near the Chapelle Expiatoire, the neoclassical monument built for Louis XVI and Marie-Antoinette on Square Louis XVI, just off Boulevard Haussmann. This puts the restaurant in a quieter, more residential pocket of the 8th, away from the Champs-Élysées crush but still close to the Grands Boulevards' department stores and the Madeleine and Saint-Lazare areas. The nearest useful Métro connections are around Saint-Augustin and Saint-Lazare (served by Lines 3, 9, 12, 13, and 14, plus the RER), both within a short walk. If arriving from the Right Bank's shopping districts, it's an easy add-on stop before or after browsing Boulevard Haussmann.

## What to expect at the table

Because Eskal is a young opening, treat any menu description as directional rather than fixed: expect a chef's-counter format where courses are designed to showcase technique — think cured or aged fish and meat, ferment-driven sauces or condiments, and vegetables treated with the same slow-food patience typically reserved for proteins. The counter seating means portions and pacing are often built for a tasting-menu rhythm rather than an à la carte free-for-all, so come with time rather than a fixed agenda. Given the format, this is a restaurant better suited to diners curious about process and technique than those wanting a quick, familiar bistro plate.

## When to go

Newly opened, buzzed-about counters like this tend to fill their small number of seats fast, especially on weekend evenings once word spreads through Paris's dining press and social feeds. If flexibility allows, a weekday dinner or an early seating gives a better shot at availability and a calmer room. Because opening hours and days are still being finalized in a restaurant's first months, confirm current service days and seating times directly with the restaurant (via its own website or booking platform) shortly before your visit — don't rely on aggregated listings alone.

## Nearby to combine with your visit

The Chapelle Expiatoire itself is worth five minutes if you're early for your reservation — a compact, little-visited monument with a genuinely unusual backstory tied to the French Revolution. From there it's a flat walk to the Église de la Madeleine and the grand food halls and terraces around Place de la Madeleine, or north to the Saint-Lazare train station area. Combining a visit with an afternoon around Boulevard Haussmann's flagship department stores makes for a natural half-day loop before settling in at the counter for dinner.

## How to visit like a local

Book ahead — for a small, counter-format room built around a handful of seats, walk-ins are a gamble, and reservations (typically made online) are the standard way Parisians secure a spot at newer tasting-style openings. Dress smart-casual rather than formal; the counter setup signals a relaxed but attentive atmosphere, not black-tie. Payment in Parisian restaurants of this caliber is almost always by card, and a service charge is generally included in the bill by French law, so tipping is a discretionary rounding-up rather than an expectation — a few euros for excellent service is plenty, not 15-20%. The most common mistake visitors make at counter-style restaurants like this is treating it as a quick meal: settle in, ask the kitchen questions about technique if you're curious, and expect the pacing of a multi-course experience rather than a fast turnover table. If you want the best view of the kitchen's actual work, request a centre-counter seat when booking rather than one at the room's edges.
