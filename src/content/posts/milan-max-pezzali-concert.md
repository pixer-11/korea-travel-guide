## A Pezzali night at Unipol Dome

Max Pezzali plays Unipol Dome on December 23, 2026. That is a Wednesday, two days before Christmas, in a city already lit up for the holidays.

The venue is the newer indoor arena in Santa Giulia, the redevelopment district on Milan's south-eastern side near Rogoredo. Expect a modern bowl with seated tiers rising around the floor, built for big crowds rather than intimacy. For a singer whose songs Italians learned at school discos, that scale suits him.

## The songbook the arena knows by heart

Pezzali was born in Pavia, a short train ride south of Milan. In the late 1980s he formed 883 there with his school friend Mauro Repetto. Their early-1990s hits turned small-town Lombard life into national pop: scooters, bars, Saturday nights and friends who never leave.

Repetto left in the mid-1990s. Pezzali kept the 883 name into the early 2000s and then carried on under his own. The catalogue an Italian audience knows word for word includes:

- "Hanno ucciso l'Uomo Ragno"
- "Come mai"
- "Gli anni"
- "Nord sud ovest est"
- "Sei un mito"
- "La regola dell'amico"
- "Tieni il tempo"

No setlist is given here, so treat that as the songbook, not a running order. What is reliably true is the crowd. It spans parents who bought the cassettes and teenagers who found the songs later, and the choruses usually come back from the stands louder than from the stage.

## Getting to Santa Giulia

The M3 yellow line is the spine of the trip. It runs straight from Milano Centrale and Duomo to Rogoredo, its south-eastern end, with no change of line.

Rogoredo is also a mainline railway station. High-speed Trenitalia and Italo trains stop there, as do the suburban S lines, including the S1, S2 and S13. Fans coming from Pavia, Lodi or further along the rail network often skip central Milan altogether and get off here.

From Rogoredo, concertgoers continue on foot into the Santa Giulia development towards the arena. On a concert night the walk is easy to follow: thousands of people move the same way. Driving is possible, but the roads around Rogoredo and the eastern ring road get slow when an arena empties. Most people go by rail.

## Two days before Christmas

The date shapes the whole evening. December 23 falls in the final stretch of Milan's holiday season, when shops stay busy and the centre fills with people doing last-minute errands.

Visitors coming in for the show often make a day of it in town first. The Galleria Vittorio Emanuele II and Piazza del Duomo are the classic pair, and both sit one M3 stop past Missori from the line you'll take to the arena. From Duomo it is a direct ride south to Rogoredo, so nobody has to cross town at rush hour with a change of trains.

The weather is a factor too. Milan's late-December evenings are typically cold and often damp, close to freezing after dark. Fans usually dress for the walk and the wait at the gates, not for the inside of a heated arena.

It is also peak travel week across Italy. Trains out of Milan on the 23rd and 24th carry people heading home for Christmas. That is why out-of-town fans often stay the night instead of catching the last train.

## Getting through the gates at a big Italian show

Italy has its own concert habits. A few of them save real trouble at the entrance.

- **Named tickets.** Large Italian concerts usually sell nominative tickets carrying the buyer's name. Staff may check that name against photo ID, so fans carry a passport or ID card. Resale outside the official channels is risky for the same reason.
- **Bags and coats.** Arenas generally limit bag size, and a winter coat takes up room in a seat. Travelling light makes security faster.
- **Paying inside.** Contactless cards and phone payments are standard in Milan. Bars and food counters around Santa Giulia take them too. Tipping is not expected at a counter.
- **Ordering a drink.** At the bar you say what you want and pay at the till. "Una birra" or "un'acqua naturale" covers most of what anyone orders.
- **Singing along.** Nobody expects silence during a Pezzali song. The crowd sings full verses, not just choruses, and joining in is the point.

## Before and after the show

Santa Giulia is mostly residential and office space, so the evening tends to start further up the M3. Two stops north of Rogoredo, Lodi T.I.B.B. puts you near Fondazione Prada in Largo Isarco. Its galleries and Bar Luce, the café designed by Wes Anderson, make an afternoon stop before heading back down the line.

Porta Romana and Corvetto, both on the M3, have ordinary neighbourhood trattorias and bars where people eat before a show. For something calmer earlier in the day, Chiaravalle Abbey stands in farmland just south of the city. It is a twelfth-century Cistercian monastery with a brick bell tower that locals call the Ciribiciaccola.

Out-of-town fans usually stay either near Centrale or around Porta Romana. Both are on the M3, so the ride back after the encore needs no change of line.
