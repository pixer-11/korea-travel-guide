The lights hang in the treetops above a steep, fern-filled gully. Below them, a path drops toward black water where a red bridge glows twice, once in the air and once in the lake. For five summer weeks, from December 19, 2026 to January 23, 2027, the TSB Festival of Lights turns Pukekura Park into New Plymouth's after-dark living room.

## What happens in Pukekura Park

The festival fills the park with lit displays, live music and cultural performances. It has run in some form since the 1950s, which makes it one of the longest-running summer events in New Zealand. Generations of Taranaki families have grown up walking the same paths.

Pukekura Park is no flat lawn. It is a deep, bush-clad valley with:

- **The main lake and Poet's Bridge**, the red bridge that ends up in most festival photos
- **The Bowl of Brooklands**, an outdoor amphitheatre with a lake between the stage and the grass bank
- **Fountain Lake and the Fernery and Display Houses**, glasshouses tucked into the hillside
- **Native bush gullies**, where the lighting picks out tree ferns and the canopy

The displays change from year to year, so each edition looks a little different from the last.

## Timing your evening

Darkness comes late here. In late December and January, the sun sets in New Plymouth at around 8:45pm–9pm, and the installations only come into their own once the sky goes fully dark. Many people plan their evening around that.

Families with small children usually come at dusk, catch the music and leave as it gets dark. Couples and groups of friends tend to stay later, when the paths are lit and quieter. The festival spans school holidays, Christmas and New Year, so weekends and the holiday period draw the biggest numbers.

## Getting to the park

The park sits on the southern edge of central New Plymouth. It is roughly a 10–15 minute walk from Devon Street and the waterfront. Main entrances are on Liardet Street, Fillis Street and Gilbert Street, and the Liardet Street gate is the one most people use coming from town.

Street parking around the park fills on festival evenings. Many locals walk in from town or get dropped off. New Plymouth Airport is about 12 km away, a short drive or shuttle into the city.

## Doing the festival the way regulars do

Taranaki locals treat the festival as a picnic first and a light show second. Regulars usually bring a rug and claim a spot on the grass bank at the Bowl of Brooklands before a performance. They eat there, then wander the lit paths once darkness falls.

- **Footwear matters.** The paths are steep, with steps and gravel. Pushchairs work on the main routes but not everywhere.
- **Layers help.** The gullies cool down fast after sunset, even in midsummer.
- **Paying is easy.** Card and contactless payment are standard in New Zealand. Tipping is not expected.
- **Mind the plants.** It is a working botanic park, so people keep to the paths and off the garden beds.

For the programme, the organiser publishes performance listings, any themed nights and access information on the festival's official site.

## Around the park and beyond

Plenty of visitors turn a festival night into a full New Plymouth day. Brooklands Zoo, a small free zoo, sits at the top of the park. Puke Ariki, the city's museum, is on the waterfront. The Govett-Brewster Art Gallery and its Len Lye Centre, with its mirrored steel facade, are on Queen Street.

Before sunset, many people walk part of the Coastal Walkway to Len Lye's Wind Wand, which bends over the sea at the end of the city foreshore. On a clear day Mount Taranaki stands over the whole city. It is the reason many visitors drive out to Egmont National Park the next morning.
