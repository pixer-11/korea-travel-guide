## Two friends from Buenos Aires, one night by the Pacific

Picture a stage set up on flat ground beside the ocean, with the dark cliffs of Lima's Costa Verde behind the crowd. That's the setting when Argentine duo CA7RIEL & Paco Amoroso play **Multiespacio Costa 21** on **November 27, 2026**.

Catriel and Paco are childhood friends who came up through Buenos Aires' rap and trap scene. Their sound keeps moving: funk basslines, trap drums, rock guitar, plenty of absurd humour and costumes. Their NPR Tiny Desk set in 2024 carried them well beyond Argentina. Fans expect a loose, theatrical show that has as much to do with performance as with music.

## Costa 21: what the venue is like

Costa 21 is an open-air events ground in the San Miguel district. It sits on the Circuito de Playas, the coastal highway that runs along the bottom of Lima's cliffs. There is no roof. The Pacific is a few steps from the fence, and the sea breeze comes straight across the ground.

That matters for clothing. Late November is spring in Lima, and the days are warming up. Evenings on the waterfront still feel cool and damp, though. Most concertgoers bring a light jacket or hoodie they can tie round their waist once the crowd warms up.

- **Ground:** flat, open, mostly standing
- **Backdrop:** the cliffs of the Costa Verde on one side, ocean on the other
- **Location:** low on the coastal road, below the clifftop neighbourhoods

## Getting down to the seafront

Costa 21 is on the coastal road, not in a neighbourhood centre, so nobody just strolls in. Most attendees use a ride-hail app (Uber, Cabify and inDrive are all common in Lima) or a taxi. From Miraflores the drive follows the Circuito de Playas north along the water. With light traffic it's a short ride. Friday evening traffic in Lima often isn't light.

From San Miguel and Magdalena the trip is shorter, down one of the roads that drop from the clifftop to the shore. Groups usually share one car and split the fare in the app. Drivers know "Costa 21" by name, so set it as your destination rather than typing a street address.

## Getting home from the Costa Verde

The trickiest part of the night is after the encore. Thousands of people leave a seafront venue at the same moment, and every phone in the crowd is calling a car. Here's how Lima concertgoers usually handle it.

- **Walk away from the gate first.** Locals tend to walk a little way along the access road before calling a car. It saves the driver from the jam at the gate.
- **Use apps over street taxis.** At night, app rides with a tracked route and plate number are the standard choice in Lima.
- **Pay digitally.** Peruvians pay each other with Yape and Plin. Visitors without them mostly use a card in the ride app, plus some small sol notes for food stalls outside.
- **Keep phones in front pockets.** In packed crowds, Limeños keep phones zipped away between songs, not sticking out of back pockets.

A few words of Spanish help too. "¿Me puedes recoger en la salida?" (can you pick me up at the exit?) is a useful message to send a driver.

## Making a night of it in Lima

Most international visitors stay in **Miraflores** or **Barranco**. Both are a coastal drive from the venue and have restaurants that stay open after the show. Barranco's bars around the Puente de los Suspiros keep going late on a Friday. Miraflores has the clifftop Malecón, where the walk past Parque del Amor is easy the next morning.

San Miguel has the advantage for anyone who'd rather keep the trip home short. Plaza San Miguel, the big shopping centre, is up on the clifftop nearby. Whichever neighbourhood you pick, plan your Friday around Lima's slow evening traffic. A ceviche lunch at a cevichería, which in Lima is traditionally a daytime meal, makes a good start to a concert day.
