## Why go

The Mostra is the world's oldest film festival, founded in 1932, and it remains one of the "Big Three" alongside Cannes and Berlin. Unlike Cannes, its home base isn't the main Venetian islands but the Lido, a slim barrier island a short boat ride from San Marco. For the 2026 edition (September 2–12), Maggie Gyllenhaal presides over the jury that awards the Golden Lion, the festival's top prize. Beyond the competition, expect world premieres, gala screenings, and the kind of red-carpet density that turns a quiet beach town into a paparazzi corridor for ten days.

## Getting there and getting around

Fly into Venice Marco Polo Airport, then take an Alilaguna waterbus or water taxi into the city, followed by a vaporetto (Line 1, 5.1, or 6) from the city center to Lido S.M.E. — the Lido's main stop. The festival's hub is the Palazzo del Cinema on the Lungomare Marconi, alongside the Palazzo del Casino and the Sala Grande, all within walking distance of the Lido vaporetto stop. During the festival, the ACTV runs a densely scheduled shuttle service between the historic center and the Lido, since most accredited guests and press stay in Venice proper or Mestre rather than on the Lido itself, where hotel inventory is limited and prices spike hard in early September.

## What to see (even without a badge)

You don't need accreditation to feel the festival. The public can typically watch arrivals along the red carpet outside the Palazzo del Cinema in the late afternoon and evening, when cast and jury members arrive for premieres. The Lido's Gran Viale Santa Maria Elisabetta, the main pedestrian strip connecting the vaporetto stop to the beach, fills with festival-goers, photographers and pop-up bars. Some sidebar sections — Orizzonti, Venezia Classici — screen restored classics and independent work with a very different, calmer crowd than the main Competition. Certain public screenings and sidebar events have historically been open to festival pass holders of various tiers, not just press and industry, so check the official ticketing structure for what's actually purchasable by general visitors this year.

## When to go

The festival runs September 2–12, 2026, and the atmosphere shifts noticeably across those ten days: opening weekend and the final awards weekend (when the Golden Lion is announced) draw the heaviest crowds and tightest hotel availability. The middle days, roughly the Tuesday through Thursday of the festival's first full week, tend to be calmer for casual visitors who just want to see the Lido in festival mode without elbowing through opening-night crowds. September on the Lido is still warm enough for the beach in the mornings before the day's screenings and arrivals ramp up.

## Practicalities to sort in advance

Book Venice (or Lido) accommodation as early as possible — rooms on the Lido itself sell out fast and prices climb sharply during festival dates. If you want any kind of ticketed access beyond watching from the street, that runs through La Biennale di Venezia's official accreditation and public ticketing system, which has historically distinguished between industry/press accreditation and a general public ticket package (sold through Venezia Unica or the Biennale's own portal in recent editions). Because dates, venues, ticket categories and prices can shift year to year, always verify the current details — exact daily schedule, screening locations, and how to buy public tickets — on labiennale.org before finalizing plans.

## How to visit like a local

Venetians and festival regulars treat the red carpet as a spectator sport best watched from a distance rather than the crush right at the barriers — arrive at the Palazzo del Cinema an hour or more before a scheduled arrival and stake out a spot along the Lungomare Marconi rather than fighting the front rows late. Payment on the Lido during the festival is overwhelmingly by card or contactless, though small beach kiosks and bars still favor cash for quick transactions. The etiquette mistake most first-timers make is assuming they can wander into the Sala Grande or Palazzo del Cinema without any pass — nearly all screenings require accreditation or a purchased ticket, so plan your visit around the free public spectacle (arrivals, the atmosphere on Gran Viale, sidebar exhibitions) rather than assuming walk-up access to premieres. If you do have festival tickets, Italians queue in an orderly single line and dress code for evening galas is genuinely enforced, so don't show up to a red-carpet screening in beachwear even though you're technically steps from the sand.
