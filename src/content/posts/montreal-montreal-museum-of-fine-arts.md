## Five buildings on one street

Stand on Rue Sherbrooke Ouest and you'll see the museum on both sides of the road. On the north side is a white marble Beaux-Arts temple from 1912, with broad steps and columns. Across the street, Moshe Safdie's 1991 pavilion rises behind the preserved brick front of an older apartment building. The two are joined underground.

That split is the first thing to understand about the Montreal Museum of Fine Arts. In French it's the Musée des beaux-arts de Montréal, and locally it's just the MBAM. It grew out of the Art Association of Montreal, founded in 1860, and has kept adding buildings ever since. Today it runs to five pavilions and is one of the busiest art museums in Canada.

## The tunnel under Sherbrooke Street

The museum's layout makes most sense from below. Underground galleries link the pavilions, so you can walk from European Old Masters to contemporary work without stepping outside. In a Montreal January, that means you never have to put your coat back on.

What each building holds, roughly:

- **Michal and Renata Hornstein Pavilion** (the 1912 marble building): the original home, built for the older collections and archaeology.
- **Jean-Noël Desmarais Pavilion** (Safdie, 1991): the main entrance and the big temporary exhibitions.
- **Claire and Marc Bourgie Pavilion**: Québec and Canadian art, including Inuit works, in a converted church. It still has its Tiffany stained-glass windows, and Bourgie Hall concerts are held in the old nave.
- **Liliane and David M. Stewart Pavilion**: decorative arts and design.
- **Michel de la Chenelière International Pavilion** (2016): international art across several floors.

Pick up a floor plan at the entrance. The levels don't line up neatly between buildings, and it's easy to come up a staircase in the wrong century.

## What to see if your time is short

Go for the Canadian and Québec galleries first. The museum is best known for these, and you'll struggle to find this range anywhere outside the country. Expect Group of Seven landscapes and a strong run of Inuit sculpture. There's also abstract work by the Montreal painters who signed the 1948 *Refus global*, such as Paul-Émile Borduas and Jean-Paul Riopelle.

If you have another hour, cross back underground to the international collections. Then look at what's showing in the Desmarais Pavilion. Temporary exhibitions here are often the reason Montrealers come back, and the biggest ones draw queues of their own.

Three hours covers the highlights at a steady walk. A full day is realistic if a major exhibition is on.

## Wednesday after dark

The museum's late night is its timing quirk. At the moment it's open 10am–5pm Tuesday to Sunday and closed Mondays. On Wednesdays it stays open until 9pm.

That evening is the best slot of the week. The weekday window from 5pm to 6pm is one of the two quietest times we have data for, and only Wednesday opening reaches it. Office workers drift in later in the evening. If you arrive straight after 5pm, the first stretch is often yours.

Like most museums, the MBAM shifts its hours for holidays and exhibition runs. Check this month's schedule on the official site before you plan around the late opening.

## Getting past the crowds

Expect company: this is one of the most-visited sights in the city. Timing makes more difference than anything else you can do.

- **Weekdays, 10am–11am** is the calmest stretch. Come at opening and head straight to the Canadian galleries.
- **Wednesday, 5pm–6pm** is the other quiet window, as described above.
- **Weekends, 12pm–5pm** is the busiest stretch. If a weekend is your only option, be at the door at 10am.

Book major exhibitions ahead online. Timed tickets for the big shows can sell out on weekends, and current prices and options are posted on the official site.

## Doing it the Montreal way

The museum works in both French and English, as does most of downtown. Staff will switch to English straight away. A "bonjour" as you reach the desk is still how Montrealers start any exchange.

A few habits help the visit go smoothly:

- **Cloakroom first.** In winter everyone arrives in a heavy parka. Check yours along with your bag, because large backpacks aren't welcome in the galleries.
- **Cards are standard.** Tapping a card or phone is normal at the ticket desk, the shop and the café.
- **Ask before photographing temporary shows.** Phone photos without flash are usually fine in the permanent collection. Loaned exhibitions sometimes ban photography, and signs at the entrance will say so.
- **Look up the Bourgie Hall programme.** A concert in the old church is a separate ticketed event. It pairs well with a Wednesday evening visit.

## Getting there and the blocks around it

The address is 1380 Rue Sherbrooke Ouest. It's a five-minute walk north from **Guy-Concordia** station on the Green Line, and **Peel** station on the same line is a similar distance to the east. Several bus routes run along Sherbrooke itself. Parking downtown is limited, so the metro is the easier choice.

This is the Golden Square Mile, where the city's 19th-century merchant mansions still stand, and McGill University's campus is a short walk east. Rue Crescent, a few blocks south, has the nearest cafés and bars. North of the museum, the streets climb steeply toward Mount Royal. If you have energy left, walk uphill into the park afterwards.
