## Bamboo arches over every street

Denpasar looks different in mid-January 2027. Tall curved bamboo poles called *penjor* stand outside nearly every house gate. Their tips hang over the road, hung with woven palm leaf, rice and small offerings, so whole streets become a row of arches.

That is the visible sign of Galungan, Bali's most important Hindu festival cycle. It comes around every 210 days on the Balinese *pawukon* calendar. It celebrates the victory of *dharma* (good order) over *adharma*. The ancestors' spirits are believed to return home for ten days, and Kuningan sees them off again.

## The 2027 dates, day by day

This edition runs from January 13 to 23, 2027. The days in between are not all equal, so it helps to know the shape of the cycle:

- **Tuesday 12 January, Penampahan Galungan:** the preparation day. Families slaughter pigs and cook *lawar* and *babi guling* for the feast, and the penjor go up.
- **Wednesday 13 January, Galungan:** families pray at household shrines, then at village and district temples, dressed in white and in ceremonial *kebaya*.
- **Thursday 14 January, Umanis Galungan:** a visiting day, when people travel to relatives and home villages.
- **Saturday 23 January, Kuningan:** the closing day, with offerings of yellow rice and woven *tamiang* discs. Prayers traditionally finish before midday, when the ancestors are believed to depart.

## Where to see it in Denpasar

There is no ticket office, because this is worship, not a staged show. The "venue" is every temple and household compound on the island. In the capital, a few places give visitors a clear view.

**Pura Jagatnatha** sits on the east side of Puputan Badung Square, beside the Bali Museum. It is a state temple to Sang Hyang Widhi Wasa, with a white stone *padmasana* shrine rising from a moat. On Galungan, worshippers file through with offerings stacked on their heads.

**Pura Sakenan** on Serangan Island, in south Denpasar, holds a large temple festival around Kuningan. Processions arrive with tall fruit-and-flower offerings, and the causeway road fills with families in white. For a quieter view of the season, any residential lane in Sanur or Renon shows penjor outside each gate.

## Temple manners during Galungan

The rules are simple, and Balinese worshippers notice when visitors follow them.

- **Dress:** a sarong (*kamen*) and a sash (*selendang*) tied at the waist are required inside temple grounds. Shoulders should be covered. Larger temples sometimes lend them at the entrance; most visitors bring their own.
- **Movement:** people avoid walking directly in front of someone praying, and never stand higher than the priest (*pemangku*).
- **Offerings:** the small *canang sari* trays on the ground are stepped around, never on.
- **Photos:** visitors usually shoot from the edge of the courtyard, without flash, and ask before photographing anyone close up.

Women who are menstruating traditionally do not enter temple grounds. Visitors are expected to respect this as well.

## What changes in the city

Galungan is a family holiday, and Denpasar works differently that week. Many government offices and schools close. Some family-run warungs and shops close for a day or two, especially around Galungan and Umanis. Roads are busy on Penampahan and Umanis as people head to their home villages.

During the ten days, children often perform *ngelawang*: a small Barong (the lion-like protective figure) goes from door to door with gamelan players. Households give a small gift in return. The music carries down the lanes, and this is one of the easiest parts of the festival for a visitor to come across.

## Making the most of ten days

Most visitors give the festival two set pieces: the morning of Galungan itself and the morning of Kuningan. Ceremonies happen mainly in the morning, and the Kuningan prayers end before noon. Visitors who stay in Sanur or Renon tend to have temples and penjor-lined streets within walking distance.

The food is part of the festival too. Restaurants that stay open often serve *babi guling* and *lawar* for the season. Visitors also tend to find *jaja*, coloured rice cakes, stacked in markets such as Pasar Badung. Treat the whole cycle as a chance to notice things rather than chase events, and the island gives you plenty.
