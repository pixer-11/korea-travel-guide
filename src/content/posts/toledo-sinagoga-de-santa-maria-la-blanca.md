## A synagogue that became a church

Built around 1180, this is the oldest surviving synagogue building in Europe, though it hasn't functioned as one since the early 1400s. Almeric, a Mudejar architect working for Toledo's Jewish community, designed it in the horseshoe-arch style of the city's Moorish builders. In 1405, after anti-Jewish riots, it was seized and converted into a church dedicated to Santa María la Blanca, the name it still carries.

Nothing about the exterior hints at what's inside: a plain whitewashed wall on a narrow street, no dome, no facade ornament.

## What the hall actually looks like

Step through and you're in a single rectangular nave, roughly 20 meters deep, divided into five aisles by four rows of octagonal pillars. Each pillar carries horseshoe arches trimmed with pinecone and pine-cone-scroll capitals, a Almohad-style detail borrowed straight from Andalusian mosque architecture.

The plaster capitals and painted wood ceiling survive largely intact from the 12th and 13th centuries. There's a 16th-century altarpiece and a large crucifix at the far end, the only obviously Christian elements in an otherwise Islamic-Jewish architectural shell. No stained glass, no side chapels: the whole point is the repetition of those arches receding down the hall.

## Visiting: hours, tickets, timing

The building is open every day of the week, 10am to 6:45pm, with no midday closure.

- Address: C. de los Reyes Católicos, 4, 45002 Toledo
- Time needed: 20-30 minutes covers it fully
- It's one of Toledo's most-visited monuments, so expect company, especially midday
- Quietest slot, weekday or weekend alike: 10am–11am, right at opening
- Busiest: weekend afternoons from 1pm to 6pm, when tour groups and day-trippers from Madrid stack up

It sits a short walk from the Sinagoga del Tránsito and the El Greco museum, so pairing all three in one loop through the Judería is the efficient move rather than backtracking later.

## Reading the space without a guide

There's no synagogue furniture, no ark, no women's gallery left to see; the building was stripped and reconsecrated centuries ago, so treat it as an architecture stop rather than a working religious site. Photography is generally allowed inside without flash, but keep voices down since it still functions as consecrated space.

Entry is via ticket at the door, and given the volume of visitors, having exact change or a card ready speeds things along. Because the whole visit is one open hall, there's no fixed circulation route: walk the full length first to feel the scale, then double back along the side aisles to look closely at the capital carvings, which vary more than they first appear.

Arriving right at the 10am opening beats both the tour buses and the early-afternoon crowds documented here, and gives you the hall close to empty for photos down the nave.
