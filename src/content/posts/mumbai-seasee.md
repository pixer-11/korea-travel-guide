## Why Seasee, why now

Bandra doesn't need another restaurant, but it keeps getting them anyway, and Seasee is the kind of arrival that makes sense of that appetite. It comes from the team behind Mirai, one of the city's more talked-about kitchens, and instead of repeating that formula, they've turned toward the street stalls of Bangkok, Jakarta and Kuala Lumpur. The result isn't a pan-Asian menu trying to please everyone — it's a specific, researched take on Southeast Asian street food, anchored by dishes like Tom Yum, a sharp, herbaceous Thai Mango Salad, and a Singapore Chilli Crab Lajiao that leans into the sweet-hot, tomato-and-chilli lacquer the dish is known for.

## What the room feels like

Walk in and the kitchen isn't tucked away — it's part of the draw. An interactive wok station sits where you can watch it: flames jumping as the metal gets seasoned between orders, the quick toss-and-flip of noodles or crab catching the light, the hiss when a ladle of sauce hits hot oil. That open-kitchen energy is very deliberate. It's the same instinct behind a lot of the best street-food counters in Southeast Asia itself — you eat with your eyes on the cook, not just the plate. Expect the noise and heat of an actual working kitchen rather than a hushed, tasting-menu hush.

## What to order

Given the restaurant's own influences, a first visit works best if you let it move geographically. Start Thai — the Tom Yum for its citrus-and-chilli broth, the Mango Salad for its raw, palate-clearing crunch — then head toward the wok station for something cooked to order, before landing on the Singapore Chilli Crab Lajiao as the centerpiece. Chilli crab of this style is meant to be eaten hands-on and shared, sauce mopped up with whatever starch is on the table, so go with people you don't mind getting messy in front of.

Because this is a newly opened restaurant, the full menu, prices and portion sizes are still settling — treat any specific dish list you see online as a snapshot, and ask your server what's new or seasonal that week rather than assuming the opening menu is fixed in stone.

## Getting there

Seasee is in Bandra, Mumbai's western-suburb neighborhood that's long been the city's most concentrated strip of new-generation restaurants, bars and cafés — this is the same pocket of the city that produced Mirai, so if you already know that address, you know the general area to aim for. Bandra is best reached by car or auto-rickshaw from South Mumbai (allow for traffic, especially at night) or via Bandra railway station on the Western Line if you're coming from the suburbs.

Because exact addresses and hours can shift for a newly opened venue, look up Seasee's current location pin and confirm it before you set out, rather than relying on an old listing.

## When to go

Southeast Asian street-food spots with live wok stations are built for dinner — the theatre of the open kitchen reads best after dark, and dishes like Tom Yum and chilli crab are the kind of food you want to sit with over a couple of hours, not rush through at lunch. As with any buzzy new Bandra opening, weekend dinner service is where you'll meet the longest waits; a weeknight gives you a calmer room and more attention from the kitchen.

Confirm Seasee's actual opening hours and whether it takes walk-ins before you go, since a restaurant this new may still be adjusting its schedule.

## How to visit like a local

Book ahead if the restaurant offers reservations — new Bandra openings from established teams tend to fill up fast, and turning up unannounced on a Friday or Saturday night is the most common way visitors end up waiting at the bar for a table. Dress code here is Bandra-casual, not formal. Payment is typically by card or UPI (India's mobile payment system) rather than cash-only, though it's worth having some cash on hand as backup.

Order the way locals order at Southeast Asian street-food places: several smaller dishes shared across the table rather than one main each, so the mango salad, a wok dish and the chilli crab all land together for everyone to pick at. The mistake first-timers make is treating this like a quiet date-night restaurant — the open kitchen means it runs loud and lively, and that's the point, not a flaw.
