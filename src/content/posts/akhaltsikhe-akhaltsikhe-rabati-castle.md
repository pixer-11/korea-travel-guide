## Seven centuries on one hilltop

Rabati is a walled town in miniature. Its sand-coloured stone walls climb the slope in tiers, and from them you look down on Akhaltsikhe's rooftops and the Potskhovi valley. Inside, a Georgian church, an Ottoman mosque, a minaret, a madrasa and a palace share the same courtyards.

That mix is the story of the place. The Jaqeli family, Georgian atabegs of Samtskhe, ruled from here in the Middle Ages. Ottoman control followed from the late 16th century until Russia took the fortress in 1828. The Ahmediye Mosque, with its gilded dome, comes from that Ottoman period and is the image on most postcards.

## What the 2012 rebuild means for you

The fort's roots go back to the 9th century. In 2012 the Georgian government spent many millions of dollars reconstructing it. Much of what you walk through is therefore new stone on old footprints: clean ramparts, fresh paving, restored interiors.

Some visitors love how complete it looks, and others find it too polished. Know this before you arrive and you'll read it right. It's a full reconstruction of how the complex may have looked, not a ruin. The lower town gives you the setting. The upper citadel holds what most people come for:

- **Ahmediye Mosque**, with the gilded dome and the minaret beside it
- **The Jaqeli palace and courtyards**, with the arcades and fountains
- **Samtskhe-Javakheti History Museum**, which covers the region's archaeology and ethnography
- **The upper ramparts**, for the widest views over the town and the hills

## Walking the walls without the crowds

This is one of the most visited sights in southern Georgia, so plan around the tour traffic.

- **Timing:** it currently opens at 9am. Most organised tours fit Rabati in alongside Vardzia and Borjomi, so arriving at opening, or going in the late afternoon, usually beats the main wave.
- **Tickets:** the lower grounds are open to wander. The citadel and museum need a ticket, sold at the inner gate, where the current price is posted.
- **Footwear:** the stairs on the ramparts are steep and uneven in places. Some sections have no railings.
- **Dress:** the church and mosque are religious buildings. Cover your shoulders and knees, and women should carry a scarf for the church.
- **Language:** staff and signs work in Georgian first. Locals call the place *Rabati* (რაბათი), and that's the word to give a taxi driver.

The walls and towers are floodlit after dark, which makes the hill worth a second look from town if you're staying the night.

## Getting there and where to go next

Akhaltsikhe is about three hours from Tbilisi. Marshrutkas (shared minibuses) leave from Didube bus station, and the castle is a short taxi ride or a walk uphill from Akhaltsikhe's bus station.

Most visitors use the town as a base for the region:

- **Vardzia**, the 12th-century cave monastery, about an hour and a half south
- **Sapara Monastery**, in the hills roughly 12 km from town
- **Borjomi**, the spa town known for its mineral water, about an hour to the northeast

If you have one full day, do Rabati in the morning and Vardzia in the afternoon.
