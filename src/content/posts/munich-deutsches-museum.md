## An island given over to machines

This is a building made to be walked through for hours. The Deutsches Museum fills most of Museumsinsel, a narrow island where the Isar splits in two, and its tall tower is visible from the bridges on both sides. Oskar von Miller, an engineer, founded it in 1903, and the main building opened in 1925.

Today it ranks among the largest science and technology museums in the world. It keeps original objects rather than replicas wherever it can, and the collections cover energy, transport, astronomy and much more. Many displays are built to be touched, cranked or switched on. It is one of the most-reviewed sights in Munich and gets very busy, so expect company and plan for it.

## Getting onto Museumsinsel

The island lies just south-east of the old town. Your choice of approach depends on where you start:

- **S-Bahn:** Isartor station is on the central trunk line, so every S-Bahn line stops there. Walk south-east along the river to Ludwigsbrücke, which crosses onto the island, in roughly ten minutes.
- **Tram:** Line 16 stops at Deutsches Museum on Zweibrückenstraße, right at the bridge.
- **U-Bahn:** Fraunhoferstraße (U1/U2) is a short walk away via Corneliusbrücke, which brings you to the island's southern tip.
- **On foot from Marienplatz:** go past the Viktualienmarkt stalls and through the Isartor gate. It takes around 15 to 20 minutes.

Driving into this part of Munich is rarely worth it. Public transport drops you closer than any car park will.

## A museum rebuilt in halves

The Deutsches Museum is partway through a long modernisation, and that shapes your visit more than anything else. The first renovated section reopened in 2022 with a block of rebuilt exhibitions. Other halls stay closed while work goes on, and some well-known displays from older guidebooks may be out of view.

Read the official website before you go. It lists which exhibitions are open right now, and the list changes as construction moves on. Getting your hopes up for one specific hall and finding it boarded off is the most common disappointment here. Prices and ticket types are also posted there, not in this guide.

## Choosing a route through the halls

Nobody sees all of it in one go. Even with sections closed, the open floors take more than a day to read properly. Pick two or three themes and stick to them.

Some ways to plan it:

- **With children:** go to the hands-on, interactive rooms first, while everyone still has energy. Leave the text-heavy galleries for later or skip them.
- **For engineering history:** spend your time on the original machines and instruments. Seeing the actual object, rather than a model, is the reason this museum exists.
- **For astronomy:** the astronomy displays sit high in the building. Check on arrival whether the planetarium or observatory has sessions on that day.

Allow three to four hours for a focused visit. A full day suits people who like reading every label. The museum closes at 5pm every day, so a visit started after lunch is short whatever day you choose.

## Timing your entry, booking and the queue

Timing matters a lot here. On weekdays it is quietest from 9am to 11am, and school groups and families tend to arrive after that. At weekends the calm only lasts from 9am to 10am. From 11am to 5pm on Saturdays and Sundays the halls are at their busiest, so try to avoid that window.

Practical points:

- **Book ahead online.** The museum sells timed tickets on its official site, and a slot at 9am opening puts you inside during the quietest hour.
- **Head straight for your top hall.** Most people start in whatever is near the entrance. Going up or deeper into the building first keeps you ahead of the crowd for a while.
- **Paying:** cards are widely accepted at German museums, but cash is still common in Germany. Some coins in your pocket help at lockers and small cafés nearby.
- **Language:** labels are in German and English. The German name is used everywhere, so "Deutsches Museum" is what you'll see on tram displays and station signs.

## The two outposts: transport and aircraft

The island isn't the whole museum. Two branch sites take the large vehicles and aircraft, and both make a good add-on if you can't get enough of the main building.

- **Verkehrszentrum (Transport Centre):** on Theresienhöhe, next to the Theresienwiese Oktoberfest grounds. It holds the road and rail vehicles in historic exhibition halls.
- **Flugwerft Schleißheim:** north of the city in Oberschleißheim, on a historic airfield next to Schleißheim Palace. This is where the aircraft collection lives.

Each has its own hours and tickets, so check the official site for both before you combine them with the island.

## Back out along the Isar

When you leave, you're beside the river, and that's worth using. The Isar's gravel banks run along both sides of the island. In warm weather people sit on them, and walkers and cyclists pass along the riverside paths.

Things within easy reach:

- **Müller'sches Volksbad:** an Art Nouveau public swimming baths just across Ludwigsbrücke on the east bank.
- **Gasteig:** the cultural centre on the slope above the baths.
- **Viktualienmarkt:** the city's food market, back towards the old town past the Isartor.
- **Gärtnerplatz:** the round square with its theatre, a short walk west of Corneliusbrücke, lined with cafés.

A museum morning followed by lunch near Viktualienmarkt and a walk along the river makes an easy, unhurried day in central Munich.
