Most temples in Lombok pick a side. Pura Lingsar doesn't. It was built in 1741 specifically so that Balinese Hindus and Sasak Wetu Telu Muslims could worship on the same ground, and that arrangement has held for close to three centuries.

## Two courtyards, one temple

The layout tells you everything before you've read a sign. Pura Lingsar is split into two terraced courtyards on a gentle slope: the upper one, Pura Gaduh, is Hindu, with a black-and-white poleng-checked shrine facing Gunung Rinjani. The lower one, Kemaliq, belongs to the Sasak Wetu Telu tradition and centers on a spring-fed pool.

That lower pool is the temple's quiet centerpiece. It's home to a colony of holy eels, considered sacred by Sasak worshippers, and caretakers sell boiled eggs at the edge so you can try to coax one out from under the rocks. Don't expect a performance; the eels surface when they surface.

Between the two courtyards, walkways cut through clipped gardens and past lily ponds fed by the same natural spring that supplies the pool. Water is the whole logic of the place: it's a rice-growing region, and Lingsar's spring has long been treated as the source that keeps the paddies around it fertile.

## Getting there and what to expect

Pura Lingsar sits in Batu Kumbung village, Lingsar district, on Jalan Raya, roughly 20 to 30 minutes by car or scooter northeast of Mataram. There's no train or public bus that gets you to the gate directly, so most visitors arrive by rented scooter, private car, or as a stop on a driver-guided day trip that also covers Mataram's old town or the Narmada water gardens nearby.

Budget 45 minutes to an hour for a full walk through both courtyards and the pool area. Entry involves a small donation rather than a fixed ticket price, and a sarong or sash is provided or required at the gate; wear one even in the Sasak courtyard, since covering the legs is expected throughout the complex.

## Visiting outside festival season

Under normal conditions, Pura Lingsar stays well below the crowds you'd find at Bali's big temples, and that's part of its appeal if you'd rather explore at your own pace than shuffle through with a tour group. Weekday late mornings tend to be the easiest time to have the gardens mostly to yourself.

The exception is the Pujawali festival, usually held around November or December on the Sasak calendar's Purnama Sasih Kepitu (seventh full moon), when Balinese and Sasak communities gather for a ceremonial rice-and-fruit throwing ritual called Perang Topat. If you want atmosphere and ceremony, time your visit for that; if you want stillness, avoid it.

## Reading the shrines without a guide

A few details are worth knowing before you walk in, since nothing is heavily signposted in English.

- The poleng (black-and-white checked) cloth on the upper shrine marks it as Hindu and represents the balance of opposing forces, a common motif across Bali and Lombok's Hindu sites.
- The Kemaliq courtyard's name comes from a Sasak word roughly meaning "forbidden" or "sacred," referring to the spring and pool.
- Boiled eggs sold near the pool are for feeding the eels, not for eating; locals treat an eel sighting as a small good omen.
- Photography is generally fine in both courtyards, but ask before photographing anyone mid-prayer.

## Etiquette that actually matters here

This is a working temple for two active faiths, not a museum, and both communities still use it for regular worship. Move quietly near anyone praying, keep your voice down inside the Kemaliq courtyard, and don't step onto shrine platforms even when they look unattended.

Tipping isn't expected, but the temple relies on the small entry donation and modest fees for sarong rental or egg-feeding, so carry a little cash in rupiah rather than assuming card payment. If you're pairing this with the Narmada gardens or a Mataram city stop, Lingsar works well as the mid-morning leg before traffic on the ring road picks up later in the day.
