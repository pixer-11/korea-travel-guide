## Red brick on Avenida Almirante Lacerda

The building is red, as the name promises. Red Market stands on a corner of Avenida do Almirante Lacerda, a block of brick and pale trim with a clock tower above the junction. Low-rise flats and shop signs in Chinese and Portuguese surround it.

Inside, it is a working wet market. Expect counters of fish on ice, butchers' blocks, vegetable stalls and wet tiled floors, along with the sound of cleavers and Cantonese bargaining. Nobody built it for visitors, and that is why it rewards an hour of your morning.

It sees a fraction of the traffic that goes to the Ruins of St. Paul's or Senado Square. That makes it an easy place to watch how northern Macau actually shops for dinner.

## A 1936 building that never stopped trading

The market opened in 1936. Portuguese architect Júlio Alberto Basto designed it in the Art Deco style that was fashionable across colonial ports at the time. The clean vertical lines, banded windows and corner clock tower all date from that design.

Its Chinese name, 紅街市 (Hung Gaai Si), simply means "red market". Taxi drivers know it by that name rather than by the Portuguese, Mercado Vermelho.

Few markets of this age in the region still do their original job. Here the architecture is the backdrop to daily trade, not a museum exhibit. Look up from the stalls at the ceiling heights and window frames. The 1930s bones are easy to read once you notice them.

## Getting to the Lacerda corner

Red Market sits on Macau's northern peninsula, between the historic centre and the Border Gate (Portas do Cerco) with mainland China. A few routes work well:

- **From the Border Gate:** walk south for roughly 15 to 20 minutes, or take a short taxi ride.
- **From Senado Square:** take a taxi or one of the many buses running north along Avenida do Almirante Lacerda. The trip is short, and the clock tower is easy to spot from the bus window.
- **By taxi:** show the driver 紅街市 written down. Pronunciation matters less than the characters.

Macau's Light Rapid Transit doesn't serve this part of the peninsula, so plan on buses, taxis or your own feet.

## The 7am window, and the 11am wall

Timing decides what kind of visit you get. The market opens at 6:00 AM every day of the week. It is calmest between 7am and 9am, on weekdays and weekends alike.

During that window the produce has just been laid out and the aisles still have room. You can stop and look at the fish counters without blocking anyone's shopping.

Try not to arrive on a weekend after 11am. From then until the 7:00 PM close it is the market's busiest stretch. The aisles fill, the floors get wetter, and a slow-moving visitor with a camera becomes an obstacle.

If a weekend is your only option, come early, then head for breakfast nearby as the crowds build.

## Shopping among the stalls without getting in the way

This is a place of work, and it helps to behave like a shopper rather than a spectator. A few habits make the visit smoother:

- **Carry cash.** Small stalls in Macau often prefer it. Hong Kong dollars are widely accepted across the city alongside patacas, but expect your change in patacas.
- **Ask before photographing people.** Stallholders are working, so a gesture toward your camera and a nod goes a long way. Wide shots of the hall are less intrusive than close-ups of someone at their chopping block.
- **Point and use numbers.** Many stallholders speak mainly Cantonese. Pointing at what you want and holding up fingers for quantity works fine.
- **Mind your feet.** Floors around the seafood and meat sections are routinely hosed down. Closed shoes with grip beat sandals.
- **Keep moving in the aisles.** Step to the side when you stop, especially at busy counters.

No tipping is expected at market stalls. Prices aren't always displayed, so if you're buying fruit to eat on the go, ask before the stallholder weighs it.

## What to buy if you're not cooking

Most travellers have no kitchen, but you don't have to leave empty-handed. Fresh fruit is the obvious buy: whatever is in season in southern China, sold by weight and ready to carry back to your hotel.

Dried goods, snacks and small packaged items also travel well. The fish and meat sections are mostly for looking at. They show you the range of seafood the city cooks with, which makes restaurant menus easier to decode later.

## Around the Three Lamps

Red Market makes a good anchor for a morning in a part of Macau that most day-trippers skip.

- **Three Lamps District (San Kio):** a short walk southwest, centred on Rotunda de Carlos da Maia. This is a dense area of street shopping, cheap clothing and small eateries, with strong Southeast Asian and Burmese influences.
- **Lin Fong Temple:** a Qing-era temple dedicated to Tin Hau and Kun Iam, north toward the Border Gate.
- **Kun Iam Tong (Kun Iam Temple):** in the Mong Ha area, a short ride or walk away. The 1844 Treaty of Wangxia between China and the United States was signed here.
- **Border Gate:** worth pairing with your visit if you're crossing to or from Zhuhai.

Plan on 45 minutes to an hour in the market itself. Add a couple of hours if you're walking on to the temples or Three Lamps.
