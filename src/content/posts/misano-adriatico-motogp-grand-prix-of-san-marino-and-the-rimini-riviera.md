## Why this race weekend is different

Most MotoGP rounds feel like a big sporting event dropped into a random town. Misano feels like the opposite: a seaside strip that spends all year waiting for this. The Misano World Circuit Marco Simoncelli sits just inland from the beach resorts of Misano Adriatico, and for one weekend in September the whole Riviera (the bike shops, the gelaterias, the boardwalk bars) turns yellow and orange in tribute to Valentino Rossi and, quieter but ever-present, to Marco Simoncelli, the local rider the circuit is named after.

That history is felt the moment you see the grandstands: this isn't a neutral venue, it's home ground for Italian motorcycle racing.

## Getting there

Misano Adriatico sits on the Adriatic coast between Rimini and Riccione, in Emilia-Romagna. The nearest airport is Federico Fellini International Airport (Rimini), a short taxi or shuttle ride away; Bologna's airport is a workable backup when Rimini flights are full, with a train connection down the coast. By rail, Rimini and Riccione stations both sit on the main Adriatic line, and race weekends have typically brought extra shuttle buses running from the stations to the circuit gates.

Roads around the circuit clog on Saturday and Sunday mornings, and locals and returning fans build in an extra hour without complaint.

## The race weekend itself

The Grand Prix was set for three days, September 11-13, 2026. Friday was given over to free and timed practice, a day organisers pitched as a good time to walk the paddock area and get close to the garages with a fraction of the crowd. Saturday's programme was final practice, qualifying, and the MotoGP sprint race. Sunday was race day proper, with the Moto3, Moto2 and MotoGP races back to back, building to the main event in the last slot of the afternoon.

## What to see and do beyond the circuit

The paddock and fan zones outside the main straight were promoted as an attraction in their own right, with team merchandise stalls, rider appearances, and a fan village atmosphere. Between sessions, it's a five-minute walk into Misano Adriatico's town center for a coffee or plate of piadina, the flatbread that's the signature street food of this whole coast.

Riccione's beach promenade and Rimini's old town (with its Roman arch and Malatesta Temple) are both a short bus or train ride away, and make a good decompression from two days of engine noise.

## When to go and how long to stay

For serious fans, the full three days offered practice, qualifying and race day, each with a different rhythm, and the sprint format gives Saturday nearly as much weight as Sunday. On a single-day visit, Sunday carried the most racing and also draws the heaviest crowds and traffic; Friday is the calmer alternative, better for soaking in the paddock atmosphere than for watching a full race.

September on this stretch of the Adriatic is still warm enough for the beach in the mornings before heading to the circuit.

## How to visit like a local

Regulars typically book their hotel in Misano, Riccione, or Cattolica months ahead, as rooms within walking or short cycling distance of the circuit tend to disappear early, with prices rising as the weekend approaches. Most fans get to the track by bike or the shuttle buses rather than driving in, since parking near the circuit is limited and exit traffic after the main race can take well over an hour.

Cash tends to help at some of the smaller food and merchandise stands inside the fan zones, though cards are widely accepted. Ear protection is standard near the start-finish straight, even among seasoned fans, along with a cap and sunscreen, since Riviera grandstands offer little shade in September. The rookie mistake is treating this like a one-day trip to the coast: the circuit sits a little inland from the beachfront hotels, so the walk or shuttle each way eats into the day rather than allowing a dash back to the sand between sessions.
