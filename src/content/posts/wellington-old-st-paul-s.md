Pull open the door on Mulgrave Street and the noise of government Wellington stops. Inside, everything is wood. Native timber columns rise into pointed arches, and dark beams cross the ceiling overhead. Coloured light falls from the stained glass onto the pews. This is a Gothic cathedral built in a country with plenty of forest and very little cut stone.

## A cathedral built from the forest

Old St Paul's dates from the 1860s. It was designed by the Reverend Frederick Thatcher, who took the pointed arches and tall proportions of English Gothic Revival and built them in New Zealand timber instead of masonry. The result is lighter and warmer than any stone church, and the joinery is all on show.

Look up and the roof trusses read like the ribs of an upturned ship's hull. Visitors make that comparison all the time, and it explains why the space feels intimate even though it is tall. The wooden arches along the nave frame the view towards the chancel. The walls of glass, many of them memorial windows, give each bay its own colour.

For about a century this was Wellington's Anglican pro-cathedral. The congregation moved to the new Wellington Cathedral of St Paul on Molesworth Street in 1964, and a public campaign saved the old building from demolition. Today it is cared for as a heritage site by Heritage New Zealand Pouhere Taonga. It is still consecrated and is still used for services, weddings, funerals and concerts.

## Finding Mulgrave Street

The church sits in Pipitea, the low ground between the waterfront and Thorndon. The address is 34 Mulgrave Street, just a few blocks north of the parliamentary precinct.

- **On foot from the station:** Wellington Railway Station is a few minutes' walk away. It is the terminus for the Kāpiti, Hutt Valley, Melling, Johnsonville and Wairarapa lines.
- **From Lambton Quay:** walk north past the Beehive and turn onto Mulgrave Street.
- **By bus:** many routes stop around the station and Lambton Quay, so the walk from either end is short.

Parking in the government district is limited. Walking or arriving by train is the easier choice.

## How long, and when

Thirty minutes is enough to walk the nave, read the windows and sit for a while. If you join a guided tour, or want to read the memorials and learn the building's history properly, plan on an hour.

At the moment it opens 10am–4pm every day of the week. Heritage buildings change their hours through the year and close on some holidays. Because this is a working church, private weddings or funerals can also close it to visitors, so check the Heritage New Zealand Old St Paul's page on the day you plan to go.

On weekends it is calmest between 10am and 1pm. Arriving at opening gives you the best chance of having the nave nearly to yourself. Afternoon light through the windows on the western side is a good reason to come back later, as long as you arrive well before the 4pm close.

## Reading the light and the timber

Most people walk straight up the centre aisle. The better route is slower.

- **Start at the back.** Stand just inside the door and look along the nave. This is where the arches line up and the ship's-hull ceiling makes the most sense.
- **Walk the side aisles.** The memorial windows and plaques sit at eye level here, and many of them tell the stories of early Wellington families and servicemen.
- **Look at the joints.** The way the timber is pegged and braced is the real craftsmanship, and you can see it up close on the columns.
- **Sit for a minute.** Wood absorbs sound differently from stone. A quiet nave here feels hushed rather than echoing.

Photography of the interior is generally welcome when no service is under way. Without a flash, the timber keeps its warm tone.

## Etiquette for a church that still holds services

Old St Paul's is a heritage site, but it is also a consecrated church, and how you behave should reflect that.

- **Ask about a tour at the entrance.** Guided tours run here, and the guides at the door can tell you whether one is starting soon. A guide will point out details you would otherwise walk past.
- **Watch for events.** If a wedding, funeral or rehearsal is on, staff will tell you, and you may be asked to come back later. Plan your day so you can return.
- **Keep your voice low** and step aside for anyone who has come in to pray.
- **Supporting the building:** upkeep is paid for partly by visitors. Any donation box or shop is inside, and current tour details are on the official site.

Since it is a quieter find than the waterfront museums, you often get room to linger. Lingering quietly is what the place rewards.

## Pairing it with Thorndon and the Beehive

This is a good first stop for a half-day walk around Wellington's civic north end.

- **Parliament Buildings and the Beehive:** a few minutes south. Free guided tours of Parliament run from the visitor centre, and they are booked separately.
- **Wellington Cathedral of St Paul:** on Molesworth Street. You can compare the 1960s concrete successor with the timber original.
- **National Library of New Zealand:** also on Molesworth Street, with exhibitions on the country's founding documents.
- **Thorndon's historic streets:** wander north-west into one of the city's oldest suburbs, with Katherine Mansfield House & Garden on Tinakori Road.
- **The waterfront:** downhill past the railway station, for the walk towards Te Papa.

Going from Old St Paul's to Parliament and then the National Library makes a neat loop of a couple of hours. Every stop is within about ten minutes' walk of the last.

## Why it holds the attention

Wellington has newer and larger attractions, but few spaces are this complete. The whole building is a single idea: a Gothic cathedral built by settlers from the timber they had to hand. Even visitors who don't usually go into churches tend to stay longer than they planned.
