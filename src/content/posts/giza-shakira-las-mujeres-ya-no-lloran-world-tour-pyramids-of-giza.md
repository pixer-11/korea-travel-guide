## A stage below 4,500 years of limestone

The backdrop here is older than any song. On November 28, 2026, Shakira brings her Las Mujeres Ya No Lloran World Tour to the Pyramids of Giza. It is a one-off stop, set against the most recognisable skyline of the ancient world.

The plateau holds the Great Pyramid of Khufu, the pyramids of Khafre and Menkaure, and the Great Sphinx. All of them are built from huge blocks of local limestone, and they sit on a desert shelf above the green sprawl of Giza city. After dark the stone is floodlit, so the pyramids show up as warm, pale triangles against a black sky.

## Why this setting suits this tour

There is a family link here. Shakira's father is of Lebanese descent. She has drawn on Middle Eastern dance across her whole career, from the belly-dance moves of the "Whenever, Wherever" era onward. A show at the edge of the Western Desert fits that thread.

The tour takes its name from her 2024 album *Las Mujeres Ya No Lloran*. The record includes "TQG" with Karol G and her session with Bizarrap. A stadium-scale show draws on decades of hits, but nobody has given us a setlist, so we will not guess at one.

The pyramids have a short, famous concert history. The Grateful Dead played in front of them in 1978, and Jean-Michel Jarre staged a millennium-eve show there. Concerts on the plateau are rare events, not a regular circuit.

## Tickets and where the details live

Resale through unofficial sellers is where fans most often get burned at landmark one-off shows. Egyptian ticketing usually ties entry to an ID or a named e-ticket, so attendees typically carry the passport or ID used at purchase.

## Reaching the plateau from Cairo

No metro line runs to the pyramids. Cairo Metro Line 2 stops at Giza station, but that is still a long drive from the plateau. Most people go door to door instead:

- **Uber and Careem** both work across Cairo and Giza, and the fare is set in the app before you ride.
- **Hotel cars and taxis** are common from Zamalek, Downtown and New Cairo. Traffic on Pyramids Road (Al Haram Street) slows to a crawl in the evening.
- **Staying close** cuts out the drive. The Marriott Mena House sits at the foot of the plateau. Smaller guesthouses in Nazlet el-Semman, the village beside the Sphinx, have rooftops facing the monuments.

Fans flying in usually land at Cairo International Airport, east of the city. The smaller Sphinx International Airport, on Giza's western side, is closer to the plateau.

## Getting through the gates at Giza

Event nights on the plateau work differently from a normal sightseeing day. Expect airport-style security at the gates. Big bags, drones and professional cameras are usually what slows the line.

A few local habits make the night easier:

- **Cash in Egyptian pounds** still matters for small purchases and tips, even though cards and app payments are normal in hotels and ride-hailing.
- **Baksheesh**, a small tip for help, is part of daily life. Drivers, porters and anyone who carries a bag expect it.
- **Touts with camels and horses** work the roads around the plateau. A polite, firm "la, shukran" (no, thank you) while you keep walking ends most offers.
- **Layers** help. Late-November desert evenings are noticeably cooler than Cairo afternoons, and the open plateau catches the wind.

Fans typically leave the venue slowly. Ride-hailing pickups jam at the exits, so many people walk a little way toward Pyramids Road before calling a car.

## Making a trip of it

Daytime on the plateau is a separate experience from the concert. Visitors usually spend half a day there: inside the Great Pyramid's narrow passages, at the panoramic viewpoint to the southwest, and down at the Sphinx and the Valley Temple of Khafre.

The Grand Egyptian Museum stands just beside the plateau and holds the Tutankhamun collection. Further south, Saqqara's Step Pyramid of Djoser and the pyramids of Dahshur make a full-day trip. Back in central Cairo, Khan el-Khalili bazaar and the Islamic Cairo mosques round out a long weekend.
