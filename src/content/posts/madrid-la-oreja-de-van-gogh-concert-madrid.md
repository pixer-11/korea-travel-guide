## Inside Movistar Arena

The building is a large circular indoor arena. It stands on the site of Madrid's old Palacio de los Deportes, and its round shape is easy to spot from the end of Calle Goya. For concerts it splits into two main zones, and Spanish ticketing uses the local terms for both:

- **Pista**: the standing floor in front of the stage
- **Grada**: the tiered seating that rings the bowl, split into lower and upper levels

On the pista, fans of a band like this usually start queuing well before doors to get close to the barrier. The grada suits anyone who wants a guaranteed view and a seat for the quieter songs. The bowl is steep, so the upper rows see the whole stage, though from a distance.

## Doing the night the Madrid way

Madrid eats late, and concert nights follow that clock. Locals rarely eat a full dinner before a show. Instead they meet for a caña (a small draught beer) and a tapa in the bars along Calle Goya and Calle del Doctor Esquerdo, then head to the arena together.

- **Ordering**: "una caña" gets you a small beer and "un vermut" the vermouth Madrid loves. Many bars bring a small free tapa with each drink.
- **Paying**: cards and phone payments are accepted almost everywhere. Spaniards tip lightly, usually by rounding up or leaving small change.
- **After the show**: many groups go back to the same bars or walk towards the Goya junction for a late plate of something. Metro de Madrid normally runs until around 1:30am, so most people take the train home.
- **Singing along**: it's expected. In the slower songs the whole arena often sings the chorus without the band.

Madrid in late December is cold and dry, often near freezing after dark. Concertgoers usually wear layers they can strip down to on a warm pista floor.
