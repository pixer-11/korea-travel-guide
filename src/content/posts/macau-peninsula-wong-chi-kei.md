## A noodle name with a long memory

Wong Chi Kei is a name Macau already knows. The brand traces back to a Guangzhou noodle stall of the 1940s that moved to Macau. It built its reputation on springy egg noodles and small, shrimp-heavy wontons.

Its best-known branch faces Senado Square. This one is at 51 Rua de Cinco de Outubro, a working shopping street in the older western part of the peninsula. It gets far fewer reviews than the big names nearby, but it is consistently well rated by the people who do find it.

## What lands on the table

The menu follows the Cantonese noodle-shop pattern. Every dish is built around a few staples: thin egg noodles, rice congee, wontons, brisket and greens. Bowls come out quickly, because that is how this style of cooking works.

What to order:

- **Wonton noodles (wan tan min):** a clear broth with fine, firm egg noodles and shrimp wontons. This is the benchmark dish.
- **Dry shrimp roe noodles (ha zi lo min):** noodles served without broth and dusted with dried shrimp roe. A bowl of soup usually comes on the side.
- **Beef brisket noodles:** slow-braised brisket over noodles, for something heavier.
- **Congee (juk):** smooth rice porridge, the gentlest choice for breakfast or a late night.
- **Gai lan with oyster sauce:** the standard plate of greens to balance the starch.

The noodles are what to judge the place on. Wong Chi Kei is associated with the old bamboo-pole method of pressing noodle dough, which gives the strands their bite. Ask for your soup noodles to come out first and eat them fast, before they soften in the broth.

## Finding Rua de Cinco de Outubro

The street sits in the tangle of lanes west of Senado Square, towards the Inner Harbour. From the square it is a short walk downhill on foot. Rua das Estalagens and Rua da Felicidade, with its red-shuttered shopfronts, are close by. That makes the shop easy to pair with an afternoon of wandering the old quarter.

The light rail does not reach this part of the peninsula. Your options are walking, taking a taxi or riding one of the many public buses towards Avenida de Almeida Ribeiro. The free casino shuttles from the ferry terminals drop you in central Macau, and from there it is all walkable. Give a taxi driver the Portuguese street name and number, and it helps to have it written down.

## Ordering at a Cantonese noodle counter

These shops are built for speed. Don't expect to linger over a menu.

- **Sharing tables is normal.** At busy times you may be seated with strangers. A nod is all the etiquette needed.
- **Point if you need to.** Staff deal with visitors often. Pointing at the menu or saying the Cantonese dish name gets you served.
- **Tea and condiments:** Cantonese noodle tables usually carry chilli oil or red vinegar. Add them a little at a time, because the broth is seasoned already.
- **Paying:** the bill is normally settled at the counter or cashier on the way out, not at the table. Tipping isn't expected at casual noodle shops.
- **Currency:** prices are in patacas, and Hong Kong dollars are widely taken across Macau. Change usually comes back in patacas. If paying by card or mobile wallet matters to you, ask before you order.

For prices, look at the menu when you sit down. The price level is firmly budget.

## Timing your bowl

It opens 10am to midnight, seven days a week. That long window is the shop's best feature for a visitor. A late breakfast of congee at 10am fits in, and so does a supper of wonton noodles after the evening crowds leave Senado Square.

Cantonese noodle shops tend to fill around the office lunch hour and again at dinner. If you want a table to yourself, mid-afternoon is the sensible bet. A noodle stop here takes 20 to 30 minutes, so it slots easily between sights.

## Making an afternoon of it

The surrounding streets are some of the most rewarding on the peninsula for walking. Senado Square and its black-and-white wave-pattern paving are minutes away. Beyond them uphill are the Ruins of St. Paul's.

In the other direction, Rua da Felicidade leads towards the Inner Harbour. Its restored shopfronts were once the old red-light district. Eat your noodles first, then walk it off through the lanes. For something sweet afterwards, egg tarts and almond cookies are sold all along the route to St. Paul's.
