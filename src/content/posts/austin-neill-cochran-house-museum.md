## A Portico Among the Apartment Blocks

West Campus is mostly student housing. Much of it is new mid-rise, with bikes chained to every railing and scooters parked across the sidewalks. Then, on San Gabriel Street, a white Greek Revival house appears, set back on its lawn behind a row of columns. It looks like it wandered in from another century, and it did.

The house went up in 1855. Its architect was Abner Cook, the builder behind many of Austin's antebellum landmarks. The portico and the balustrade running above it are his trademarks, the same ones you'll see on his better-known work downtown. Here you can walk up to them, stand under them and look at how the columns are put together.

## What Cook Built, and Who Lived Here

Cook was Austin's leading master builder in the years before the Civil War. He is best known for the Texas Governor's Mansion, finished a year after this house. Seeing the two lets you read his style clearly: a symmetrical front, tall columns and a strong horizontal line along the roof.

The house takes its name from two families, the Neills and then the Cochrans, who lived in it for many decades. It was also used for other purposes over the years. The museum's guides and exhibits tell those stories in more detail than any plaque outside, and that history is the reason to go inside rather than just photograph the front.

## Inside the Rooms

Expect a house museum rather than a gallery. You move through the period rooms, and the furniture and objects are arranged to show how a household of that era actually lived. The ceilings are high and the rooms are laid out symmetrically, as the Greek Revival style demands. Tall windows let in plenty of light.

The museum is small, so an hour is usually enough. If you like to read every label and ask questions, allow a little longer. Take a few minutes on the grounds as well. The lawn is the best place to take in the whole facade, and the balustrade is easiest to see from the front walk.

## Timing Your Visit to San Gabriel Street

Getting the hours right matters. The museum opens Wednesday through Sunday, 11am to 4pm. It is **closed Monday and Tuesday**, so a Tuesday walk from campus will end at a locked door.

- **Late morning, Wednesday to Friday:** there are fewer weekend visitors, and students are in class.
- **Weekends:** the same 11am to 4pm hours apply, so aim to arrive by early afternoon. That leaves time for a full tour before closing.
- **Summer:** Austin afternoons are hot. Visiting at the 11am opening means you spend less time crossing West Campus in the heat.

Admission and tour details can change. Check the museum's official website before you go, and see what's posted at the door when you arrive.

## Getting In and Getting Around

Most people who know this house heard about it from a friend. It is a quiet, under-the-radar stop that rarely gets a mention next to the Capitol or South Congress, and part of its appeal is that you won't be fighting a crowd for the view of the portico.

Some practical points:

- **On foot:** from Guadalupe Street ("the Drag") and the UT campus, head west through West Campus. The walk takes a few minutes.
- **Parking:** West Campus street parking is tight and much of it is restricted. Read every sign before you leave the car, or come by rideshare or bus along Guadalupe.
- **Inside:** treat the rooms as a historic home. Keep your hands off the furniture and follow the guide's route. If you want to take photos, ask first.
- **Ask questions:** at a small museum the guides usually have time to talk, and they're the fastest way to hear about the families who lived here.

## Pairing It With the Rest of Central Austin

The house combines easily with an afternoon near campus. The University of Texas lies just east. The Harry Ransom Center and the Blanton Museum of Art are on the far side of the campus, and a walk or short ride will get you there.

Head west instead and you reach Pease Park, a long green strip along Shoal Creek that suits a stroll after the tour. To round out the Abner Cook story, go downtown to see the Texas Governor's Mansion near the Capitol. It shows the same architect working on a grander scale a year later.
