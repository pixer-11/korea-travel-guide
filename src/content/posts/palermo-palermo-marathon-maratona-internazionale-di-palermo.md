## Mid-November, on Sicilian stone

Palermo is a strange city to race through. Its centre is built on a grid of Baroque streets, Arab-Norman domes and baroque church fronts, and the marathon route was planned right through that grid. By mid-November the cruise crowds have usually thinned and the city belongs mostly to Palermitani again.

The event paired a full marathon with a half marathon. That gave entrants two options: the full distance for the whole experience, or the half for a fast morning and a long lunch afterwards.

## The venue: the city itself

There is no stadium here; the venue is the street network of Palermo. The organisers' summary described the course as running through the city's historic streets. These are the landmarks that frame the old centre:

- **Quattro Canti**, the baroque crossroads where Via Maqueda meets Via Vittorio Emanuele
- **Teatro Massimo**, Italy's largest opera house, at the top of Via Maqueda
- **Palermo Cathedral**, on Corso Vittorio Emanuele, a mix of Norman, Gothic and later styles
- **Foro Italico**, the open seafront park along the bay
- **Via della Libertà**, the wide 19th-century boulevard heading north to the newer city

Underfoot, Palermo's streets mix asphalt with the stone paving typical of the old city. That stone gets slippery when it's wet, which makes a shoe with decent grip a sensible choice on rainy mornings.

## Getting to Palermo for race weekend

Most visitors fly into **Falcone-Borsellino Airport** (Punta Raisi), about 35 km west of the city on the coast. From there, there are three ways into town:

- **Trinacria Express** train to Palermo Centrale, with stops at Notarbartolo along the way
- **Airport coaches** to the city centre and the Centrale area
- **Taxis**, from the rank outside arrivals

From elsewhere in Sicily, regional trains and intercity coaches reach **Palermo Centrale**, at the southern end of Via Roma. From there it is a short walk into the old quarters.

Location matters on a race morning. A room within walking distance of the start avoids the hunt for transport while roads are closed, and big race weekends tend to fill central hotels quickly.

## Arancina, panelle and the post-race refuel

Palermo has some of the best street food in Italy, and a race morning here traditionally ends at the friggitoria counter. The classics:

- **Arancina**: the fried rice ball, called feminine *arancina* in Palermo (not *arancino*, as in eastern Sicily). The classic fillings are *al burro* (ham and mozzarella) and *alla carne* (meat ragù).
- **Panelle and crocchè**: chickpea fritters and potato croquettes, often tucked into a soft sesame roll
- **Sfincione**: thick, spongy Palermo pizza topped with tomato, onion and breadcrumbs
- **Pane con la milza**: the city's famous spleen sandwich, for the brave
- **Cannoli**: ricotta-filled shells, best filled to order

The old markets show the full scene. **Ballarò** sits near Palermo Centrale; **Capo** spreads out behind the Teatro Massimo; **Vucciria** is close to Piazza San Domenico. Sunday trading can be quieter at some stalls, so the day after a Sunday race is often a better market day.

## Cheering, coffee and race-day manners

Palermo does a lot of its living outdoors, and race days are no exception. A few local customs:

**At the kerb.** Spectators shout "*Forza!*" or "*Dai, dai!*" to push runners on. Runners often put their first name on their bib or shirt so strangers can shout it.

**At the bar.** Italian coffee etiquette works the same on a race morning. In many bars you pay at the *cassa* first, then hand your receipt (*scontrino*) to the barista and drink your espresso standing at the counter. Sitting at a table can cost more, and the menu board shows it.

**Paying.** Cards are widely accepted in hotels and restaurants. Small market stalls and street-food carts are more cash-oriented, so coins come in handy for arancine. Tipping isn't expected in Italy; rounding up or leaving small change for good service is plenty.

**Road closures.** On race mornings, streets on the route close to traffic for hours. For spectators and non-runners, getting around on foot is the simplest option.

## Stretching the trip beyond race day

A recovery day with little walking is easy to arrange in Palermo.

- **Palazzo dei Normanni and the Cappella Palatina**: gold Byzantine mosaics, part of the UNESCO-listed Arab-Norman Palermo site
- **Monreale Cathedral**: on the hill above the city, with one of the largest mosaic cycles in the Christian world
- **Mondello**: Palermo's beach suburb below Monte Pellegrino, good for a slow seafront lunch
- **Cefalù**: about an hour east by regional train, with a Norman cathedral and an old harbour

November is low season for these sights, which usually means shorter waits than in summer. Opening hours at each site change by season.
