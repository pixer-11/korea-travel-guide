## Why go

You duck in off the street and the noise of Manila traffic just stops. The room swallows it — thick timber beams, iron scaffolding gone rust-orange with age, and a ceiling thick with butterflies, wings catching whatever low light escapes the bulbs below.

This is The Iron Fairies, a concept that started in Bangkok and has since landed in London, Ibiza, and now Manila. It isn't trying to look Filipino, or even Southeast Asian — it's trying to look like a blacksmith's workshop that fairies moved into a century ago, and it commits hard to the bit.

## What the space feels like

Every surface looks handled, not decorated. Iron chains loop from the ceiling, workbenches double as tables, and the butterflies — reportedly over 30,000 of them, each one wired by hand in the brand's original ateliers — cluster thickest above the bar.

It's dim enough that your eyes need a minute to adjust. That's deliberate. The whole design leans into shadow, letting small pools of warm light do the work instead of one bright overhead glow.

Live music runs nightly, which shifts the room's whole mood after dark. Expect the crowd to thicken and the tables near the stage to fill first.

## What to order

The cocktail list plays on the same forge-and-fairy theme. The signature to try:

- **Old Balete Tree** — the house cocktail, named for the gnarled trees of Philippine folklore said to house spirits
- **Sisig tacos** — a local twist folded into an otherwise international menu, sisig's smoky, garlicky pork flavor repackaged into taco form

Beyond that, expect the kind of small-plates-plus-cocktails menu these bars run globally, though the Manila-specific dishes are the reason to look at the menu twice rather than assume you've seen it before.

## Getting there and settling in

Because this is a newly opened venue, exact address details, hours, and reservation policy are worth double-checking on the day — new Manila openings sometimes adjust their schedule in the first months. Look up the current listing or call ahead before you commit an evening to it.

Budget a couple of hours minimum. This isn't a one-drink-and-go spot; the design rewards lingering, and live music sets tend to structure the night around them, not around a quick turnover.

## How to visit like a local

Manila's serious nightlife crowd treats a spot like this as a late start, not an early one — arriving well after dinner hour once the live sets are underway, rather than right at opening.

Book ahead if reservations are offered. A new, buzzed-about bar in the city fills its tables fast on weekends, and walk-ins may face a wait or standing room only.

Dress a notch up from casual. Manila's trendier bars skew smart-casual after dark, and a space this theatrical rewards a bit of effort.

Cash and card are both standard at Manila bars in this tier, but confirm whether the venue is cashless — some newer spots have gone card- or app-only. Tipping isn't obligatory in the Philippines, though rounding up or leaving 10% for good service is common practice and appreciated by staff.

The mistake first-timers make is treating it like a photo-op pit stop. Order slowly, let a cocktail arrive, and stay for at least one live set — the room is built to be experienced over an hour, not photographed and abandoned in ten minutes.

If you're bringing a group, call ahead about seating. Tables near the stage go first once the music starts, and larger groups fare better with an advance heads-up than a hopeful walk-in.
