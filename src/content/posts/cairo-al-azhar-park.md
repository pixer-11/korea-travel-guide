Cairo is short on open green space, and from the top lawns of Al-Azhar Park you can see why. On one side the city spreads out in tan concrete and satellite dishes. On the other the Citadel and the pencil minarets of the Muhammad Ali Mosque stand along the skyline. Between them run clipped grass, water channels and palms, set on a hill in the middle of medieval Cairo.

## A park built on centuries of rubbish

The hill under your feet is made of debris. For hundreds of years this ground beside the historic city was a dumping site. It built up into mounds of rubble that sat between Salah Salem Street and the old quarter of Al-Darb Al-Ahmar.

The Aga Khan Trust for Culture cleared and landscaped the site, and the park opened in 2005. It covers roughly 30 hectares. The design borrows from historic Islamic gardens: a long formal axis, narrow water runnels, geometric beds and fountains. It isn't a wild green space. Every edge is trimmed, and the paths are paved and wide enough for prams.

## Walking the main axis

Enter from Salah Salem Street and the layout soon makes sense. A central promenade runs along the spine of the hill, lined with water channels and shaded seating. Lawns slope away on both sides.

Here is what you'll find along the way:

- **Fountains and runnels** on the formal axis, the park's most photographed stretch
- **Cafés and restaurants**, several placed to look out over the city
- **A lake** near one end, with tables along its edge
- **An open-air theatre** used for performances; check the park's own listings for what's on
- **Broad lawns** where Cairene families picnic in the evenings

Give it two to three hours if you plan to walk the whole length, stop for tea and stay into dusk. A quick loop for the views takes about an hour.

## The Ayyubid Wall on the western edge

The excavation turned up a stretch of the 12th-century Ayyubid city wall along the park's western boundary. It had been buried under the rubbish for centuries. It is now one of the longest surviving sections of Cairo's medieval fortifications.

You can look down on its stone towers and ramparts from the park side. Beyond the wall sit the dense lanes of Al-Darb Al-Ahmar, one of Cairo's oldest neighbourhoods, where the same restoration programme has worked on houses and monuments. Taken together, the park, the wall and the district beyond show the city's layers from one spot.

## Sunset over the Citadel

Most people come for the panorama. From the higher points the Citadel of Saladin sits to the south, and the domes and minarets of Islamic Cairo fill the view west. On clear days the skyline stretches much further.

Late afternoon is the obvious time to go, since the low sun turns the stone of the Citadel and the mosques honey-coloured. When the call to prayer starts, you hear it from many minarets at once, overlapping across the old city below. That happens five times a day in this part of Cairo, and it's one of the best reasons to stay past sunset.

Keep the crowd forecast in mind, though. Weekend evenings from 4pm to 10pm are the park's busiest hours. In Egypt the weekend usually means Friday and Saturday. If you want the sunset with fewer people around, go on a weekday.

## Arriving at the Salah Salem gate

The address is 10 Salah Salem St, on the busy highway along the park's eastern side. Most visitors come by taxi or ride-hailing app (Uber and Careem both work in Cairo). Ask for "Hadiqat al-Azhar," which is the Arabic name, and drivers will know it.

No metro station is close enough to walk from comfortably, so plan on a car for at least one leg. The park works well with a day in Islamic Cairo. The Al-Azhar Mosque, Khan el-Khalili bazaar and the gate of Bab Zuweila are all within a short ride. If you're happy walking through busy streets, the Blue Mosque (Aqsunqur) in Al-Darb Al-Ahmar is close to the western side.

## Fitting in on the lawns

The park is one of the most-reviewed places in Cairo, and it isn't a secret. Plan around the crowds rather than hoping to find it empty.

- **Timing:** weekdays from 9am to 1pm are the quietest window. On weekends, 9am–12pm is calmest.
- **Tickets:** you buy entry at the gate. The current price is posted there, and foreigners and Egyptians are often charged different rates. Carry some Egyptian pounds in small notes in case the booth won't take your card.
- **Dress:** this is a family park in a conservative part of the city. Covering your shoulders and knees means you get fewer looks.
- **Picnics:** locals spread blankets on the lawns, and the security staff and gardeners will tell you if an area is off limits. Stay off any grass that has been roped off.
- **Photos:** couples and families come here for portraits, especially on weekend evenings. Ask before you photograph people, and expect some to ask to photograph you.
- **Cafés:** table service is normal and a small tip for the waiter is usual. Check the menu for current prices before you order.

## Currently 9am to 10pm

At the moment Google lists the park as open every day from 9:00am to 10:00pm. Parks and gardens in Cairo can change their hours for the season, during Ramadan and on public holidays. Check the official site or ask your hotel before planning around a late visit.

During those hours you have two good options. Arrive at 9am on a weekday and you'll have the main axis mostly to yourself, with gardeners still working on the beds. Or come later on a weekday, settle at a café facing the Citadel, and watch the lights come on across the old city.
