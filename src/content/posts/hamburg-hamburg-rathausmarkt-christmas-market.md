Hamburg's Rathaus fills the whole back wall of the square. It is a sandstone neo-Renaissance front with a green copper clock tower, finished in 1897. For one month a year a village of wooden huts stands in front of it, and the city hall becomes the backdrop to Hamburg's best-known Christmas market.

## A month of Roncalli on the Rathausmarkt

The 2026 edition runs from 23 November to 23 December. Its full name is the Roncalli Historischer Weihnachtsmarkt. The name comes from Circus Roncalli, whose founder Bernhard Paul is behind the market's old-fashioned, theatrical look.

That lineage shows in the details. The huts have decorated roofs and carved figures rather than plain canvas, so it looks like a fairground from another century. The market closes the day before Christmas Eve, so it covers Advent only. There is no stretch into the week between the holidays.

## Tickets, entry and what costs money

The "tickets" question has a short answer: the square is open, and walking through the market costs nothing. You pay at each stall for what you eat, drink or buy. There is no gate and no timed slot.

The organiser publishes dates and opening times on the market's official site. Prices are on the boards at each hut. Handicraft stalls price their goods individually, and they sell things like:

- wooden toys and carved figures
- glass baubles and candles
- woollens and leather goods

## What fills the cups and the paper trays

The stalls run along narrow lanes between the huts. The menu covers the North German winter staples:

- **Glühwein**, red or white, plus Feuerzangenbowle and Eierpunsch at some huts
- **Bratwurst** in a split roll with mustard
- **Gebrannte Mandeln**, sugar-roasted almonds sold in paper cones
- **Schmalzkuchen**, small fried dough squares dusted with icing sugar
- **Lebkuchen** hearts strung on ribbon, which double as presents

The market is also known for its Santa act. A Weihnachtsmann in a sleigh crosses the square on a high wire several times each evening, and the lanes fill as people stop to watch. The organiser lists the show times with the opening hours.

## Mugs, deposits and the Glühwein queue

Hot drinks come in ceramic mugs, and every mug carries a deposit, called *Pfand*. Hand the mug back at any hut from the same operator and you get the deposit returned. Or keep it: plenty of visitors take a mug home on purpose, because the designs change from year to year.

Drinks queues work as a loose crowd at the counter rather than a line. People usually say clearly what they want and how many, as in "zwei Glühwein, bitte". Cash moves fastest, though many stalls also take cards.

Nobody tips at food huts. Rounding up a little at a drinks counter is common but not expected.

Weekday afternoons are usually easier than Friday and Saturday evenings. After work and on weekends, office crowds and day-trippers pack the lanes.

## Getting to the square

Public transport is how nearly everyone arrives. The Altstadt has little parking, and much of the area around the Rathaus is pedestrian. You have two easy stations:

- **Rathaus (U3)** opens onto the edge of the square.
- **Jungfernstieg (U1, U2, U4, S-Bahn)** sits a few minutes north on the Binnenalster.

From Hamburg Hauptbahnhof it is one or two stops by U-Bahn. On foot it is a walk of roughly 15 minutes down Mönckebergstraße, the main shopping street. Several more Christmas stalls line that street in Advent.

## Making an evening of it

Most visitors give the Rathausmarkt one to two hours, then carry on walking. The market sits inside a compact loop of sights:

- **Binnenalster and Jungfernstieg.** In winter the lake usually has a lit Christmas tree floating on the water.
- **The Alsterarkaden.** These white arcades run along the canal between the square and the lake.
- **St. Petri and the St. Nikolai memorial.** St. Petri is a short walk away. St. Nikolai's spire survived the war bombing and now stands as a memorial.
- **Other markets.** Gänsemarkt, the Fleetinsel and Mönckebergstraße have their own Advent markets, so you can move between several in one evening.

Late afternoon is the classic time to arrive. That way you see the Rathaus facade in daylight and then lit up once darkness falls, which comes early in a Hamburg December.
