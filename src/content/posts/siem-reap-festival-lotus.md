## Silk, Stages and Two Kitchens

Organisers planned to turn a silk farm into a festival ground for nineteen days. Festival Lotus was set for the Lotus Silk Farm in Siem Reap from November 7 to 25, 2026. The announced programme brought artisans, musicians and chefs from Cambodia and France onto the same site.

The venue's name points to an unusual craft. Lotus silk is spun from fine fibres drawn out of lotus stems. It is slow, hand-worked and one of the rarest textiles in Asia, so even a narrow scarf is a serious object. A Franco-Khmer festival at a place named for it suited the theme: making things by hand, in two traditions, side by side.

## Why Siem Reap, and Why This November

The timing came from diplomacy. Festival Lotus was scheduled alongside the Francophonie Summit, the gathering of French-speaking nations, with Cambodia named as host for 2026. The French link goes back to the protectorate era (1863–1953), and it still shows in the colonial shophouses around the Old Market.

November is the start of the dry season. The rains ease off and the humidity drops, which makes it one of the more comfortable months for walking around Angkor. Summit weeks usually bring delegations, press and extra security. Central hotels tend to fill up, and some roads can close at short notice.

## Tickets, Sessions and What This Guide Leaves Out

With almost three weeks on the calendar, the festival was designed less as a single concert and more as a place to drop into. No single headline night anchored the announcement.

Long-running festivals like this usually suit a free afternoon or evening between temple days. A few hours is typically long enough to:

- watch the artisans at work
- eat from both culinary traditions
- catch whatever music is playing

## Getting Out to the Lotus Silk Farm

Most trips start from central Siem Reap, around Pub Street and the Old Market (Psar Chas). From there, the options are a tuk-tuk, the motorbike-pulled carriage found on nearly every corner, or a ride ordered through PassApp or Grab. The apps show a fixed fare up front, so there is no negotiating with the driver.

With a street-hailed tuk-tuk, people agree the fare before climbing in. For longer rides, travellers often hire the same driver for the return trip and ask them to wait. International flights land at Siem Reap–Angkor International Airport, roughly an hour's drive from town. Many hotels arrange airport transfers.

## Buying From the Makers, Politely

How you buy here matters as much as what you buy. Some local habits that make the day go smoothly:

- **Money:** US dollars and Cambodian riel both circulate. Small purchases often come back with riel as change, and small vendors tend to prefer cash. Many also take KHQR payments, the national QR code system, through Cambodian banking apps.
- **Haggling:** bargaining is normal at the Old Market. At an artisan's table it's a different matter. Lotus silk takes weeks to produce, so hard haggling there comes across as dismissive.
- **Photos:** asking before photographing someone weaving or cooking is the norm. A gesture toward the camera and a nod usually does it.
- **Greetings:** *chom reap suor* means hello and *or kun* means thank you. A *sampeah*, palms pressed together at chest height, is the respectful way to greet older vendors. With the French theme, *merci* gets a smile too.
- **Clothing:** covered shoulders and knees are required to climb the upper levels of Angkor Wat, which matters on any day that combines the farm with the temples.

## Building the Rest of Your Days Around It

A festival this long was meant to fit around a temple itinerary. Angkor Wat at sunrise, the stone faces of the Bayon and the trees growing through Ta Prohm make a full first day. Angkor passes are sold at the official ticket centre on the road out to the temples, where current prices are displayed.

Evenings in town centre on Pub Street and the night markets along the Siem Reap River. Phare, The Cambodian Circus, puts on acrobatic theatre performed by Cambodian artists, a natural companion to a festival built around Khmer craft and performance. Three or four nights leave time for the temples, the farm and a slower day by the river.
