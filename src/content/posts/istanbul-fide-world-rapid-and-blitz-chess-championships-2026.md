Fifteen minutes on the clock, plus ten seconds added after every move. That is the rapid time control, and it is the slow half of this tournament. In blitz, each player starts with three minutes and gains two seconds a move. Games that would take a classical match an entire afternoon end here before your tea goes cold.

From December 25 to 30, 2026, Istanbul hosts the FIDE World Rapid and Blitz Championships. The world's top grandmasters come to compete for four world titles, and Magnus Carlsen arrives as defending champion.

## Four titles, two speeds

There are two time controls and two sections. The open and women's events each crown a rapid champion and a blitz champion, so four people leave Istanbul as world champions.

Rapid usually comes first. The games are long enough for real plans to unfold, so you can follow a position on a board and still keep up. Blitz is different. Hands move faster than eyes, pieces get knocked over, and flags fall in time scrambles that commentators can barely narrate.

What to watch for:

- **Carlsen's title defence**, the storyline most fans follow from round one
- **Time scrambles** in blitz, when both clocks drop under ten seconds
- **The women's section**, which plays on the same days and carries the same title status

## Six days in late December

The championships run from December 25 to 30, 2026. That puts the final rounds just before New Year's Eve. Christmas is not a public holiday in Turkey, so the city works as normal on the opening day.

Visitors following the whole event usually stay all six days. Those with less time tend to pick the blitz days, since they pack the most drama into the fewest hours. FIDE publishes the round-by-round schedule on its official site.

Most spectators watch a full session in a sitting. A rapid round can take an hour or more, and a blitz session runs as a long string of short games back to back.

## Venue and tickets

FIDE is the organiser. It publishes the venue, the schedule and the ticket links for spectators on its official site, along with the broadcast partners for anyone watching from home.

At top chess events, the playing hall and the spectator area are often kept apart. Fans may watch from a gallery, through glass or on big screens in a commentary zone. How you see the boards depends on the hall's layout, so the official spectator information is the reference.

The games are also broadcast live online with grandmaster commentary. Many fans in the hall keep a phone stream running on silent with headphones in, so they hear the analysis while watching the players.

## Spectator etiquette in the hall

Chess halls are quiet, and blitz does not change that. Players are thinking at speed, and any noise from the gallery can cost someone a game. These are the norms experienced spectators follow:

- **Phones go silent**, and flash photography is off-limits near the boards
- **No talking about positions** within earshot of players, even in a whisper
- **Clapping waits** until a game is clearly over and the players have shaken hands
- **Commentary areas** are where fans discuss the moves out loud

Elite events often scan spectators at the entrance as part of their anti-cheating measures. Experienced fans carry little, which keeps the line moving at security.

Autograph and photo moments usually happen away from the boards, after a player's last round of the day. Grandmasters who have just lost a blitz game rarely want to stop.

## Istanbul in the last week of December

Late December in Istanbul is cool and often wet. Grey skies over the Bosphorus are common, and days are short. A waterproof layer is worth packing because you will walk between transport and the venue.

Most international visitors fly into Istanbul Airport (IST) on the European side or Sabiha Gökçen (SAW) on the Asian side. Your choice of airport depends on where the venue sits, since crossing the city at rush hour can take a long time.

Around the city, people pay for the metro, trams, Marmaray trains, funiculars and ferries with an Istanbulkart. It is a reloadable card that one person can tap for a whole group. A ferry between Eminönü, Karaköy, Kadıköy and Üsküdar is the cheapest way to see the Bosphorus between rounds.

On rest evenings, chess fans tend to scatter across the old city:

- **Sultanahmet**, for Hagia Sophia, the Blue Mosque and Topkapı Palace
- **The Grand Bazaar**, roofed and dry on a rainy afternoon
- **Karaköy and Galata**, for cafés and the walk up to the Galata Tower
- **Kadıköy**, a short ferry ride away, for its food market streets

New Year's Eve follows right after the final day. Many visitors who come for the chess stay for it, and the city's waterfront fills up that night.
