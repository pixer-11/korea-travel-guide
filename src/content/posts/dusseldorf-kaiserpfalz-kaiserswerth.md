## Black stone at the water's edge

Kaiserpfalz Kaiserswerth is a wall before it is anything else. Dark basalt columns are laid sideways in the masonry, so their hexagonal ends face you like a honeycomb of stone. The walls run right down to the Rhine embankment. Cargo barges slide past close enough to read their names.

There is no roof and no reconstructed hall. What you get is a broken shell: thick walls, stairways climbing into nothing, and window openings framing the river and the flat green meadows of the far bank. The editorial line sums it up well: castle remains, river views and countryside.

## Nine centuries in one ruin

The name holds the story. A "Werth" was a river island, and Kaiserswerth began as one. Saint Suitbert founded a monastery here around 700, before the Rhine shifted course and joined the island to the east bank.

In 1062 the island became the scene of a famous kidnapping. Archbishop Anno II of Cologne lured the boy king Henry IV onto a boat and carried him off, taking control of the empire. Historians call it the Coup of Kaiserswerth.

The ruin you walk through dates mostly from Frederick Barbarossa's rebuilding in the later 12th century. He turned the site into a fortified imperial palace and toll station on the Rhine. A Latin inscription naming him survives in the stonework. The palace finally came down in 1702, when it was besieged and blown up in the War of the Spanish Succession.

## Getting out to Kaiserswerth

The U79 is the easy route. It runs underground through central Düsseldorf, stopping at the Hauptbahnhof and Heinrich-Heine-Allee, then surfaces and heads north.

- **Stop:** Klemensplatz or Kaiserswerth, both a short walk from the old village
- **Journey:** roughly 30 minutes from the city centre
- **On foot from the stop:** head west through the village towards the river, then follow Burgallee to the ruin
- **By bike:** the Rhine cycle route passes right by, and the ride from the Altstadt follows the river most of the way

Driving works, but Kaiserswerth's lanes are narrow and parking near the river fills up on fine weekends.

## Walking the walls without a misstep

This is a ruin, not a museum, and it is run that way. Expect uneven stone, steps of different heights and few handrails. Wear soles with grip, especially after rain, when worn basalt gets slick.

- Keep a hand on small children near the drops and higher openings.
- Signage is mostly German. "Pfalz" means palace, "Burg" means castle, and that covers most of it.
- Visitors don't climb on loose masonry or the wall tops; stick to the marked paths.
- Many people bring a snack and eat it on the Rhine meadows outside, not inside the ruin.
- The surrounding cafés and bakeries often prefer cash, so carry some euros even though cards are spreading.

## The village around the ruin

Kaiserswerth's old centre is worth as much time as the palace. The Kaiserswerther Markt is a long, tree-lined square of historic houses running down towards the river.

- **Basilica of St. Suitbertus:** the Romanesque-Gothic church holds the gilded shrine of Saint Suitbert, the monastery's founder.
- **Kaiserswerther Diakonie:** Theodor Fliedner founded his deaconess institute here in 1836, and Florence Nightingale trained with it in 1850 and 1851, before the Crimean War.
- **Rhine ferry:** a small car and bike ferry crosses to Langst in Meerbusch, a short ride that gives you the best view back at the ruin's river wall.

A loop of ruin, Markt, basilica and riverbank takes a relaxed half day.

## Choosing your moment

The ruin gets plenty of visitors, and families and cyclists pour along the Rhine promenade when the weather turns warm. Weekday mornings shortly after the 10am opening usually beat the weekend strollers. Arriving before the afternoon walkers reach the river makes the photos easier too.

Late afternoon in summer is also good: the low sun warms the dark stone, and there is still time before the 6pm close. The site is seasonal, so for a visit between late autumn and early spring, confirm on the city's website that it is open. In winter you can still see the riverside walls from the embankment.
