Phnom Penh has plenty of restaurants that put "Cambodian" on the menu almost as an afterthought, wedged between pad thai and pizza. This festival does the opposite: for a run of days, Rosewood Phnom Penh turns its dining spaces over entirely to Cambodian cuisine, treating it as the main event rather than a nod to location.

## What the festival actually is

This is the hotel's inaugural edition, built as a multi-day celebration of Cambodian food rather than a single gala dinner. The premise is straightforward: gather dishes, techniques and ingredients from around the country and present them the way a five-star kitchen would present French or Japanese cooking, with real technique and plating rather than tourist-menu shortcuts.

That framing matters here. Cambodian cuisine gets overshadowed regionally by its Thai and Vietnamese neighbours, and a festival like this is partly an argument that Khmer cooking, from prahok-based sauces to slow-cooked samlor soups, deserves the same attention.

## Where to find it

Rosewood Phnom Penh occupies the upper floors of Vattanac Capital Tower, the glass skyscraper that has become one of the city's most recognisable landmarks. The hotel's restaurants and event spaces sit well above street level, with views over the Tonle Sap and Mekong rivers and the older, lower-rise city beneath.

That height is part of the draw. You're eating Cambodian food that's rooted in market stalls and home kitchens, but the setting is a glass-walled room dozens of floors above the streets where those dishes originated. The contrast is deliberate and part of what generated buzz when the festival launched.

## Why it's generating buzz

Word travelled fast among both Phnom Penh residents and visiting food writers, not because the concept of a Cambodian food festival is unusual on its own, but because of who's hosting it. A luxury international hotel group putting its full kitchen resources behind Khmer cuisine, rather than a safer continental menu, is a statement about where the country's food scene is heading.

Locals see it as validation of a cuisine they already know well. Visitors see it as an accessible, comfortable entry point into dishes they might otherwise only encounter at plastic-stool street stalls, which is no bad thing but a very different experience.

## Booking a table without the guesswork

Because this is a recently launched, seasonal event rather than a permanent restaurant fixture, none of the usual fixed details apply yet in any reliable long-term way.

- Confirm current festival dates directly with Rosewood Phnom Penh before booking flights or clearing your schedule around it.
- Call or email the hotel to check whether the festival is running as a set menu, a series of themed dinners, or an open buffet-style format, since this can change between editions.
- Ask about dress code. Rosewood properties generally lean smart-casual to formal for dining, and a rooftop-adjacent event in a five-star tower is not the place to show up in flip-flops.
- Reserve earlier rather than later. Limited-run hotel dining events at high-profile properties tend to fill up once local food media covers them.

## Getting there and getting oriented

Vattanac Capital Tower sits in central Phnom Penh, an easy tuk-tuk or ride-hail trip from most riverside and central hotels. Traffic in the city can be unpredictable at peak hours, so build in extra time rather than cutting it close to a reservation.

Once you're in the building, the hotel occupies the upper section of the tower, separate from the office floors below, and hotel staff at the ground-floor entrance can direct you to the correct lift bank. If you've never visited Rosewood Phnom Penh before, arriving a little early gives you time to take in the view before your table is ready.

## Eating around the edges of the festival

Even outside festival dates, Rosewood Phnom Penh's dining venues are worth knowing about if you're building a broader Phnom Penh food itinerary that mixes fine dining with street-level eating.

Pair a visit here with a wander through the Central Market (Phsar Thmei) or the riverside food stalls near Sisowath Quay, so you get both ends of the spectrum: the polished, elevated take on Cambodian cooking upstairs, and the plastic-table, market-stall version at ground level. Neither one is the "authentic" version and the other the imitation; they're two honest ways the same cuisine gets cooked and eaten in this city.
