## What the Kyushu Basho actually is

This is the last of Japan's six annual Grand Sumo tournaments, closing out the year after Nagoya in July and Tokyo in September. The 2026 tournament was set for fifteen consecutive days, November 8 to 22, with no rest days built in. It is held at the Fukuoka Kokusai Center, a dedicated arena in the city's Hakata Ward rather than a multi-purpose stadium.

Lower-ranked wrestlers bout in the late morning, and the venue fills through the afternoon as rank climbs. By the time the top-division makuuchi wrestlers enter, mid-to-late afternoon, the hall is at its fullest and loudest. This is the stretch most spectators come for.

## Getting tickets and picking a day

General tickets for the 2026 Kyushu Basho went on sale September 19. Seating runs from ringside box seats, sold in blocks of four and usually the first to go, up through arena chairs at the back.

- Ringside boxes: closest to the dohyo, highest demand
- Box seats (masuseki): floor-level, seat 2-4, shared cushions
- Arena chairs: individual seats, upper level, easiest to buy singly

Weekdays in the first week are usually the calmest; the final days, especially the last Sunday (senshuraku), typically draw the biggest crowds as the championship is decided. Seating charts, prices and sale channels differ from one tournament to the next.

## Fukuoka Kokusai Center: layout and access

The Kokusai Center sits a short walk from Hakata and Gion subway stations, both on Fukuoka's compact two-line metro system. It's a dedicated sumo and events hall, not a converted gym, so sightlines are built around the dohyo rather than retrofitted.

Doors usually open well before the first bouts, and the building has coin lockers, a merchandise counter, and food stalls selling chanko nabe, the stew wrestlers themselves eat to bulk up. The concourse gets tight during the top-division bouts, so bag and coat storage is easiest before the afternoon rush.

## Reading a day at the dohyo

Bouts are brief, often over in seconds, but the ritual around each one, the salt-throwing, the leg stamps, the staring, lasts several minutes. That build-up is half the show and explains why a match list can look sparse for how long an afternoon actually takes.

The program (banzuke) tracks rank and match-ups, and wrestler names and rankings change each tournament. English-language signage is limited, so a translation app or the sumo association's own English site helps more than a guidebook once you're in your seat.

Bout schedules, gate times and seating maps are specific to each basho.
