## A race that starts before sunrise

Bangkok runs this one in the dark. Road races here traditionally start in the small hours, long before sunrise, because the city's heat and humidity make a mid-morning marathon a punishing idea even in late November.

Picture it at the start: closed roads under orange streetlights and thousands of headlamps and race bibs. Food vendors are already setting up on the side streets. November 28-29, 2026 marks the ninth edition, and with over 30,000 runners it is the largest road race in Thailand.

## The course: palace walls and a river crossing

The route gives you the old city at its emptiest. The course runs past the Grand Palace, the walled complex of gilded spires and white crenellated walls on Rattanakosin Island that was the royal seat from 1782. Normally its gates are crowded with tour groups. At race hour you have the walls nearly to yourself.

Then there is Rama VIII Bridge. It is a cable-stayed span over the Chao Phraya River, opened in 2002, with a single tall pylon and its cables fanned out in a harp shape. Running across it gives you one of the few long, open river views in a city that usually hides its waterway behind buildings.

Expect flat roads. Bangkok sits barely above sea level, so the bridge approaches are about the only climbs you'll meet. The real opponent is humidity, not hills.

## Race weekend logistics

Two days, several moving parts. The event spans November 28 and 29, and the organiser's official site carries the distance categories, start location and registration links. Like most big-city marathons, it gives out race packs before the race itself.

Getting to the start is the part that catches visitors out. The BTS Skytrain and MRT don't run in the pre-dawn hours, so runners usually get there by:

- **Grab or metered taxi**, which most runners order from their hotel
- **Walking**, if they are staying in the old town
- **Hotel shuttles**, which some larger hotels arrange for race weekend

For that reason, many international runners stay in Rattanakosin or nearby Banglamphu, around Khao San Road. From there the palace area is a short walk or ride.

## Running it the Bangkok way

The rhythm here is local. These habits make race weekend easier:

- **Learn the cheer.** Spectators and volunteers shout "Su su!" (สู้ๆ), roughly "fight on!" A smile or a quick wave back goes down well.
- **Pay with apps or small notes.** Grab takes card in the app. Street vendors and some taxis prefer cash in small denominations, and drivers rarely have change for big notes at 2am.
- **Use 7-Eleven.** There's one on almost every block, open around the clock. Runners stock up there on electrolyte drinks, bananas and water the night before.
- **Wai the volunteers.** Pressing your palms together with a slight bow is the polite Thai thank-you, and volunteers notice it.
- **Respect royal sites.** Anything to do with the monarchy is treated with real seriousness. Runners don't climb on palace walls or monuments for finish photos.

## After the finish line

By the time you finish, the old town is waking up. Wat Pho, home of the giant Reclining Buddha and a traditional Thai massage school, sits just south of the Grand Palace. A post-race massage there is a long-standing runner's ritual.

If you visit the Grand Palace or Wat Pho later in the day, the dress code is enforced at the gate. That means covered shoulders and knees, so race singlets and shorts won't do. The temples' hours shift through the year, and their official sites list the current times.

Across the river sits Wat Arun, the Temple of Dawn, reached by the short cross-river ferry from Tha Tien pier. Its porcelain-studded prang is the view most runners have been staring at from the bridge.

## Who this race suits

It suits runners who want a city break attached to a medal. Late November falls in Bangkok's cool, dry season, the most comfortable time of year to be in the city. Many visitors stretch the trip into a week of temples, street food and maybe a few days on the islands.

First-time marathoners should know the conditions are tougher than the flat profile suggests. Even before dawn, the air is warm and heavy. Experienced runners usually treat it as a race to run steadily, not a personal-best course.
