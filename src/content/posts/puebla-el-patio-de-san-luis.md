## Five blocks up from the Zócalo

Puebla's grid does half the navigating for you. The even-numbered avenues climb north from the cathedral square, so Avenida 10 Oriente sits five streets up. Number 2 is at the very start of it, where the "Oriente" half begins at the Calle 5 de Mayo axis.

That puts EL PATIO DE SAN LUIS within the Centro histórico's UNESCO-listed core. It's a short, flat walk from the Zócalo past Templo de Santo Domingo. You can make it your breakfast before the churches or the late lunch after them.

It's a well-known address with plenty of reviews, not a secret. Reviewers rate it solidly rather than rapturously. The price level is mid-range: more than a market stall, well short of a hotel dining room.

## Reading a menu in Puebla

The venue is listed simply as a Mexican restaurant, so check the printed menu for what's on today. Puebla is one of Mexico's great food cities, though, and its local dishes turn up on menus all over the old town. These are the names worth knowing:

- **Mole poblano**: the dark sauce of chiles, spices and chocolate that Puebla claims as its own, usually served over chicken or turkey.
- **Chiles en nogada**: a stuffed poblano chile in a walnut cream sauce with pomegranate seeds. It's a seasonal dish, mostly August and September.
- **Chalupas**: small fried tortillas topped with red or green salsa and shredded meat.
- **Molotes**: torpedo-shaped fried masa turnovers.
- **Cemitas**: Puebla's sesame-seeded sandwich roll, often with the herb pápalo.

If you see any of these, order them here, since this is the city they come from. If you're unsure about heat, ask "¿pica mucho?" and you'll get an honest answer.

## Timing your table

The hours make planning simple. It opens at 10am every day and closes at 8pm, except Sunday, when the doors shut an hour earlier at 7pm.

On weekdays, foot traffic stays light across the whole 10am to 8pm window. You can turn up whenever suits you. Weekends are different: from 1pm to 6pm the room is at its busiest. That stretch overlaps the Mexican *comida*, the long main meal of the day.

For a weekend visit, aim for the 10am opening and order breakfast. Or treat it as an early dinner after 6pm on Saturday. On Sunday that leaves you only the last hour before 7pm, so mornings are the safer bet.

## Ordering and paying on 10 Oriente

A few habits make the meal go smoothly:

- **Wait to be seated.** In a sit-down Mexican restaurant you wait at the entrance to be shown to a table.
- **Ask for the bill.** Nobody brings it unprompted, because hurrying a table is considered rude. Say "la cuenta, por favor," or catch the waiter's eye and mime writing a signature in the air.
- **Tip 10 to 15 percent.** That's standard in Mexico. When you pay by card, the terminal often comes to the table and you can add the tip there. Otherwise, leave cash in pesos.
- **Check card acceptance first.** Ask "¿aceptan tarjeta?" before you order if you're relying on a card. Small Centro businesses vary on this.

Prices are on the menu, so read it on arrival rather than relying on outdated figures online.

## Walking it off nearby

You don't need transport afterwards. Everything below is within a few blocks on foot:

- **Templo de Santo Domingo and the Capilla del Rosario**: walk south on Calle 5 de Mayo. The chapel's gilded stucco interior is the most lavish baroque room in the city.
- **Zócalo and the Catedral de Puebla**: keep heading south. The cathedral's towers are among the tallest in Mexico.
- **El Parián and the Barrio del Artista**: head a few blocks east. El Parián is an old market arcade selling talavera pottery and *dulces típicos*, Puebla's traditional sweets. Painters work in the open studios of the Barrio del Artista next door.
- **Biblioteca Palafoxiana**: just beside the cathedral. Founded in 1646, it's often described as the first public library in the Americas.

A good half-day loop goes like this: breakfast at 10am, Santo Domingo, the Zócalo, then El Parián for sweets.

## Getting there without a car

The simplest approach is to walk from wherever you're staying in the Centro. Give a taxi or ride-hail driver the full address, "Avenida 10 Oriente número 2, Centro." Puebla's street names repeat with Oriente and Poniente halves, and the wrong half puts you on the other side of 5 de Mayo.

Driving into the old town means narrow one-way streets. Paid public car parks are scattered across the Centro. Leave the car there and walk the final blocks.
