## Getting a bib

Paying for a place is not the whole process. Large Chinese marathons usually take registrations during a set window and then allocate places, often by ballot. Results are published afterwards and confirmed runners then pay.

Registration for 2026 was through the official race website. Reseller listings and "guaranteed entry" offers carry real risk, because bibs at Chinese races are normally tied to the runner's ID.

What international runners usually need:

- A passport whose details match the registration exactly
- Any medical or fitness declaration the organisers ask for
- Collection of the race pack in person at the expo, with the same passport
- A Chinese visa or visa-free entry eligibility, which is handled separately through Chinese embassies

## Cheering "jiayou": race-day manners

One word matters on the course: 加油, *jiāyóu*. It literally means "add oil", and spectators shout it at every runner. Shouting it back at the volunteers usually earns a grin.

**Paying.** Hangzhou is Alipay's home city. Almost everything works on mobile payment: cafés, metro gates, convenience stores near the start. Both Alipay and WeChat Pay accept international cards, which makes a race weekend much simpler for visitors.

**Getting in.** Marathon-morning metro trains fill with runners, and station security checks take time. Bags are scanned on the Hangzhou Metro every day, not just on race days.

**On the course.** Water and refreshment stations are staffed by volunteers who hand cups out at arm's length. The usual etiquette is to take one without stopping dead and drop the empties in the bins or zones marked after the table. Runners in China also carry small flags and wear team shirts. A group in matching colours is a running club, and it is usually loud.

**Spectating.** Watching from the West Lake shoreline is popular with locals out for their Sunday walk. Spots reachable on foot or by metro are the easiest, and crossing the course is controlled, so spectators generally stay on one side of it.

## A weekend around the race

Hangzhou rewards an extra day or two. November is a comfortable month to walk the city, and the start area is a short distance from some of Hangzhou's best-known sights.

Near Huanglong:

- **Yellow Dragon Cave** (Huanglong Dong), a small garden in a fold of hillside, which gives the area its name
- **Baoshi Hill** and **Baochu Pagoda**, a short climb with views straight down over West Lake
- **Zhejiang University's Yuquan campus**, a leafy stretch at the foot of the hills

For recovery days:

- **Su Causeway** and **Bai Causeway**, flat walkways across West Lake that are easy on tired legs
- **Lingyin Temple** and the Feilai Feng rock carvings in the western hills
- **Longjing** tea villages, the home of Dragon Well green tea, terraced into the slopes southwest of the lake
- **Hefang Street**, the restored old shopping street near Wushan Square

Hangzhou food is easy on the stomach after a marathon. Local favourites include West Lake fish in vinegar sauce, Dongpo pork and shrimp stir-fried with Longjing tea leaves. Menus and prices are posted at each restaurant.
