## Why go

You come around the bend on Marine Drive and the road just... ends, at a squat brick fortress with the underside of the Golden Gate Bridge roaring quietly overhead. That juxtaposition is the whole draw: a 19th-century masonry fort, all arched casemates and cast-iron gun ports, wedged directly beneath one of the most photographed steel structures on Earth. Fort Point was built between 1853 and 1861 to guard the entrance to San Francisco Bay, and it's one of the few brick "Third System" coastal forts left standing on the West Coast. It never fired a shot in anger — by the time it was finished, rifled artillery had already made brick forts obsolete — but the Army kept a garrison here through both World Wars, and the fort's presence is actually why the bridge's southern end was engineered with that dramatic arch instead of a straight approach.

## What you'll actually see

Inside, the fort unfolds as three tiers of vaulted brick casemates stacked around a central parade ground, with cannons still mounted at several gun ports facing the strait. Spiral stairways connect the levels — steep, cool, and slightly damp, the brick holding onto a chill even on warm days. Climb to the top tier and you're standing at eye level with the bridge's massive orange girders, close enough to hear the traffic deck flex and the occasional foghorn moan from the strait below. Ranger-led tours cover the fort's Civil War role and its odd second life as a bridge-construction staging area in the 1930s; on weekends, rangers in period wool uniforms run cannon-loading drills that walk through the actual (unfired) motions of 19th-century artillery crews. Panels throughout explain the fort's brickwork and why its guns were already outdated the day it opened — a nice bit of military-history irony.

## Getting there

Fort Point sits at 201 Marine Drive, in the Presidio, right at the water's edge below the bridge's toll plaza. There's no direct transit stop at the fort itself — the most common approach is on foot or bike along the Golden Gate Promenade / Crissy Field shoreline path, a flat, scenic 20–30 minute walk from the Crissy Field parking areas. Muni bus 28 runs along the Presidio to spots within a 10–15 minute walk. Driving, there's a small parking lot right at the fort that fills early on weekends, with overflow lots nearby at Crissy Field. If you're already walking or biking across the Golden Gate Bridge, a paved path descends from the bridge's east side directly down to the fort — a genuinely dramatic way to arrive.

## When to go

The fort is open Thursday through Monday, 10am to 5pm, and closed both Tuesday and Wednesday — plan around that if you're building a Presidio day. Mornings are your best bet for calmer wind and clearer light for photos of the bridge's underside arch; by early afternoon, gusts funneling through the Golden Gate can make the parade ground genuinely uncomfortable, especially in spring and summer. Fog is common year-round but tends to burn off by midday in late summer and fall, which are generally the clearest months to see the bridge towers rather than a wall of grey.

## How to visit like a local

Treat Fort Point as a stop on a longer shoreline walk rather than a standalone errand — pair it with Crissy Field's beach and marsh trails or continue on to the bridge itself. Entry is free and there's no advance booking system, so you simply walk in during open hours; ranger talks and cannon drills run on a posted schedule at the visitor desk, so check times as soon as you arrive rather than assuming one will start soon. Because it's well-known and sits on one of the city's busiest walking/biking corridors, weekend afternoons draw real crowds of both tourists and cyclists passing through — arriving right at 10am on a weekday, when the fort is open, gets you the emptiest casemates and the best light for photos. Dress in layers no matter the forecast: the wind off the Golden Gate strait is sharper here than almost anywhere else in the city, and the fort's brick interior stays cool even when it's warm outside. The mistake most visitors make is glancing at the fort from the bridge's pedestrian path above and never actually walking down into it — the view from inside, looking straight up through a gun port at the bridge's underside, is the whole reason to come.
