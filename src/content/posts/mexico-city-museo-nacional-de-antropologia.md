## Under the Umbrella

A single column holds up the roof over the courtyard. The locals call it *El Paraguas*, the umbrella: a vast concrete canopy balanced on one pillar, with water pouring down from its rim into a pool below. The column is wrapped in bronze reliefs by José Chávez Morado, and the falling water keeps the courtyard noticeably cooler than Reforma outside.

Architect Pedro Ramírez Vázquez designed the building, and it opened in 1964. The layout is the first thing to understand. The exhibition halls run around the courtyard in a long rectangle, each one a self-contained room, so you can step out between civilisations and get your bearings again.

On the ground floor the archaeology moves region by region. Upstairs, the ethnography halls cover the Indigenous peoples of Mexico today. Most visitors never climb the stairs, and they miss half the museum's argument because of it.

## The Sala Mexica and the Sun Stone

Head for the far end of the courtyard first. The Sala Mexica sits on the main axis, opposite the entrance, and it is the room everyone comes to see. Its centrepiece is the Piedra del Sol, the carved basalt disc often called the Aztec Calendar. It is mounted on the wall with the face of a deity at its centre, ringed by bands of glyphs.

The rest of the hall is just as strong:

- **Coatlicue**, the earth goddess, with a skirt of serpents and a necklace of hands and hearts
- A scale model of **Tenochtitlan**, the island capital that lies under today's Centro Histórico
- Feathered-serpent sculpture and sacrificial vessels from the Templo Mayor era

This room fills up first. If you arrive at 9am, walk straight here and save the early halls for later.

## A Route Through the Other Halls

Three hours is the bare minimum. A full day is better if you want to see the upstairs floor too, and a lot of people come back for a second visit. Pick your priorities before you go in, because nobody does all of it well in one go.

- **Teotihuacan hall:** a full-colour reproduction of part of the Temple of the Feathered Serpent façade, worth seeing before or after a trip to the pyramids
- **Gulf Coast hall:** an Olmec colossal head, the helmeted basalt faces that are among the oldest monumental sculptures in the Americas
- **Maya hall:** a reproduction of the tomb of Pakal from Palenque, down a set of steps, plus a jade death mask and carved stelae
- **Oaxaca hall:** Zapotec and Mixtec goldwork and the funerary urns of Monte Albán

Upstairs, the ethnography rooms show clothing, house reconstructions and ritual objects from living communities. Reading them after the archaeology halls below links the stone gods to the people still living in those same regions.

## Getting to Chapultepec

The museum is on the north side of Paseo de la Reforma, inside the first section of Bosque de Chapultepec. The nearest Metro station is **Auditorio** on Line 7. From there it is a short walk east past the Auditorio Nacional and along Reforma.

**Chapultepec** station on Line 1 also works. It takes longer, but the walk through the park is pleasant. Metrobús Line 7 runs along Reforma past the entrance, which makes it handy from the Centro Histórico or the Ángel de la Independencia. Taxis and ride-hail cars can drop you right on Reforma.

On your way in you pass a huge stone monolith, usually identified as Tláloc, the rain god. It stands by the road in front of the museum, and it was moved here from Coatlinchan when the museum opened.

## Beating the Weekend Crush

This is one of the most-visited museums in Latin America, and weekends prove it. **From 10am to 5pm on Saturdays and Sundays, the place is at its busiest.** That covers almost the whole day. School groups, families and tour buses all pack into the Sala Mexica at once. Sundays are especially crowded, since entry is free for Mexican nationals and residents.

How to handle it:

- **Go Tuesday to Friday** if your itinerary allows. Remember it is **closed on Mondays**, which catches out a lot of visitors.
- **On a weekend, be at the door at 9am.** You beat the 10am surge and get the big rooms almost to yourself for a while.
- **Buy tickets online** through the official INAH ticketing site if you can, and check current prices there. Otherwise the ticket windows are by the entrance.
- **Check large bags and backpacks** at the cloakroom (*guardarropa*) inside the lobby. You cannot take them into the halls.
- **No flash.** Photos without flash are generally fine. Hold the camera back in the dimmer rooms, where the guards will step in.

Staff speak some English, but a simple "*buenos días*" when you arrive goes down well. Most label text is in Spanish, with English on the main panels. An audioguide or a guided tour gets much more out of the smaller rooms.

## Before and After: Bosque de Chapultepec

Plan to make a day of the park around the museum. Just across Reforma is the **Museo Tamayo**, with contemporary art in a low concrete building among the trees. Next to it, the **Museo de Arte Moderno** holds Mexican modernist painting.

To the south, **Castillo de Chapultepec** sits on its hill above the park. The climb is steep, and the views run down Reforma towards the skyscrapers. The boating lake, **Lago de Chapultepec**, is a short walk away too.

If you need lunch, Polanco's restaurant streets are just north of the museum, around Avenida Presidente Masaryk. The museum also has a café on site. Many visitors stop there halfway through, then go back in for the upstairs halls.

Either way, your last entry has to fit inside a 5pm close. Allow far more time than you think you need, because the halls you meant to skip are the ones that hold you up.
