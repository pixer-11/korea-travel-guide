## A fortress built to end a rebellion

Fort George exists because of Culloden. After the Jacobite army was defeated in 1746, the government wanted a garrison that could hold the Highlands down for good. Construction began in 1748 and ran for over two decades, designed by military engineer William Skinner. The Adam family, the dynasty of Scottish architects, worked on it as contractors.

The result is enormous and almost unchanged. You cross a timber bridge over a dry ditch and pass through a gate set in angled stone walls. Inside, the walls open onto a parade ground framed by long, regular barrack blocks.

It was never attacked. That is partly why it survives so complete, and partly why it still works as a base.

## The walk around the walls

Start with the ramparts. They run close to a mile around the whole site, and the grassy walkway on top gives you water on three sides. Across the narrow firth sits Chanonry Point on the Black Isle. The channel between them is one of the best places in Britain to see bottlenose dolphins from land.

Along the way you pass:

- Gun emplacements with cannon still pointing out over the water
- Bastions with views back toward Inverness and the Kessock Bridge
- A small dog cemetery where officers buried their regimental pets

The walls are exposed. Wind comes straight off the Moray Firth, so a windproof layer matters more here than at most sites around Inverness.

## Inside the garrison

Below the walls, the fort shows what soldiers' lives looked like. Recreated barrack rooms show how crowded the quarters were, with bunks and kit laid out as they were in different eras. The Grand Magazine was built to store gunpowder and now holds a large collection of muskets, bayonets and swords.

There is also the garrison chapel, plain inside, still used by the military community. Remember that this is a working army barracks. Some buildings and areas are off limits, and you may see soldiers going about their day.

## The Highlanders' Museum

The Highlanders' Museum sits within the fort and tells the story of the Highland regiments, including the Queen's Own Highlanders, the Seaforths and the Camerons. Expect medals, uniforms, regimental silver and personal letters spanning more than two centuries of campaigns. It is one of the largest regimental collections in Scotland outside Edinburgh.

Allow an hour here on its own if military history interests you. It runs separately from the main fort, so check its opening days before you plan around it.

## Walking the ramparts properly

Most visitors head straight for the buildings by the entrance. Turn the other way and do the rampart loop first, while you still have energy. Then work back through the barrack rooms and museum.

- **Dolphins:** watch the channel toward Chanonry Point. Sightings are best around a rising tide, so check tide times before you go.
- **Timing:** the fort opens 10am to 4pm every day. On weekends visitor numbers stay low across that whole 10am–4pm window, so you won't have to dodge crowds. Arriving close to 10am still gives you time to see everything without rushing.
- **Paying:** card is accepted at the ticket desk. Fort George is run by Historic Environment Scotland, and current prices are on its website. Its membership and Explorer Pass are accepted here.
- **Military rules:** obey the signs on restricted areas, and don't photograph soldiers or base operations without asking.

The most expensive mistake is turning up mid-afternoon. With a 4pm close, a 2:30pm arrival leaves you rushing the walls and skipping the museum.

## Getting there and pairing it up

Fort George is about 11 miles northeast of Inverness, beyond the village of Ardersier. By car, take the A96 toward Nairn, then follow the B9006 north through Ardersier to the fort. There is parking at the site. Inverness Airport is only a few minutes away, so the fort works well on an arrival or departure day.

Public transport is possible but limited. Buses run from Inverness to Ardersier, leaving a final stretch on foot, so check current timetables first.

To fill a day, pair the fort with:

- **Culloden Battlefield**, about 15 minutes' drive, where the story that led to the fort begins
- **Cawdor Castle**, a short drive southeast
- **Nairn**, for its beach and harbour, east along the coast
- **Chanonry Point** on the Black Isle, if you want the dolphins from the other side
