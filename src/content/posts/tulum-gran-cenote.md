## A sinkhole with a staircase

Wooden steps take you down into a split in the limestone. At the bottom, two open pools are joined by a low cavern, and the water is clear enough that you can see your feet against the pale rock several metres down. Boardwalks run around the rim of the pools. Swimmers float between the light at either end.

This is one of the most-visited cenotes on the Riviera Maya. Its draw is that you can snorkel straight from a shallow ledge into a cave. Gran Cenote is an opening into Sac Actun, one of the longest known underwater cave systems in the world. Cave divers use it as an entry point, and snorkellers get to look into the start of that system.

## Getting out of Tulum town

The cenote is on Highway 109, the road from Tulum to Cobá, about 4 km west of the town centre. That distance makes it easy to reach by any of these:

- **Bicycle:** a flat ride out of town, and rentals are everywhere on Avenida Tulum. The shoulder is narrow in places, so ride early, before the traffic builds.
- **Colectivo:** the shared vans heading toward Cobá pass the entrance. Tell the driver "Gran Cenote" and he will stop at the turn-off.
- **Taxi:** about ten minutes from town. Agree the fare before you get in, because Tulum taxis do not use meters.
- **Car:** there is parking at the entrance, just off the highway.

Lots of people pair it with the Tulum ruins in the morning or with the Cobá ruins further up the same road. It also sits on the way to Cenote Calavera, a smaller sinkhole closer to town.

## Under the stalactites

The swim-through is what sets this cenote apart. A short stretch of the cavern links the two pools, and the ceiling drops low enough that you notice it. Stalactites hang above the water, and stalagmites rise from the bottom. In some spots the two have joined into columns.

Inside, the light turns blue-green, and you will often hear bats moving in the shadowed corners of the roof. Ropes mark the area where snorkellers may swim. Past them, the cave keeps going into flooded passages that are only for certified cave divers with a guide. Keep to the roped side. The darkness beyond is real cave, not scenery.

## Turtles, fish and the boardwalk ledge

Freshwater turtles live in the open pools. They often rest on the rocks and roots near the boardwalk, so look before you climb down. Small fish gather around the ledges as well, and a snorkel mask shows you far more of them than you can see from above.

Turtles and fish are wildlife, not a petting zoo. Watch them, photograph them, and leave them alone. They are a big reason the water rules here are so strict.

## Rules at the water's edge

Gran Cenote has more rules than a beach, and staff enforce them. The water is part of the aquifer that supplies drinking water across the Yucatán Peninsula, and whatever you wear on your skin ends up in it. Expect these:

- **Shower first:** you rinse off at the showers before you go in.
- **No sunscreen or repellent before swimming:** even products labelled "reef-safe" are usually not allowed. Put on a rash guard for sun protection instead.
- **Life jackets:** often required for some swimmers or in some areas. They are available to rent, and weak swimmers should take one anyway.
- **Rentals and lockers:** snorkel gear, life jackets and lockers are available on site. Bring your own mask if you prefer one that fits you well.

The water is cool. It feels cold after the heat of the road and fresh after twenty minutes in it. Water shoes help on the slippery limestone steps.

## Beating the vans to the water

This place is well known, and it fills up the way you would expect. Tour vans from Tulum, Playa del Carmen and Cancún usually arrive mid-morning, after collecting guests from hotels. If you get to the gate at opening, you will often have the pools mostly to yourself for a while. Check the opening hours before you set out, because they can change.

A few habits make the visit smoother:

- **Carry Mexican pesos.** Card payment at small cenotes along this road is not something to count on. The current entry price is posted at the ticket booth.
- **Say "buenos días" at the ticket window.** It costs nothing, and staff notice.
- **Ask whether drones and tripods are allowed before you set one up.** Many cenotes charge extra for cameras or ban certain kit, and the rules are posted at the entrance.
- **Leave food on the boardwalk.** Eating near the water is generally not allowed.

Tipping is not expected at the gate. If someone helps you with gear or fits a life jacket, a small tip in pesos is a common courtesy.

## How long to give it

One to two hours is right for most visitors. That gives you time to shower and swim the cavern twice. Then sit on the boardwalk and watch the turtles while your hair dries. Strong snorkellers who like to stay in the water can easily stay longer.

If you only see one cenote in Tulum, this one shows you the most variety in a single stop: open-air pools, a lit cave, wildlife and clear water. If you want quiet, the busier hours here will feel like a public pool. In that case, go first thing in the morning, or add a second, smaller cenote further along the Cobá road to the same day.
