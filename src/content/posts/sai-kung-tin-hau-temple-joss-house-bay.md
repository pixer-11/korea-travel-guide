This is the largest and oldest of Hong Kong's Tin Hau temples, dating back to 1266 according to a stone tablet on site, though the current structure was rebuilt in 1962 after fire damage. It sits on a slope overlooking Joss House Bay, the small inlet that takes its name directly from the temple. Fishing families have prayed here to Tin Hau, goddess of the sea, for protection on the water for generations.

## Finding your way to the temple

There's no MTR station nearby; the practical route is bus or taxi from Sai Kung town or Clear Water Bay Road. Minibus 16 runs from Sai Kung to Po Toi O, from which it's a signposted walk up to the temple gate. Drivers can use the small car park just below the entrance, though it fills quickly on weekends.

The approach itself is part of the experience: a steep staircase lined with incense coils and stone lions leads up from the road. Views open up over Joss House Bay and the moored fishing boats below. Give yourself 30 to 45 minutes, more if you want to sit and take in the harbour.

## Reading the temple grounds

Inside, the smell of sandalwood incense sits thick in the low-ceilinged main hall. The temple's centrepiece is a large carved stone inscribed in 1274, one of the oldest dated inscriptions in Hong Kong, recording the visit of two Song dynasty officials. Side halls hold smaller shrines to other folk deities, including Kwun Yum and the Earth God.

Outside, a huge incense burner stands permanently lit, and worshippers circle it clockwise before entering. A separate pavilion nearby houses a giant pair of fortune-telling blocks and a large ceremonial anchor, a nod to the temple's maritime purpose. Photography is generally fine outdoors; be more discreet inside if people are actively praying.

## Timing your visit around the crowds

The temple is genuinely under-the-radar for a site this historic, rarely mentioned in the same breath as Sai Kung's seafood restaurants or the Country Park trails. Weekdays between 8am and 9am are the quietest window, with the halls largely empty and the incense smoke still settling from the morning's first visitors.

Weekends are busier, with steady footfall from 9am to 4pm as local families come to make offerings. The one exception is Tin Hau's birthday, the 23rd day of the third lunar month (usually April or May), when the site draws crowds and a flotilla of decorated fishing boats into the bay. Outside that festival, expect a calm, unhurried visit even at midday.

## What locals actually do there

Regular worshippers bring their own incense sticks and small fruit offerings rather than buying elaborate sets. Bowing three times with incense held at chest height, facing the main altar, is the standard gesture; you're welcome to observe quietly without joining in. Donations go into marked boxes rather than to individuals collecting cash, and shoes stay on throughout. Since opening hours run only 8am to 5pm daily, plan to be back down the staircase before dusk, when the site closes and lighting on the path is minimal.
