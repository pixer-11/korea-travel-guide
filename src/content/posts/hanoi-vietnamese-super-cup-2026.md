## Why this match matters

The Super Cup is Vietnamese football's season-opener curtain-raiser in reverse: rather than kicking off the year, it closes the loop between two separate competitions, matching whoever won the V.League 1 title against whoever lifted the Vietnamese Cup that season. It's a one-off, winner-take-all evening — no away legs, no group stage, just 90 minutes (plus whatever it takes) to settle bragging rights.

The 27th edition was set for Hanoi on August 30, 2026, at Hàng Đẫy Stadium, one of the country's most storied football grounds. If the same club wins both the league and the cup, the runner-up in one of those competitions usually steps in instead.

## Getting to Hàng Đẫy Stadium

Hàng Đẫy sits in Đống Đa District, close to the heart of Hanoi, not out in a suburban sports complex — which is part of its charm. It's walkable from the Old Quarter and West Lake area if you don't mind a 30-40 minute stroll, but most visitors arrive by taxi or ride-hailing app (Grab is the standard here), which is cheap and simple to flag from anywhere downtown.

The stadium is wedged into a dense residential grid, so on match days the narrow streets around it fill with parked motorbikes and street vendors well before kickoff. Coming on foot or by bike-taxi often beats sitting in a car queue.

## What the atmosphere feels like

Hàng Đẫy is a compact bowl, and that's exactly why it has a reputation for noise. Unlike Vietnam's larger national stadiums, the stands here sit close to the pitch, so the drums, flares, and chanting from ultras groups (Hanoi FC's supporters are especially vocal when the club is involved) bounce right back at the players. Outside the gates there's the smell of grilled corn and sugarcane juice from vendors, plastic stools set up on the pavement for pre-match beer, and a crowd that skews toward hardcore domestic fans rather than casual tourists — this is a proper club-football night, not a sanitized showcase event.

## What to see and do around the fixture

Because the Super Cup is a single-city, single-night event, most travelers build a broader Hanoi visit around it. The Temple of Literature, Vietnam's first national university, is a short ride from the stadium and rewards a couple of unhurried hours. The Old Quarter's 36 streets — each historically tied to a trade — are a natural pre-match wander for food: bún chả, chả cá, and egg coffee are all within easy reach.

For a football-adjacent stop, there are Hanoi FC memorabilia shops near the ground; supply is modest but genuine.

## When to go and how long to budget

Fans tend to be in the neighborhood two to three hours before kickoff — not because gates necessarily open that early, but because street food, pre-match beer culture, and simply soaking in the build-up outside the stadium are half the experience. The match itself runs the standard \~90 minutes plus stoppage time, with extra time and penalties possible since a winner must be decided on the night.

Late August in Hanoi is deep in the hot, humid, rainy season, a time for a light rain layer, and the air stays thick and warm even after sunset.

## How to visit like a local

Vietnamese fans typically buy tickets in person at the stadium box office in the days leading up to a big match, or through official club/VFF channels online when available. Resale scalping happens outside the gates, but prices and legitimacy there are unpredictable. Cash is still king for the street stalls and stools around Hàng Đẫy; card machines are rare at the outdoor vendors, so small-denomination đồng notes are the useful currency.

Tipping isn't expected. Fans stand and sing through the whole match rather than sitting quietly, and a row can empty into an aisle chant. This is a domestic club-football ground rather than a tourist showcase: English signage is limited and ticketing leans offline. Fixture, kickoff time, venue, and ticket details for the match were published by the Vietnam Football Federation and V.League official channels.
