## The town at the end of the line

No road reaches Aguas Calientes. You arrive by train along the Urubamba River, from Ollantaytambo or from Poroy outside Cusco, with PeruRail or Inca Rail. The line runs down a narrowing gorge until the valley walls are almost straight up on both sides.

The town is officially called Machu Picchu Pueblo, but nearly everyone uses its old name. It is a tight grid of hotels, restaurants and the market, squeezed between the river and the tracks. Almost every street exists to feed, house or transport people bound for the ridge above.

Most visitors sleep here at least one night. A night in town is the only realistic way to be at the gate for the first entry slots without a pre-dawn train.

## Up the Hiram Bingham road

The ride up takes around 25 minutes. Shuttle buses leave from the stop by the river and grind up a dirt road of tight switchbacks. The drop off the outside edge gets steeper with every turn.

The alternative is your legs. A steep stone staircase climbs from Puente Ruinas, the bridge below town, to the sanctuary entrance. Fit walkers usually need around an hour and a half, and it is steps nearly all the way.

Bus tickets for the shuttle are sold in town and online, and the price is posted at the ticket office. Buy your return leg in advance too. The queue for the descent in mid-afternoon can be long.

## Reading the stones

The site is a 15th-century Inca city, generally linked to the emperor Pachacuti. It sits on a saddle between two peaks at around 2,400 metres. Hiram Bingham brought it to international attention in 1911. UNESCO listed it in 1983.

Your ticket assigns you a fixed, one-way circuit, so you won't see everything on a single visit. Depending on the route, look for:

- **The agricultural terraces**, stepped down the slope, often with llamas grazing on them
- **The Temple of the Sun**, a curved tower of fitted granite blocks
- **The Temple of the Three Windows**, which faces the main plaza
- **The Intihuatana**, a carved ritual stone on a raised platform
- **The Guardhouse viewpoint**, where the classic photograph of the whole city is taken

The stonework is the real draw. Up close, the best walls are cut so precisely that the blocks fit without mortar.

## The 7am window

Machu Picchu's crowd pattern follows the timed-entry system, and our foot-traffic data is clear about it. The calmest hour is 7am–8am, on weekdays and weekends alike. That is just after gates open at 6:30am and before the morning trains have delivered their day-trippers.

Try not to land on a weekend between 12pm and 3pm. That is the busiest stretch, with groups bunched up on the narrow stairways of the circuit.

Early slots have a second advantage. Cloud often fills the valley at first light and then lifts off the peaks as the morning goes on. Watching the ruins appear out of the mist is reason enough to set an alarm. Keep in mind that opening hours can shift through the year, so check the official site for this month's times.

## Getting through the gate without a hitch

The rules here are enforced, and getting them wrong at the gate is costly. Daily visitor numbers are capped and tickets are sold for a specific entry hour, so book well ahead. Use the Ministry of Culture's official ticket site or a licensed agency. In the dry season, tickets for Huayna Picchu and Machu Picchu Mountain sell out first.

At the entrance:

- **Bring the original passport** you used to book. Staff check the name against your ticket.
- **Arrive within your hour.** Late arrivals can be turned away.
- **Pack light.** Large backpacks, tripods, drones and walking sticks with metal tips are restricted, and lockers sit outside the entrance.
- **Use the toilets first.** The only ones are outside the gate, and the circuit doesn't loop back to them.

Inside, stay on the marked path, don't climb the walls and don't eat among the ruins. Guards will whistle at you if you cross a rope. Licensed guides wait at the entrance if you didn't book one, and on some circuits a guide is mandatory.

## Dry months, green months

The weather changes the trip. May to September is the dry season: clearer skies, cold early mornings and the heaviest demand for tickets and trains. Book these months earliest.

November to March brings the rains, with afternoon downpours, slick stone steps and much greener terraces. The classic Inca Trail closes every February for maintenance, but the sanctuary itself stays open. Whatever the month, pack a light rain layer, sunscreen and insect repellent. Sandflies around the lower terraces are a known nuisance.

## After the ruins, back in Aguas Calientes

Most people spend three to four hours on the site, so you're back down by early afternoon. The town gets its name from the thermal baths at the top of the main street, a popular place to soak legs sore from the stairs.

Near Puente Ruinas, the Manuel Chávez Ballón Site Museum covers the excavation and the Inca builders. Next to it is a botanical garden with orchids. Both are a short walk from town along the river road and make a quieter last few hours before your train back up the valley.
