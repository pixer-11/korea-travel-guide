You've probably already sung the song about it without knowing what you were picturing. "Sur le pont d'Avignon" is one of France's most exported nursery rhymes, and the bridge behind it is a stub of stone arches jutting into the Rhône, stopping abruptly halfway across the river.

That abrupt stop is the whole story. What you're looking at is the survivor of a structure that once had 22 arches and ran the full 900 meters to Villeneuve-lès-Avignon on the opposite bank. Flooding took out sections repeatedly over the centuries; by the 1600s, rebuilding it was abandoned for good, and the Rhône simply kept the remaining four.

## Why this bridge, and why here

The bridge dates to the 12th century, and legend credits its construction to a shepherd named Bénézet, said to have been moved by a divine vision to build it. Whether or not you buy the miracle, the name stuck: Pont Saint-Bénézet.

It mattered because Avignon mattered. Once the Rhône crossing was in place, the city became the key link between Provence and the Comtat Venaissin, and later the seat of the papacy itself during the 14th-century Avignon Papacy. The bridge and the Palais des Papes rising above it were built for the same reason: to control this stretch of river.

Partway along the remaining arches sits a small two-level chapel dedicated to St Nicholas, patron saint of boatmen, a reminder that this was a working river crossing, not a monument.

## What you actually see and do

There's no crossing to the other side anymore. You walk out onto the surviving arches, look down at the Rhône moving underneath, and look back at the walled city and the Palais des Papes over your shoulder. The view of both together is the reason people come.

Inside the entry fee is an audio guide, included in most tickets, that walks you through the bridge's construction, collapses and rebuilds, and its odd afterlife as a folk song. It's worth taking; there's nothing else on-site explaining what you're looking at.

Give it 30 to 45 minutes. It's not a long visit, but the light on the water and the angle back toward the Palais des Papes are worth lingering for a few extra minutes rather than rushing through.

## When to go

This is one of Avignon's most visited sights, and it shows, especially on weekend afternoons.

- Quietest on weekdays, 9am to 11am
- Weekends are calmer 9am to noon
- Avoid weekend afternoons from 2pm to 7pm, when it's busiest
- The bridge is open daily, 9am to 7pm, year-round

Arriving right at opening on a weekday is the easiest way to get the view to yourself, or close to it, before coach groups visiting the Palais des Papes next door spill over.

## Getting there and tickets

The bridge sits on Boulevard du Rhône, right below the ramparts, an easy walk of 10-15 minutes from the city centre and the Palais des Papes. There's no dedicated car park at the bridge itself; the Parking des Italiens or Parking de l'Île Piot, across the river, are the usual options, with a shuttle bus into town from the Île Piot side.

Ticket booths are at the entrance on the city side. A combined ticket with the Palais des Papes is generally the better value if you're visiting both, since they sit a few minutes apart. Prices and exact combo deals shift year to year, so check the official Avignon tourism or Palais des Papes site before you go.

## Making sense of the ruins

Don't expect a bridge you can walk across; that's the most common confusion for first-time visitors, since the postcard image suggests a full span. What you're paying to see is four arches and the chapel, plus the story told through the audio guide.

Pair it with a walk along the Rhône bank opposite, in Villeneuve-lès-Avignon, where you can see the bridge's stubby end from across the water and understand its original scale. The Tour Philippe le Bel there marks roughly where the bridge used to land.

Photographers favour late afternoon light from the Rocher des Doms gardens above the river, which frame the bridge, the water, and the Palais des Papes in a single view without needing a ticket at all.
