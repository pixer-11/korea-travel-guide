## A festival built around one idea

Every edition has a single theme. The Cairo International Art Festival picks one subject each year and gathers the work around it, so the whole programme answers the same question from different angles.

The stated aim is dialogue. The festival places Cairo as a hub where the Arab art scene and the international one meet, rather than a stop on someone else's touring circuit. That framing matters on the ground. You are as likely to stand in front of work from Beirut, Amman or Casablanca as from Europe, often in the same room.

## Ten days, two Fridays

This year's festival runs from Wednesday, November 11 to Friday, November 20, 2026. That calendar quirk shapes the crowd more than anything else.

Egypt's weekend is Friday and Saturday, not Saturday and Sunday. So the festival opens midweek, passes through one full Cairo weekend on November 13–14, and closes on a Friday. Local visitors, students and families mostly come on those two Fridays and the Saturday between them. The Sunday-to-Thursday days belong to working Cairo, and gallery rooms usually feel calmer then.

A rough way to split the ten days:

- **November 11–12 (Wed–Thu):** opening days, when the art crowd and press usually turn out
- **November 13–14 (Fri–Sat):** the busiest stretch, with Cairenes off work
- **November 15–19 (Sun–Thu):** working-week days, the slower pace for long looking
- **November 20 (Fri):** closing day, and the second weekend crowd

Friday midday prayer is a fixed point in the week. Streets near mosques get quiet around noon, then the city fills up again in the afternoon.

What does stay fixed is the geography. Nearly all of Cairo's serious exhibition space sits in a few districts beside the Nile, so the next section is useful wherever this year's rooms turn out to be.

## Cairo's art map: Gezira, Zamalek and Downtown

Most of Cairo's art scene sits within a few kilometres of Tahrir Square. Knowing these districts makes a festival week far easier to plan.

**Gezira island, the southern half.** This is the state's cultural compound. The Cairo Opera House grounds hold the Museum of Modern Egyptian Art and the Palace of Arts, a large hall used for national exhibitions. Opera station on Metro Line 2 is a short walk from the Opera gates.

**Zamalek, the northern half of the same island.** This is the private-gallery district. Leafy streets of old apartment blocks hide commercial galleries on ground floors and in converted flats, often marked only by a small brass plate beside the door. People hop between them on foot over an afternoon.

**Downtown (Wust el-Balad).** Here the galleries and independent spaces sit among the belle-époque buildings around Talaat Harb Street, with peeling façades and wrought-iron balconies. Sadat station, the Line 1 and Line 2 interchange under Tahrir Square, puts you on its edge.

**Dokki, across the river to the west.** The Mahmoud Khalil Museum holds a well-known collection of French Impressionist and 19th-century European painting in a riverside villa. It makes a useful counterpoint to contemporary festival work.

## Crossing the city between rooms

Cairo traffic is the real timetable. A ride that looks like ten minutes on a map can take forty around 2pm or after dark on a weekday.

The Metro is the reliable option for the Gezira and Downtown legs. Line 2 links Sadat and Opera in a single stop under the river, and the system has women-only carriages near the middle of each train. Ride-hailing apps such as Uber and Careem are widely used, and the price shows before you set off, which saves haggling.

Walking works well inside each district but not between them. The bridges over the Nile, Qasr al-Nil from Tahrir to Gezira most of all, are long and exposed. They are pleasant in November and much less so in a jammed rush hour. Visitors who group venues by district usually see far more than those who cross the city twice a day.

## Gallery manners in Cairo

Cairo galleries are relaxed, but a few habits make the visit smoother.

- **Greet whoever is at the desk.** A quick "salaam alaykum" or "masa' el-kheir" (good evening) goes a long way. Attendants often walk you through the show if you show interest.
- **Ask before photographing.** Some spaces welcome phones and some exhibitors don't. A gesture towards your camera and a raised eyebrow does the job.
- **Dress for the street, not the white cube.** Shoulders and knees covered is the easy default for moving through Downtown and between districts.
- **Carry small Egyptian pound notes.** Cards work in many galleries and cafés, but cash pays for taxis, tea and the baksheesh tips given to doormen and parking attendants.
- **Expect late evenings.** Openings in Cairo tend to start after sunset and run on, and the crowd often moves on to a nearby café afterwards.

Conversation is part of the point here. Artists and gallerists often attend their own shows, and a question about the work usually gets a long, generous answer.

## November in Cairo

November is one of the most comfortable months to be in the city. Days are warm and dry without summer's heat, and evenings cool enough that a light layer helps after dark.

The festival dates also fall in the main sightseeing season. Many visitors pair a day of galleries with the Giza Plateau or the Egyptian Museum on Tahrir Square. Downtown is the practical base for an art-focused stay: it sits between Gezira, Zamalek and the Metro interchange at Sadat.
