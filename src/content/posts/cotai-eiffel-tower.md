## Half the height, every girder

It stands on the Cotai Strip, half the size of the original. Steel lattice rises out of the front of The Parisian Macao, painted in the same bronze-brown as the Paris tower. The four splayed legs and the arched base all match, and so does the tapering spire. At half scale it still looms over the taxi drop-off and the fountain-lined forecourt.

The replica is part of the resort, not a separate monument. Its base feeds straight into the hotel and the shopping arcade. You reach the platforms from inside the building, and that shapes the whole visit. You will walk through marble lobbies and past shopfronts to get to a lift up a lattice tower.

## Two platforms, two different trips

There are two observation platforms, and they suit different visits. The lower one sits in the broad first section of the tower, where the legs curve together. Up here you are close to the ironwork itself: rivet heads, cross-bracing, the underside of the structure framing the view.

The upper platform is the reason most people buy a ticket. It sits high in the narrowing upper shaft, on the level the resort calls the 37th floor. It feels exposed in a way the hotel towers around it never do. Expect a fenced, compact deck rather than a wide terrace. Space is tight when a group arrives together.

## What the top actually shows you

The view is mostly Cotai, and that is the point. Cotai is reclaimed land between Taipa and Coloane, and from above it reads like a planned city of hotels:

- **The Venetian Macao** directly across the road, with its campanile replica and long cream façade
- **The resort row** running along the Strip, with City of Dreams, Studio City and Galaxy Macau in their distinct shapes
- **Taipa's hills** and older low-rise streets towards the north-east
- **Hengqin** across the narrow channel to the west, already part of mainland China

You won't get the Macau Peninsula skyline or the Ruins of St. Paul's up close. For those, head north across the bridges. The view here is about the strange geometry of the resort district itself.

## After dark on the Strip

The tower changes character once the sun goes down. It is lit after dark, and the bronze lattice turns into a frame of light at the end of the Strip. Many visitors photograph it from the forecourt or from across the road outside The Venetian. That spot gives you the whole tower without craning your neck.

Closing time is currently 9:15 PM, so an evening visit to the platforms is possible. You can ride up in daylight, watch the resort lights come on, then come down to photograph the tower itself. Sunset in Macau falls earlier in winter than in summer, so check the time on the day you go. Then arrive with enough margin to see both.

## Reaching The Parisian

The address is simply the hotel: 巴黎人酒店, The Parisian Macao. Every taxi driver in Macau knows it. Showing the Chinese name helps if your driver's English is limited.

- **Free resort shuttles:** the Sands resorts run complimentary buses from points such as the ferry terminals and the Border Gate area. Routes and stops change, so check the current list on the official Sands/Parisian site.
- **Light rail:** the Taipa Line of the Macau Light Rapid Transit runs through Cotai, with stations within walking distance of the Strip.
- **On foot from next door:** indoor walkways link The Parisian with neighbouring Sands properties. If you are already at The Venetian, you can stay out of the heat or rain most of the way.

Inside, follow the signs for the tower through the Shoppes at Parisian. The building is large and the signage is mostly reliable, but leave a few minutes to find it.

## Getting up without wasting the afternoon

Tickets are sold inside The Parisian, and the current prices are posted at the ticket counter and on the official site. Check whether your ticket covers one platform or both before you pay. That decides how long you'll spend.

Some practical habits help here:

- **Pay how Macau pays.** Patacas are the local currency, and Hong Kong dollars are widely accepted across the city. Change often comes back in patacas, so card is simpler for one-off purchases.
- **Expect a queue for the lift.** The lifts are small and groups go up in batches. When a tour group has just arrived, waiting a few minutes or visiting the lower platform first can save you standing in line.
- **Travel light.** You'll pass through a hotel-casino complex before you ever reach the lift. A small bag is easier to manage than a backpack or tripod.
- **Mind the casino rules.** Gaming floors in Macau are off-limits to under-21s, and photography inside them is not permitted. Route around them if you are with children or carrying a camera.

The tower is a well-known stop on Cotai itineraries, not a quiet corner. The calmer experience comes from timing. Weekday afternoons are generally easier than weekend evenings, when day-trippers from Hong Kong and the mainland fill the resorts.

## How long, and what to pair it with

Allow around an hour for the tower: getting through the building, waiting for the lift, and time on the platforms. Add more if you plan to stay for dusk.

The tower works best as part of a Cotai half-day. The Venetian's canal-lined Grand Canal Shoppes are across the road, and the Parisian's own shopping arcade sits at the tower's feet. Taipa Village is a short ride away, with its narrow lanes, Portuguese-era houses and snack shops along Rua do Cunha. It's a useful antidote to an afternoon spent inside resort air-conditioning.
