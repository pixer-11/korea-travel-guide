## Ousi on the Lima coast

Omar Courtz belongs to the newer generation of Puerto Rican urbano artists. His music sits between reggaeton and Latin trap, with melodic hooks over dembow rhythm. If you know the sound coming out of San Juan in recent years, you know the territory.

Peru has a huge appetite for that sound. Reggaeton plays in Lima's taxis, gyms and *combis*, and the big Puerto Rican names fill large venues here. Expect a crowd that knows every word, and mostly a young one.

## Getting to Arena 1 in San Miguel

San Miguel is a residential and commercial district west of central Lima. It runs down to the Costa Verde cliffs above the Pacific. The district is also home to the Plaza San Miguel shopping centre and the Parque de las Leyendas zoo, which drivers will know as landmarks.

Lima's Metro Line 1 does not serve San Miguel, and neither does the Metropolitano bus. In practice, concertgoers arrive by:

- **App taxi**, using Uber, Cabify or inDrive, which most visitors find simplest
- **Shared rides** with friends, splitting the fare four ways
- **Private car**, though parking near a sold-out venue is a gamble

Traffic is the real obstacle. Saturday evening jams on Avenida La Marina and Avenida Universitaria can turn a short drive into a long one. Fans coming from Miraflores or Barranco usually allow far more time than the map suggests.

Jorge Chávez International Airport is in Callao, next door to San Miguel. That suits anyone flying in for the weekend.

## Spring nights on the Pacific

Late November is late spring in Lima. The grey winter mist, the *garúa*, has mostly lifted, and the days feel warm. Evenings by the coast are cooler, and sea air comes up off the cliffs.

Locals going to a night show usually bring a light jacket or hoodie they can tie round the waist once the crowd heats up. Sunset over the Pacific comes a little after 6pm at this time of year. By the time the headliner is on, it is fully dark.

## Ceviche before, pollo a la brasa after

Lima eats on its own schedule, and that shapes a concert day. Cevicherías traditionally serve at lunchtime, because limeños like their fish fresh and in daylight. If you want ceviche on show day, have it at lunch, not dinner.

After the show, the city turns to night food:

- **Pollo a la brasa**, rotisserie chicken with fries and salad, the default late meal across Lima
- **Anticuchos**, grilled skewers of beef heart, sold from carts and in *anticucherías*
- **Salchipapas**, sliced sausage over fries with sauces, a cheap street favourite

Chicken shops (*pollerías*) are everywhere in San Miguel and on the main avenues heading back towards Miraflores. Groups of fans usually end up in one, still wearing their tour shirts.

## Moving through a Lima concert night

A few habits will make the night easier. Most of them are about money, phones and getting home.

**Paying.** The currency is the Peruvian sol. Cards work in malls and restaurants, but street vendors and kiosks often prefer cash or Yape and Plin, the mobile wallets most Peruvians use. Visitors without a Peruvian bank account can't use those, so small notes help.

**Phones.** Pickpocketing in a packed crowd is a known risk at big Lima shows. Regulars keep phones in a front pocket or a zipped bag. They film in short bursts rather than holding a screen up all night.

**Getting home.** When the encore ends, thousands of people open the same ride apps at once. Fares jump and drivers struggle to find riders. Many fans walk a few blocks from the venue before requesting a car, or wait at a nearby pollería until the rush thins.

**Taxis off the street.** Limeños generally avoid flagging unmarked cars late at night. They stick to app rides or official taxis, and that's the safer default for visitors too.

**The language of the crowd.** The chants will be in Puerto Rican Spanish slang, and the crowd will join in. Fans also yell *"¡otra!"* (another one!) when they want an encore.

## Where fans base themselves

Most visitors flying in for the show stay in **Miraflores**, which has the widest choice of hotels and the clifftop Malecón. **Barranco**, just south, suits people who want bars and a later night. **San Isidro** is quieter and business-focused, and it's slightly closer to San Miguel.

**Magdalena del Mar** sits right beside San Miguel. It's the short-ride option if you'd rather skip the cross-city traffic after the show. Whichever district you pick, the trip home after midnight is quicker than the trip there at dusk.
