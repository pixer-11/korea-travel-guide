## One November night on Avenida Felipe II

Bryan Adams has one Madrid date on this tour: November 10, 2026. He plays Movistar Arena, the indoor arena at the eastern end of the Salamanca district. Inside, the seating rises in steep tiers around a flat floor, so even the upper rows look down on the stage rather than across at it.

This is his only show in the capital on this run. The next Spanish stop is Palau Sant Jordi in Barcelona on November 16, six days later. That gives fans from outside Spain two cities to choose from, and gives the most committed ones a reason to see both.

## The songs people come for

Adams has spent four decades turning out radio staples. *Reckless* (1984) gave him "Summer of '69", "Run to You", "Heaven" and "Somebody". "(Everything I Do) I Do It for You", from *Robin Hood: Prince of Thieves*, spent sixteen straight weeks at number one in the UK in 1991. That record still stands.

The setlist for this date isn't public, so nobody can promise you a particular song. What his arena shows do have is a reputation. They run long, they lean on singalong choruses, and they draw crowds that know every word. Madrid arena audiences sing loudly, and a catalogue built on big choruses suits that.

## Inside Movistar Arena

The building has carried several names. Older locals still call it the Palacio de los Deportes, and for years it was the WiZink Center. The original sports hall burned down in 2001. The current dome-topped arena opened on the same site in 2005, and it now hosts basketball, concerts and big touring shows all year.

On Spanish tickets you will see a few words:

- **Pista**: the standing floor in front of the stage.
- **Grada**: the raked seating around the bowl, split into lower and upper tiers.
- **Puerta**: the numbered entrance gate printed on your ticket.
- **Acceso**: the specific access point to your section once you are inside.

Bars and merchandise stands line the inner concourse. Bag rules and entry policies appear on the venue's own website.

## Getting there without a car

The Metro is the obvious way in. **Goya** (Lines 2 and 4) puts you a short walk from the main façade along Calle de Goya and Felipe II. **O'Donnell** (Line 6) sits just to the south. Several EMT city buses run along Calle de Goya and Calle de Alcalá.

Parking near the arena is limited, and the streets around it fill up fast on show nights. Most concertgoers come by Metro and leave the same way. On a weekday night the Metro runs past midnight, which covers the end of a typical arena show. Taxis and ride-hail cars wait on the main avenues afterwards, though the queues are long when a full arena empties out at once.

## Before and after in Salamanca and Goya

The arena sits in one of Madrid's smartest neighbourhoods, so there is plenty around it. Calle de Goya is a long shopping street with tapas bars and cafés in the blocks off it. Parque del Retiro begins a few minutes' walk to the southwest. In November it is a good place for a late-afternoon stroll among the turning chestnut trees.

The Las Ventas bullring, a neo-Mudéjar landmark in red brick and tile, is one Metro stop northeast on Line 2. Visitors staying in the city centre usually take a hotel near Sol or Gran Vía. Line 2 runs from Sol to Goya directly, with no change.

## Doing a Madrid concert night the Madrid way

Madrid eats late, and concert nights change the order rather than the hour. Fans typically have a caña and a few tapas around Goya before the show. Dinner often comes after, since kitchens in the neighbourhood stay open late.

- **Ordering**: a *caña* is a small draught beer. A *tinto de verano* is red wine with lemon soda. Ask for *la cuenta* when you want the bill.
- **Paying**: contactless card and phone payments work almost everywhere, from bar counters to the arena concourse.
- **Tipping**: rounding up or leaving small change is normal. A percentage tip isn't expected.
- **Exits**: attendees heading for the Metro usually walk to Goya along Felipe II. Some walk the few extra minutes to O'Donnell, where the platforms are quieter.

Spanish arena crowds tend to stay put for encores. The rush to the Metro starts only when the house lights come up.
