## Why Miyamaso is worth the trip

Most of Kyoto's famous kitchens sit inside the grid of the old capital, a short walk from a subway exit. Miyamaso does the opposite. It's tucked into Hanase, a thickly forested valley in the mountains north of the city, where the restaurant operates as part of a traditional ryokan beside a stream you can hear from the dining room before you ever see it.

In 2026, Miyamaso became Kyoto's first restaurant in six years to be newly awarded Three MICHELIN Stars — a rare enough event that food writers across Japan took notice, since Kyoto's three-star roster had been effectively frozen for years. What sets it apart from other high-end kaiseki isn't lacquerware or rarefied technique alone, but its philosophy: tsumikusa, literally "picked grasses," a style of cooking built around what the surrounding mountainside actually yields that week — wild greens, mushrooms, roots, river fish — rather than what a supplier can ship in from elsewhere.

## What the setting feels like

You arrive to the sound of running water before you register the building itself. The ryokan sits low among cedar and maple, its wooden engawa facing the stream, so that even a dish plated indoors carries the smell of wet stone and leaf-litter drifting through the screens. Meals unfold slowly, course by course, in a rhythm dictated by the mountain's calendar rather than a printed menu: what was picked at dawn on the slopes behind the building is often what lands on the table by evening.

This is a kitchen where the forest is effectively a walk-in pantry, and the dining room is positioned so you're aware of that fact the whole time you eat.

## Getting there

Hanase lies north of Kyoto's city center, well beyond the reach of the subway or JR lines that serve Kiyomizu-dera or Arashiyama. Realistically, you're looking at close to an hour's drive from downtown Kyoto, through winding mountain roads that climb steadily away from the basin. A hired car or taxi is the most reliable option; there are local bus routes that run toward the Hanase area, but connections are infrequent and not built around visitor schedules, so budget considerably longer if you go that way and confirm the current timetable before committing to it.

Because of the distance and the pace of the meal itself, this isn't a stop you slot between two other sights — it's the whole day's plan.

## What to expect at the table

Don't go in picturing a fixed, describable menu — the entire point of tsumikusa cuisine is that it shifts with the season and even the week, built from whatever foraging in the surrounding hills has turned up. Expect a multi-course kaiseki-style progression, unhurried, with long pauses between dishes that let you take in the stream and the trees rather than rush to the next course.

Pricing at this tier of Kyoto dining sits firmly at the high end, and reservations are the single biggest obstacle — demand has understandably spiked since the Michelin announcement. Contact the ryokan directly well ahead of your trip to confirm availability, current hours, and how they'd like you to arrange transport, since none of this is the kind of detail worth guessing at.

## How to visit like a local

Kyoto diners who chase these mountain kaiseki experiences tend to build the whole day around the meal rather than around sightseeing. Book as far in advance as the restaurant allows — for a newly minted three-star kitchen in a remote setting, that can mean months, not weeks. Arrange your car or driver in advance too, since taxis are scarce once you're up in Hanase and you don't want to be stranded after a long lunch.

Dress and behave as you would at any formal ryokan meal: quiet voices, punctuality, and patience with the pacing, since courses are timed to what's fresh that day rather than to a kitchen trying to turn tables. Payment at this level of restaurant is usually settled by card or arranged in advance through the ryokan rather than paid casually at a counter, but confirm their preferred method when you reserve.

And don't try to combine it with another major sight the same day — the drive alone eats a significant chunk of daylight, so let Miyamaso be the destination, not a stop along the way.
