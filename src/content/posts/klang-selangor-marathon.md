## A race through the royal town

Klang doesn't look like a postcard. It is Selangor's royal town and an old river port that loaded tin and rubber for the British. Today it is a low-rise sprawl of shophouse rows, highway flyovers and mosque domes. On Sunday, 1 November 2026, it hosts the Selangor Marathon.

The distances run from a 5K up to the full 42.195km. That means one set of start pens holds first-timers in new shoes and seasoned runners chasing a time. When the race is over, you're in a town known across Malaysia for its breakfast, and the course is a good way to work up an appetite for it.

## Why the start gun fires in the dark

Malaysian road marathons beat the sun by starting early. Full-marathon fields across the country usually set off well before dawn, and the shorter distances follow later in the morning. Each category's start time is up to the organiser. Runners still plan around the same thing: by mid-morning the heat and humidity become the real opponent.

November on Selangor's west coast is warm and heavy even at dawn. The late-year monsoon shift often brings thunderstorms in the afternoon, and sometimes rain overnight. That leaves roads wet and the air thick at the start. Experienced local runners tend to take on water and isotonic drink at every station. They don't wait until they feel thirsty.

## Getting into Klang before the roads close

The KTM Komuter Port Klang Line runs from KL Sentral to Klang station in about an hour. It is the easy way into town on any normal day. But trains don't run in the small hours, so the railway is no help for a start before dawn.

What runners usually do instead:

- **Drive in** along the Federal Highway or the KESAS Highway, and park well outside the closed roads
- **Take a Grab**, Malaysia's main ride-hailing app, for a drop-off at the edge of the course
- **Stay the night** in Klang or nearby Shah Alam, so they can walk or take a short ride to the start

Roads on the route close for the morning. Drivers who aren't racing should expect detours around the town centre until the course reopens.

## Race-morning manners on Klang's roads

Local race culture follows a few habits that are worth knowing before the start:

- **Race kit collection** at Malaysian races usually happens on set days before the race, often at a mall or sports venue. Many organisers let a friend collect your kit if they bring a signed authorisation letter and a copy of your ID.
- **Water stations** usually hand out plain water and an isotonic drink in cups. Runners take a cup without stopping, then throw it in or near the bins just after the table.
- **Keep to the side** if you're walking, so runners can pass. Marshals often speak Malay first, and a quick "terima kasih" (thank you) goes down well.
- **Paying for things** around town works with cash or a DuitNow QR code. Touch 'n Go eWallet is accepted at most stalls and kopitiam. Tipping isn't expected.
- **Visiting a mosque or temple** after the race means covering shoulders and knees. A sweaty finisher's singlet won't do, so runners usually pack a change of clothes in their bag drop.

## Bak kut teh at the finish line

Klang says it invented bak kut teh, and here people eat it for breakfast. That makes it the obvious meal after a race. The dish is pork ribs slow-cooked in a dark herbal broth with garlic, star anise and dang gui (angelica root). It usually comes to the table in a claypot or deep bowl.

How a typical order works:

- **The soup**, with ribs, belly or offal, chosen at the counter or from a laminated menu
- **White rice**, sometimes topped with fried shallots
- **You tiao**, fried dough sticks, cut up for dipping in the broth
- **Chinese tea**, brewed at the table on a small kettle
- **Dry bak kut teh**, a thicker stir-fried version with dried chilli and dried cuttlefish, which many Klang shops also sell

A lot of bak kut teh shops open early and sell out by lunchtime, which suits a morning race. The dish is pork and isn't halal. Muslim runners and their families usually go to Little India or a mamak restaurant for roti canai and teh tarik.

## The rest of race day in Klang

Once you've showered, the town has a short list of things to see that fit around tired legs.

- **Little India, Jalan Tengku Kelana:** banana-leaf rice, sari shops and sweet stalls. Deepavali falls in October or November, and in the weeks before it the street fills with lights and festival stalls.
- **Sultan Sulaiman Royal Mosque:** built in the early 1930s to a British design. It mixes Art Deco lines with Moorish domes.
- **Istana Alam Shah:** the Sultan of Selangor's palace. Visitors see it from outside the gates only.
- **Pulau Ketam:** a fishing village on stilts, reached by ferry from the jetty at Port Klang. It's known for seafood lunches and is a gentle half-day trip if you can still walk the boardwalks.

Most out-of-town runners head back to Kuala Lumpur in the afternoon. The Komuter is running again by then, so the hour back to KL Sentral is an easy ride for anyone too tired to drive.
