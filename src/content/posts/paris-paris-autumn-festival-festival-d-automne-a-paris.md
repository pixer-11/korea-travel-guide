## A season instead of a weekend

This festival has no main stage. It runs for four months, scattered across the city's theatres, concert halls and suburban arts centres. One week you might see a choreographer's premiere near the Seine. The next might bring a five-hour piece of experimental theatre at the far end of a metro line.

The 2026 edition runs from September to December. It brings around 50 productions and draws roughly 150,000 attendees over the season. The festival was founded in 1972 and still sticks to its original idea: commission and present new, demanding work, often from artists who have never been staged in France before.

## What "avant-garde" means on this programme

Plenty of the work here won't fit one category. Expect shows that mix theatre with live music, or dance pieces built around a single repeated gesture. You might find a concert where the score matters more than the players. In past editions the festival has had long relationships with figures like Robert Wilson and Merce Cunningham, and that spirit still sets the tone.

Several shows a year are long or unusual in form, and some are performed in languages other than French. Before buying, audiences usually read the programme note for each production. It gives:

- running time and whether there is an interval
- the language of performance and any surtitles
- the discipline (theatre, dance, music, visual art, film)
- whether it is a premiere or a new commission

## The Portrait: the festival's signature thread

The festival often builds part of its season around a **Portrait**: several works by one artist, spread over the autumn. Following a Portrait means seeing an artist's early pieces beside new ones, often in different venues across the city. If you only have a few nights in Paris, one Portrait show is a good way to see what the festival stands for.

Portrait artists come from every discipline: choreographers, theatre directors, composers. Regulars sometimes plan their whole autumn around one, and these shows are where the festival's international reputation was made.

## A festival without a front door

Each production belongs to a partner venue, so the "venue" for your ticket is a specific theatre, not a festival ground. In past editions the partners have included central houses such as the Théâtre de la Ville and the Théâtre du Châtelet. Both face each other across Place du Châtelet.

Many shows also play beyond the Périphérique, at arts centres such as MC93 in Bobigny and Nanterre-Amandiers. These trips are part of the appeal. Suburban houses often stage the most ambitious productions, in large modern auditoriums built for experimental work.

Getting there is usually straightforward:

- **Châtelet** (Metro Lines 1, 4, 7, 11, 14; RER A, B, D) for the two Châtelet theatres
- **Bobigny–Pablo Picasso** (Metro Line 5) for MC93
- **Nanterre-Préfecture** (RER A) for Nanterre-Amandiers

Late shows in the suburbs can end close to the last trains. Audiences heading back to central Paris tend to leave the bar quickly after the curtain call.

## Tickets and the season pass

There is no festival wristband: you buy each show separately. The organiser publishes dates, venues and ticket links on festival-automne.com. Tickets for many productions are also sold through the partner venue's own box office. Prices change by venue and seat category, and each show's page lists the current price.

The festival has traditionally offered a subscription. You commit to several shows and pay less per ticket than buying them one at a time. Parisian regulars use it heavily, which is why the most talked-about productions in small rooms can fill up long before opening night. Visitors who are only in town for a short stay usually pick single shows instead.

## In the seat: how a Paris theatre audience behaves

Paris audiences take the starting time seriously. Many venues won't admit latecomers until a suitable break, and some never do. Regulars usually reach the foyer with time for a drink at the bar, then go in when the doors open.

A few things are worth knowing:

- **Seating:** ticket categories set where you sit. Some smaller or experimental shows have unreserved seating (*placement libre*), and that is when people queue at the door.
- **Phones:** they go off completely. Ushers here do step in when they see a screen lit up.
- **Applause:** curtain calls can go on, with several rounds for the company. Leaving during them is noticed.
- **Walkouts:** with demanding work, a few people leave mid-show. Nobody makes a scene of it, and the polite way is to wait for a natural pause.
- **Language:** surtitles are often in French only. Read the show's page for the language before buying if you don't speak French.

People dress casually, often all in black, and nobody expects formal clothes. Ask for a *programme de salle* (the house programme) at the door. These are often free and include the artist's notes.

## Building an autumn trip around it

The festival covers Paris's quieter cultural months, after the summer crowds and before Christmas. Visitors often pick a week, then choose two or three shows in different disciplines: one central evening at Châtelet, one suburban production, one concert or exhibition.

Châtelet puts you a short walk from the Île de la Cité and the Marais, so dinner before or after a show is easy to arrange. For suburban venues, many people eat near the theatre. Most of the larger arts centres have their own café or bar, open before and after the performance.

September and October usually bring milder evenings for walking between the metro and the theatre. November and December are darker and wetter, and that is also when the festival's longest, most demanding works tend to feel at home.
