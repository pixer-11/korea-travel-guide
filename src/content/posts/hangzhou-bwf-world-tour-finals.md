## Five days, five draws, no easy rounds

This is the tournament where nobody plays to warm up. Only the top eight in each discipline qualify, based on the season's World Tour standings. Every match, from the first session, is between players who spent the year at the top.

The five disciplines are:

- Men's singles
- Women's singles
- Men's doubles
- Women's doubles
- Mixed doubles

The format differs from a normal knockout. Each discipline splits into two groups of four for a round robin, and the top two from each group go through to the semifinals. Finals day is December 13, the last day of the run. The group stage means a player can lose once and still win the title.

## The arena on the Qiantang's south bank

Hangzhou staged the 2023, 2024 and 2025 editions at the Hangzhou Olympic Sports Center Gymnasium. It sits in Binjiang district, beside the petal-shaped main stadium locals call the "Big Lotus", which was built for the 2022 Asian Games (held in 2023). The BWF names the 2026 venue on its official event page.

Olympic Sports Center Station, where Metro Lines 6 and 7 meet, is a short walk from the complex. Many visitors stay across the river around Qianjiang New Town or near West Lake. From West Lake, the ride takes roughly 40 minutes by metro or taxi.

## Tickets, seats and sessions

Ticket links come from the organiser, and the BWF lists them on its official site. Early days usually run several sessions with a lot of courts' worth of matches. Weekend semifinals and finals draw the biggest home crowds, particularly when Chinese players are still in the draw.

Large sporting events in China usually sell tickets under real-name rules. Foreign fans normally buy with a passport number, and staff at the gate match the ticket to the passport. Bag checks at the entrance are standard. Lighters and large power banks are the usual items turned away.

December in Hangzhou is cool and damp, with daytime temperatures usually in single digits to low teens Celsius. Inside the arena it's warm, so fans tend to wear layers they can take off.

## Watching a rally with a Hangzhou crowd

Badminton has a loud, knowledgeable following in China. The crowd goes silent during a serve and through each rally. The noise comes in waves between points, and you'll hear "jiāyóu!" (加油, "add oil") roared for home players. Flash photography during play is not allowed, and stewards enforce that rule.

How spectators handle things:

- **Paying:** Alipay and WeChat Pay are standard at food stalls and merchandise stands. Both apps accept linked international cards, and fewer outlets take cash.
- **Asking for directions:** the Chinese name is 世界羽联世界巡回赛总决赛, and badminton is yǔmáoqiú (羽毛球). Taxi drivers know the stadium as Àotǐ Zhōngxīn (奥体中心).
- **Getting home:** at the end of an evening session, the crowd heads for the metro all at once. Many fans walk a few streets away before calling a DiDi.
- **Rest days in the city:** with matches mostly later in the day, fans often spend the mornings at West Lake, Lingyin Temple or the Longjing tea villages.
