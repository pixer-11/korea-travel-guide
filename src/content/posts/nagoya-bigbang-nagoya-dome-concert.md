## A silver roof in Higashi-ku

Nagoya Dome is hard to miss. Its huge silver shell sits in the residential east of the city, home ground of the Chunichi Dragons baseball team, and holds about 40,000 people for a game. Outside the baseball season it turns into one of Japan's big touring stops.

BIGBANG plays two nights here, Saturday December 5 and Sunday December 6, 2026, on the XX : COSMOS world tour. For a group that played Japan's dome circuit for years in the 2010s, Nagoya is familiar ground. Signs around the stadium may show a sponsor's name instead of "Nagoya Dome", but it is the same building.

## How tickets reach fans in Japan

Japanese dome tickets mostly come through a lottery. Fan-club members usually get the first draw, then general lotteries follow through the big Japanese ticketing platforms. Winners pay for a seat in a section without knowing exactly where it is.

The actual seat often stays hidden until shortly before the show. Many tours now use smartphone e-tickets tied to the buyer's name, and ID checks at the gate are common. The tour organiser lists the official sales channels on its site, and those are the only reliable source. Resale outside approved trade systems is restricted in Japan.

## Getting to Nagoya Dome-mae Yada

Most fans come by train, and three stations serve the dome:

- **Nagoya Dome-mae Yada** (Meijo subway line and the Yutorito Line guideway bus): the closest, a few minutes' walk along a raised walkway.
- **Yada** (JR Chuo Line, Meitetsu Seto Line): roughly 10–15 minutes on foot.
- **Ozone** (JR Chuo, Meijo, Meitetsu and Yutorito lines): about 15 minutes' walk, and the easiest place to spread out after the show.

From Nagoya Station, people usually take the Higashiyama Line to Sakae and change to the Meijo Line. The other common route is the JR Chuo Line straight to Ozone. Parking at the dome is limited on concert days, so few people drive.

## Lightsticks, goods lines and dome manners

Japanese concert crowds follow a clear set of habits. Many fans travel in from Osaka, Tokyo and abroad, and arrival and exit run on a routine:

- **Goods first.** Official merchandise sells from stalls outside the dome, and the lines form hours before doors open. Many fans buy online where the tour allows it and skip the queue.
- **The crown lightstick.** BIGBANG's crown-shaped official lightstick is the standard thing to hold. Homemade signs are usually kept below shoulder height so the people behind can see.
- **Phones away.** Photos and video during the show are normally banned, and staff do enforce it.
- **Stay in your space.** People stand and sing in their own spot. Pushing toward the stage or leaning over the seat in front is frowned on.
- **Regulated exit.** At the end, staff let the crowd out block by block. Waiting for your block to be called is normal, and nobody rushes it.

Coin lockers at the nearby stations fill up fast on show days. Most fans pack light. Early December evenings in Nagoya are cold, so people tend to wear layers they can tie around a waist inside the heated dome.

## Where fans sleep and eat

Most visitors stay around Nagoya Station or Sakae rather than next to the dome. Both are on direct lines and have plenty of business hotels. Sakae also has the most late-night food, which matters after a dome show finishes in the evening.

Nagoya cooking, known as Nagoya-meshi, is worth a meal on its own:

- **Tebasaki**: peppery, sweet glazed chicken wings.
- **Miso katsu**: a pork cutlet under a dark red miso sauce.
- **Hitsumabushi**: grilled eel over rice, eaten three ways.
- **Kishimen**: flat, wide wheat noodles.

Right beside the dome is a large AEON shopping mall with a food court and chain restaurants. Before doors, it fills up with fans wearing tour T-shirts.

## A free afternoon near the dome

With the show in the evening, the day is open. The Tokugawa Art Museum and the neighbouring Tokugawa Garden are in the same ward, a short ride or a longish walk away. The museum holds treasures of the Owari Tokugawa family, including scrolls from The Tale of Genji.

Nagoya Castle, with its golden shachihoko roof ornaments, is a few stops away on the Meijo Line. Osu, a covered shopping arcade, is good for secondhand clothes and street snacks. A weekend visit with both nights can fill two or three days in the city.
