## Downtown Cancún, Not the Hotel Zone

Mercado 28 belongs to the other Cancún. It sits in Supermanzana 28 of the downtown grid, among offices, pharmacies and taco stands, a world away from the beachfront towers on Boulevard Kukulcán.

The layout is a ring of open-fronted stalls built around an inner courtyard. Merchandise covers every surface: hammocks hanging from the rafters, shelves of painted ceramics, glass cases of silver. Vendors stand at the doorway of each unit and call out as you pass. It is busy, crowded and very much on the tourist circuit, so treat it as a lively shopping trip, not a quiet discovery.

## Riding In From the Beaches

Getting here from the Hotel Zone is simple and cheap, and you don't need a tour.

- **Public bus:** The R-1 and R-2 buses run the length of Boulevard Kukulcán and into downtown along Avenida Tulum. Ask the driver for "Mercado Veintiocho" and get off near Avenida Tulum, then walk a few blocks west.
- **Taxi:** Every driver knows the name. Taxis in Cancún are unmetered, so agree on the fare before you get in.
- **From the ADO bus station:** The main ADO terminal on Avenida Tulum is a walk of roughly 10 to 15 minutes. That makes the market an easy stop on the day you arrive or leave.

The address on Avenida Xel-Há is useful for a map pin. Locals mostly just say "el veintiocho".

## The Courtyard Kitchens

Go past the outer ring of stalls and you reach the core of the market. Small restaurants open onto a shared courtyard with plastic or wooden tables, laminated menus and servers who walk up waving one before you've sat down.

This is the reason many people come back. The menus lean Yucatecan and coastal, so look for:

- **Cochinita pibil:** slow-cooked achiote pork, often served in tacos or tortas with pickled red onion.
- **Panuchos and salbutes:** fried tortillas piled with shredded meat, lettuce, tomato and onion.
- **Sopa de lima:** a chicken and lime broth topped with crisp tortilla strips.
- **Ceviche and cocteles de camarón:** Quintana Roo is a seafood coast, and most kitchens here have seafood on the menu.

Ask for a michelada or an agua fresca like horchata or jamaica to go with it. Prices for each dish are on the menu, so check before you order and don't rely on what a server tells you out loud.

## What Fills the Stalls

Many stalls sell the same core stock, so browse a few before you commit. Expect:

- Silver jewellery, much of it sold as coming from Taxco
- Woven hammocks, a classic Yucatán buy
- Guayabera shirts and embroidered blouses and dresses
- Talavera-style ceramics, painted skulls and alebrijes
- Lucha libre masks and sombreros
- Bottles of vanilla, tequila and mezcal

On silver, look for a ".925" stamp, and be sceptical of anything priced like costume jewellery. Vanilla and spirits pack well, but put them in checked luggage for the flight home.

## Haggling Without the Hard Sell

Bargaining is expected here, and it goes better when you stay friendly. Treat the first price as an opening offer. Counter with a lower figure, smile, and meet in the middle. Buying several items from one stall gives you more leverage than buying one.

A few habits help:

- **Carry Mexican pesos in small notes.** Some stalls take cards or dollars, but pesos usually get a better deal and faster change.
- **A firm "no, gracias" while you keep walking** is the polite way to turn down a vendor. Stopping to explain only starts a new pitch.
- **Only make an offer if you'd actually buy.** Walking away after the seller accepts your price is considered bad manners.
- **In the courtyard restaurants,** a tip of about 10 to 15 percent is normal. Check the bill first, because some places add it already.

A few words of Spanish go a long way. "¿Cuánto cuesta?" (how much?) and "¿Me hace un descuento?" (can you give me a discount?) will usually get a smile and a better price.

## Working Around the 9-to-7 Day

The market opens from 9am to 7pm every day of the week, with the same hours on weekends. That makes it an easy daytime trip, but it's no help for an evening out. If you're coming after the beach, aim to arrive by mid-afternoon. Some stalls start closing up before the official close.

Mid-morning is a good time to come. Cruise and tour groups usually arrive later in the day, the courtyard is calmer, and you can browse before lunch, then eat when the kitchens are in full swing. Allow about two hours: one to shop, one to eat. Downtown heat builds through the day, and many of the outer aisles are open to the air.

## Around the Block

Mercado 28 works well as the anchor for a half-day downtown. A short walk away is **Parque de las Palapas**, the main square in El Centro. In the evening it fills with families and food carts selling marquesitas, the rolled crêpes of the Yucatán, which is a good reason to stay in town after the market shuts.

**Avenida Tulum**, the main downtown avenue, runs nearby with banks, ATMs and the bus stops back to the Hotel Zone. If you want to see a working market rather than a souvenir one, **Mercado 23** is a few blocks north. It's smaller and more everyday, with produce, butchers and household goods. Combine the two and you'll see both sides of downtown Cancún in one morning.
