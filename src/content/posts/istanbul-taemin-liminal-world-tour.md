## A Friday night on the Asian side

November 27, 2026 falls on a Friday. That matters in Istanbul more than in most cities.

On Friday evenings the highways out of the centre slow to a crawl. The Bosphorus bridges back up first. Ülker Sports and Event Hall sits in Ataşehir, a district of glass office towers and residential blocks on the Asian shore. Fans who know the city get there on rails and water, not by road.

The show is SHINee member Taemin's LiMiNaL World Tour. Major K-pop acts don't come to Turkey often, so this date pulls fans from all over the country and from neighbouring countries too.

## Who you're watching

Taemin is the youngest member of SHINee. The group debuted in 2008, when he was still in his mid-teens. He went solo in 2014 with "Danger" and built a reputation as one of K-pop's most exacting performers.

His solo catalogue is the reason people fly in for him. Titles fans tend to know by heart:

- "Danger", the 2014 debut
- "Move", whose understated choreography became a reference point in K-pop performance
- "Criminal", from 2020
- "Guilty", from 2023

Nobody here knows the LiMiNaL setlist, so treat that list as background reading, not a forecast. His shows are built around choreography, staging and long dance breaks. People who come for the vocals alone are often surprised by how physical the night is.

## Inside Ülker Sports and Event Hall

This is a purpose-built indoor arena. Its everyday tenant is Fenerbahçe's basketball team, which plays its home games here. The bowl is steep and enclosed, and the rows of seats climb close around the floor. Even the upper tiers keep a direct line of sight to the stage.

For concerts, the court becomes a standing or seated floor, with tiered seating rising on the sides. Basketball crowds here are famously loud. The roof holds noise in, and a K-pop crowd doing fan chants in unison fills it the same way.

There are cloakrooms and security screening at the entrances, as at any large Turkish arena. Bag checks are thorough. Fans usually travel light, and large camera gear and professional lenses are commonly refused at concerts here.

## Crossing the Bosphorus to get there

This is the part of the night first-timers underestimate. Most visitors stay on the European side, around Sultanahmet, Beyoğlu or Şişli, and the arena is not.

The two standard ways across:

- **Marmaray**: the rail line that runs in a tunnel under the Bosphorus, linking stations such as Sirkeci and Yenikapı to Üsküdar and onward along the Asian shore.
- **Ferry**: city ferries from Eminönü, Karaköy and Beşiktaş run to Kadıköy and Üsküdar. The crossing is short and it's one of the most scenic commutes anywhere.

From Kadıköy, the M4 metro line heads east through the districts around Ataşehir. Many fans ride it partway and take a taxi or bus for the final stretch. Turkish ride and taxi apps work here, but Friday-night surge traffic near the arena is the norm.

Every one of these runs on the **Istanbulkart**, the city's rechargeable transit card. You can buy and top it up at machines in stations and ferry piers. One card can be tapped for more than one person, which helps a group.

## Tickets, queues and fan etiquette

Turkish concert tickets are usually sold through the promoter's authorised ticketing partner. Fans in Turkey treat social-media resale with deep suspicion, with good reason. Screenshots of tickets get sold more than once. Names on tickets are sometimes checked against ID at the gate, so a resold ticket can be refused at the door.

On the night, a few things are customary:

- **Merch lines** form well before doors at K-pop shows here. Fans usually arrive hours ahead if they want specific items.
- **Fan projects** are common. Local fan groups hand out printed slogans or banners in the queue so the whole crowd can hold them up in the same song.
- **Fan chants** are shouted in Korean. Turkish fans learn them phonetically and they're loud about it.
- **Free sharing** is normal: small gifts, photocards and stickers traded between strangers in line.

Turkish is the working language at the venue. Security and staff often speak some English, but a simple "teşekkürler" (thank you) goes a long way. Cards are widely accepted at arena concessions and around Ataşehir. A little cash for kiosks and buses is still handy.

## Kadıköy before, Kadıköy after

Kadıköy is the natural staging post. It's the Asian side's main hub, an easy ferry ride from the old city and on the M4 line toward the arena.

The streets behind the ferry pier make up the Kadıköy market: fishmongers, pickle shops, meze counters and narrow lanes of cafés and bars. Fans often eat here before heading east. Down the hill, Moda's seafront promenade is where locals walk along the water in the evening.

After the show, the crowd usually splits. Some head back to Kadıköy for a late meal. Others go straight for the last ferries and Marmaray trains. The night ferry timetable is much thinner than the daytime one, so many fans who are staying on the European side fall back on a taxi over the bridge.

## Where visitors base themselves

Staying on the Asian side makes the night simplest. Kadıköy and Moda have plenty of mid-range hotels and apartments. They're lively after dark and only a short ride from Ataşehir.

Visitors who want the classic sights usually stay in Sultanahmet or around Karaköy and Galata. Those areas are close to the ferry piers and the Marmaray stations at Sirkeci and Yenikapı. It makes for a longer concert-night journey, but the mornings are easier for the Hagia Sophia, the Blue Mosque and the Grand Bazaar.

Late November in Istanbul is usually cool and often damp. The wind off the water on a night ferry is sharper than it feels on the street. Queuers dress in layers that can be checked into the cloakroom or tied round the waist once the arena warms up.
