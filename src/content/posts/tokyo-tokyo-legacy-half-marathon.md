## A half marathon that grew out of the Olympics

The name tells you why this race exists. The Tokyo Legacy Half Marathon was created after the Tokyo 2020 Games to keep the Olympic venues and the city's running culture in use. The first edition was held in 2022.

The race centres on the Japan National Stadium, the timber-and-steel bowl designed by Kengo Kuma. Its eaves are lined with wooden louvres made from cedar sourced from all 47 prefectures. For runners, crossing the line on a track built for an Olympics is the point of the whole day.

The 2026 edition was set for Sunday, October 18. Elite Japanese and international runners were announced alongside the mass field.

## The stadium and the streets around it

The stadium sits in Kasumigaoka, on the edge of Shinjuku and Minato wards. It backs onto the Meiji Jingu Gaien sports district. On race mornings, those wide avenues fill with runners in foil blankets and club singlets.

Several stations sit within a short walk:

- **Kokuritsu-Kyogijo Station** (Toei Oedo Line): the closest, practically at the stadium's door
- **Sendagaya Station** (JR Chuo-Sobu Line): across the road from the stadium's west side
- **Shinanomachi Station** (JR Chuo-Sobu Line): one stop east, handy for the Gaien side
- **Gaienmae Station** (Tokyo Metro Ginza Line): a walk up past the ginkgo avenue

Road closures are normal on race days. Because of that, most spectators and runners come by train, and taxis around the stadium are slow during the morning.

## Tickets, entry and where the details live

There are two kinds of "ticket" for a race like this. Runners need a race entry. Spectators along the course need nothing at all: the streets are public and anyone can stand at the barriers.

For 2026, runner entry was handled through the official Tokyo Legacy Half website, along with the course map and runner guidelines. Entry categories, eligibility rules and fees change from one edition to the next.

Seating inside the stadium for the finish was handled separately from roadside viewing.

## Lining the course on race morning

The best atmosphere is near the start and finish. Runners stream out of the stadium in a long, dense river, then return hours later to a track lap and a finish under the stands. Spectators usually split their morning between those two moments.

Mid-October in Tokyo usually means mild days and cooler mornings. That's good racing weather, and it makes standing at a barrier for an hour comfortable. In some years the ginkgo trees on the Jingu Gaien avenue are starting to turn by then. Their main gold usually arrives in late November.

Spectators who want to see the elite pack often pick a spot on a long straight away from the stadium crowds. The leaders come through fast and spread out, so a straight gives the longest look.

## Cheering, trains and race-day manners

Japanese roadside cheering has its own vocabulary. "Ganbatte!" (keep going) and the shorter "Faito!" ring out all along a route. Many spectators hold handmade signs or club banners and clap steadily rather than shouting.

What locals typically do on a race day:

- **They stay behind the barriers.** Marshals guide people across at fixed crossing points only. Ducking under the tape is a real hazard to runners.
- **They tap through with an IC card.** Suica or PASMO, on a card or phone, saves queuing at ticket machines in crowded stations like Sendagaya.
- **They carry rubbish home.** Public bins are scarce in Tokyo, and race volunteers work hard to keep the roads clean.
- **They keep the station flow moving.** Platforms near the stadium get busy at the finish. People queue in lines on the marked spots and let passengers off first.

For runners, bag drop and start corrals at Japanese races are usually organised with typical precision. Most participants follow the runner guide closely and reach their corral well within the posted window.

## Making a day of it in Jingu Gaien

The stadium neighbourhood has plenty to fill the rest of a race day. Meiji Jingu shrine and its forest are a walk or short train ride west, past Harajuku. The Shinjuku Gyoen national garden lies just north of Sendagaya Station, with wide lawns that suit tired legs.

Runners often head to Shinjuku or Aoyama afterwards for a celebratory meal. Both are a stop or two away. Sento (public bathhouses) are another post-race habit across Tokyo, and a hot soak suits the day well.
