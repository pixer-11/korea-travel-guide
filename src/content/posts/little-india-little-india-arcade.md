Walk in from Serangoon Road and the street noise drops almost immediately. The building dates to the 1920s, a row of shophouses joined under one roof, and the narrow aisles inside still feel like several separate shops pushed up against each other rather than one planned mall. That's more or less what it is: dozens of small, independently run stalls sharing corridors barely wide enough for two people to pass.

## What's actually inside

The arcade's mix leans hard into the everyday needs of the neighbourhood rather than souvenirs alone.

- Flower stalls stringing fresh jasmine and marigold garlands to order
- Sari and textile shops with bolts of cloth stacked to the ceiling
- Spice and dry goods vendors selling by the bag, not the jar
- Incense, brass lamps, bindis and other puja items
- A few stalls given over to Bollywood CDs, bangles and costume jewellery

Nothing here is curated for tourists first. You'll see aunties buying garlands for a home shrine next to a visitor picking through bangles, and the stallholders are used to both.

## The building itself

Little India Arcade occupies part of a conservation shophouse block, and it's worth slowing down to notice the architecture rather than only the goods. Five-foot ways, timber shutters and airwells between units are original to the period, even where the shopfronts have been modernised. It's a rare chance in this part of Singapore to walk through a functioning early-20th-century market building rather than a restored, roped-off version of one.

## Garland-making at the flower stalls

The flower stalls are the arcade's clearest link to daily ritual rather than retail. Garlands are strung fresh, usually on the spot or just before opening, threaded onto cotton with a needle rather than pre-packaged. If you stop to watch, you'll see how fast the stringing goes: jasmine buds first, then marigold heads packed tight behind them. These garlands are bought by locals for temple visits and home altars as much as by anyone browsing for a souvenir, so stock changes through the day as strings sell out and get restrung.

## Getting there and getting your bearings

The arcade sits at 48 Serangoon Rd, a short walk from Little India MRT station (Downtown and North East Lines); take an exit toward Serangoon Road and you'll see the shophouse row within a couple of minutes. Sri Veeramakaliamman Temple, one of the district's oldest Hindu temples, is close enough to combine with the arcade in a single loop. Tekka Centre, a wet market and hawker centre, sits at the other end of Serangoon Road if you want a food stop after browsing.

## When to go

The arcade keeps long, consistent hours: 9am to 10pm every day of the week, so there's no awkward early closing to plan around. Mornings tend to be calmer, with stalls still setting out stock and fewer people in the aisles. Given how well-known the arcade is within Little India, weekend afternoons and evenings draw noticeably more foot traffic, especially when they overlap with a festival period like Deepavali, when the whole street is strung with lights and stalls stay busier into the night.

## Bargaining and buying at the stalls

Prices at most stalls aren't fixed the way they would be in a department store, and a bit of polite bargaining, especially on textiles, jewellery or multiple items bought together, is normal and expected. Cash is still widely used here, though more stalls now take cards or mobile payment; it doesn't hurt to carry some small notes for the smaller vendors.

Stallholders are generally happy to explain what a spice or item is used for if you ask, and there's no pressure to buy just because you've stopped to look. Keep transactions friendly and unhurried: this is a market built around regular customers, not a one-shot tourist stop, and it shows in how the sellers deal with browsers.

## Making a longer visit of it

On its own, the arcade is a 30 to 45 minute stop, but it rewards being folded into a slower walk through Little India rather than visited in isolation. Combine it with the temple, a walk down Campbell Lane for cheaper trinket stalls, and a meal at one of the banana-leaf restaurants nearby on Race Course Road. Because the arcade is well-known rather than an under-the-radar find, treat the crowd as part of the experience rather than something to dodge entirely; going early or on a weekday morning simply gives you more room to look without being jostled in the narrower aisles.
