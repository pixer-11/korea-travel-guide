## A street that only exists on Sundays

From Monday to Saturday, Calle Defensa is an ordinary cobbled street of old low-rise façades. On Sunday it turns into a market roughly a kilometre long. Folding tables and canopies line both kerbs, and the traffic gives way to people walking slowly, stopping every few steps.

This is one of the most-reviewed markets in Buenos Aires, so it is not a secret. It does reward anyone willing to walk the full length. The fair has been running since 1970, when it began around Plaza Dorrego as an antiques market. Today the antiques sit beside leatherwork, mate gourds, silver jewellery, secondhand records and plenty of souvenirs.

## What fills the tables

The listed address, Defensa 320, puts you at the northern end, a few blocks from Plaza de Mayo. Stalls here lean towards crafts and clothing. The antiques get denser as you head south towards Plaza Dorrego, the old square that has always been the fair's centre.

Things worth looking for:

- **Mate gourds and bombillas**: the gourds and metal straws used for Argentina's national infusion, from plain calabash to silver-trimmed.
- **Soda siphons**: heavy coloured-glass seltzer bottles, a classic porteño collectible.
- **Filete porteño**: the curling, brightly painted lettering of old Buenos Aires signage, sold on small boards and plaques.
- **Leather goods**: belts, wallets and bags.
- **Old photographs, postcards, tango records and cutlery**, piled into trays in Plaza Dorrego.

## Plaza Dorrego and the Mercado

Plaza Dorrego is where the fair gets busiest. On Sundays the square fills with antique dealers' tables, and tango dancers and street musicians often perform for whoever stops to watch. Leave something in the hat if you stay for a song.

A couple of blocks away, the Mercado de San Telmo opened in 1897. It is a covered market under an iron-and-glass roof, with entrances on Defensa and the surrounding streets. It mixes produce stalls and antique dealers with food counters. It is the easiest place to sit down and eat without leaving the fair.

## Getting down to Defensa

The Subte (metro) gets you within walking distance. Choose your station by which end you want to start from:

- **Line A, Perú station**, or **Line D, Catedral**: both come up by Plaza de Mayo, a short walk to the northern end of Defensa.
- **Line E, Belgrano or Independencia**: closer to the middle of the fair.
- **Line C, San Juan**: puts you nearer the southern stretch and Plaza Dorrego.

Starting at Plaza de Mayo and walking south is the natural direction. It also means you can finish at Parque Lezama, or at the Museo de Arte Moderno de Buenos Aires on Avenida San Juan.

## Working Defensa on a Sunday

Arriving soon after opening usually means walking room. Vendors are still unpacking and the day-trippers haven't come yet. By the afternoon, the narrow pavements near Plaza Dorrego slow to a shuffle. Opening hours can change, so check before you go, and remember that the fair is closed on Saturday, when Defensa reverts to a normal street.

How people actually buy here:

- **Pay in pesos.** Cash is the default. Many vendors also take payment by QR code through Mercado Pago, but don't count on a card terminal.
- **Haggle gently.** A polite ask for a better price is normal, especially if you buy several things or are browsing antiques. Hard bargaining over small handicrafts is not.
- **Greet first.** A "buen día" before asking "¿cuánto sale?" (how much is it?) goes a long way.
- **Ask before photographing** a stall's goods up close, especially the antique dealers' displays.

The fair is crowded, so pickpockets work it. Keep your phone in a front pocket rather than in your hand or a back pocket. Wear your bag across your chest.

## Around the fair

Make the day longer by exploring the streets on either side of Defensa. El Zanjón de Granados, at Defensa 755, runs guided tours through restored tunnels and cisterns beneath an old house. North of the fair, Plaza de Mayo gives you the Casa Rosada and the Metropolitan Cathedral.

When the stalls pack up, the bars and parrillas of San Telmo stay open. Sunday evening is a good time for steak or a glass of Malbec within a few blocks of Plaza Dorrego.
