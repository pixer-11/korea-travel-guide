La Malmaison sits mid-Croisette, sandwiched between the seafront hotels and the beach clubs, in a building that used to be an actual hotel annex before the town took it over for art. That history shows in the bones of the place: high ceilings, tall shuttered windows facing the Mediterranean, and rooms that were clearly built for guests rather than galleries, which gives the exhibitions an oddly domestic scale.

There's no permanent collection here. La Malmaison exists to host one solo exhibition at a time, usually a 20th- or 21st-century painter, photographer, or sculptor, on rotation every few months. That means the single most useful thing to check before you go isn't the address, it's who's showing right now, since the entire visit is built around that one artist.

## The building itself is part of the exhibit

Before you even look at the art, notice the space. The rooms are modest and sequential, more like walking through an apartment than a museum, with parquet or tile floors and windows that still let in the coastal light rather than blackout gallery lighting.

This works for some shows better than others. A painter with bold, large-format canvases can feel cramped; a photographer or a sculptor working in smaller pieces tends to suit the intimacy of the old hotel rooms. Either way, expect to spend closer to 45 minutes here than a full afternoon.

## Opening hours and the midday gap

La Malmaison keeps a split schedule every day it's open: 10am to 1pm, then 2pm to 6pm, Tuesday through Sunday. It's closed all day Monday.

The one-hour midday closure catches people off guard, especially visitors used to French museums staying open through lunch. If you arrive at 1:15pm expecting to walk straight in, you'll be standing on the Croisette instead.

- Open: Tuesday–Sunday, 10am–1pm and 2pm–6pm
- Closed: all day Monday, and closed again between 1pm and 2pm on open days
- Best plan: arrive by 10am or come after 2pm, not in between

Hours for municipal galleries in France do shift around exhibition changeovers and public holidays, so it's worth a quick check of the current show and hours before you walk over, particularly if you're timing it around a Cannes Film Festival week when the whole boulevard runs on a different rhythm.

## Finding it on La Croisette

The address is 47 Boulevard de la Croisette, which puts it roughly across from the beach, a short walk east of the Palais des Festivals where the film festival red carpet runs each May. There's no metro in Cannes; you're walking, cycling, or arriving by bus along the seafront.

If you're coming from the train station (Cannes-Ville), it's a flat 20 to 25 minute walk down through the town center toward the water, or a short taxi ride if the midday heat has you avoiding it. Parking directly on La Croisette is scarce and metered; the underground Palais des Festivals car park is the more reliable option nearby.

Look for a pale stone facade set slightly back from the palm-lined promenade, easy to miss if you're focused on the beach clubs and hotel entrances either side of it.

## A gallery most people on the Croisette walk straight past

La Malmaison doesn't compete for attention with the Palais des Festivals or the big beachfront hotels, and that's exactly why it's worth the short detour. It draws a fraction of the foot traffic of the boulevard outside, so even during the busier tourist months you're rarely dealing with a crowd inside.

That under-the-radar status is likely to change if a bigger-name retrospective comes through, so treat a quiet visit as a feature of right now rather than a permanent guarantee. Locals don't necessarily flag it as their own spot, but for travelers it functions as a genuine break from the density of the seafront.

## Buying in, moving through

There's no ticket booth drama here; entry is typically a single admission fee at the door, sometimes with a reduced rate for students or seniors, though exact pricing shifts with each new exhibition and is best confirmed on the day. Cards are accepted alongside cash at most Cannes municipal sites, so you're not likely to be caught out either way.

Exhibitions are usually laid out room by room in a suggested order, but because the space is small there's no real way to get lost. Most visitors move through in one loop, then double back to a piece or two that stopped them the first time. Wall text is generally in French and English, which helps if the show leans conceptual.

## Pairing it with the rest of the boulevard

Because the visit itself is short, La Malmaison works best folded into a longer Croisette walk rather than as a single destination. Combine it with the Palais des Festivals nearby, the beach a few steps across the road, or one of the small streets running back into the old town (Le Suquet) for lunch afterward.

If you're a completist about French Riviera art spaces, pair it in the same day with the Musée de la Castre up in Le Suquet, which gives you the contrast of a permanent historical collection against La Malmaison's rotating contemporary shows.
