## Why this promenade matters

You come around the base of Monte Urgull and the whole bay opens up at once — a nearly two-kilometer curve of white balustrade, cream-colored belle-époque buildings, and sand that stretches from the Old Town all the way to Ondarreta. This is Paseo de La Concha, and it's the reason San Sebastián's shape gets compared to a shell (a "concha"): the bay is bitten into a near-perfect arc, with the small green hump of Isla de Santa Clara sitting in the middle like a stopper.

The wrought-iron streetlamps and the double-globe railings along the barandilla have become the city's unofficial emblem, showing up on postcards and tile work across town. Rated 4.8 by nearly 3,000 visitors, it's not a hidden find — it's one of the most walked stretches of coastline in northern Spain, and it earns that reputation just by existing.

## What you'll actually see and do

Walking east to west, you start near the Old Town and Alderdi Eder gardens, pass the grand facade of the Hotel María Cristina's neighborhood, and continue along a wide tiled walkway separating the road from the beach. Beach clubs (balnearios) built into the retaining wall once let Belle Époque bathers change without crossing the promenade; some of those arched structures still stand as cafés and lifeguard posts.

Midway you'll hit the ornate Puente María Cristina area and views straight across to Santa Clara island — rent a small boat in summer or just watch the ferries that occasionally run out to it. Keep going and the promenade narrows toward Ondarreta beach, backed by pine-covered slopes and the Peine del Viento (Wind Comb) sculptures by Eduardo Chillida further along the rocks — worth the extra 15 minutes if you have them.

Along the whole route, function benches, a dedicated cycling/skating lane, and low walls perfect for sitting with an ice cream make it as much a local artery as a sight.

## Getting there

The promenade runs along the city's edge, so there's no single entrance — you join it wherever suits your route. From the Old Town or Boulevard, it's a 5-10 minute walk down toward Alderdi Eder and the town hall gardens. From the bus station or Renfe/Amara train station, it's roughly a 20-minute walk or a short taxi ride. If you're staying near Ondarreta or Antiguo, you'll likely start from the western end near the funicular up Monte Igueldo instead. There's no admission and no gate — it's a public seafront path open 24 hours.

## Best time to walk it

Early morning (before 9am) gives you flat light on the water and a promenade shared mainly with joggers and swimmers doing laps in the bay. Sunset is the other prime window, when the sky over the bay's mouth turns orange and the streetlamps start to glow against the darkening water — this is when the crowd thickens, especially in summer.

Midday in July and August brings the heaviest tourist and beach-day traffic, with the walkway packed shoulder to shoulder between the Old Town and the first beach access points. Outside summer, the promenade is genuinely pleasant even in light rain — the wide balustrade and covered arcades near the old bathhouses give some shelter.

## How to visit like a local

Because this is a famous, well-visited stretch rather than a secret spot, the smartest move is timing, not discovery. Walk it early morning or after 8pm in summer to avoid the thickest tour-group traffic — locals doing their daily paseo or swim favor those hours too. There's nothing to pay and nothing to book: you just walk on. If you want to swim, look for the changing rooms and showers built into the old balneario structures rather than paying for a private beach club.

Cyclists and skaters use a marked lane on the sea side, so keep to the outer path if you're on foot with kids. The biggest mistake visitors make is treating La Concha as a quick photo stop from one bench — the real reward is walking the full arc, from the harbor end to Ondarreta, so you feel the curve of the bay rather than just seeing a slice of it.
