## Two days in December on the Pacific

This is a road race with an ocean beside it. Gran Maratón Pacífico Mazatlán is held over December 5-6, 2026, in Mazatlán, Sinaloa. It is one of Mexico's established marathons, and runners come for one reason above the others: flat kilometres beside the open Pacific.

December is dry season on this coast. Mornings are mild, the sun comes up over the Sierra Madre behind the city, and the sea sits to the west of almost everything. For a runner, that means the heat arrives later in the day. For a spectator, it makes a weekend of standing outside in a T-shirt.

## Running the seawall

The Malecón shapes the whole race. Mazatlán's seafront promenade is among the longest in the Americas, a continuous ribbon of concrete railing, wide pavement and bronze monuments stretching from the old town north toward the hotel strip. The course is famous for following long stretches of it.

On the water side you get surf breaking on rocks and sand. On the land side you pass apartment towers, seafood stands and the open-air taxis parked at the kerb. Landmarks along the route include:

- **Monumento a la Mujer Mazatleca** and **Monumento al Pescador**, two of the bronze statues spaced along the promenade
- **Olas Altas**, the old-town seafront bay with its 19th-century hotel fronts
- **Glorieta Sánchez Taboada**, the rocky point where cliff divers perform
- **Cerro del Crestón**, the hill crowned by El Faro lighthouse at the southern tip of the city

Long seafront roads like this are flat and exposed. Runners usually talk about two things afterwards: the gentle grade, and the sea breeze that helps in one direction and pushes back in the other.

## Dates, distances and registration

The race weekend runs from December 5 to December 6, 2026. The organiser publishes the dates, the race-day schedule, the course map and the registration links on its official website, and that is where the full list of distances lives.

The event is built around the full 42.195 km marathon, with shorter distances alongside it. A registration is the race's version of a ticket: it gets you a bib, a timing chip and a place in a start corral. Packet pickup and the event expo are part of the weekend programme, and the organiser's site lists where they are held.

Watching costs nothing. The Malecón is a public promenade, and spectators simply pick a stretch of railing.

## Getting into the city and across it

Most visiting runners fly into General Rafael Buelna International Airport (MZT), south of the city. Others arrive by long-distance bus at the central bus station, a short ride from the seafront. The coastal highway connects Mazatlán with Culiacán to the north and Tepic and Guadalajara to the south.

Race mornings close sections of the Malecón to traffic, so moving north to south by car gets slow. Visitors usually lean on these instead:

- **Pulmonías**: open-sided, golf-cart-like taxis found only in Mazatlán
- **Aurigas**: pickup trucks with benches in the bed, used as shared taxis
- **Walking**: the old town and Olas Altas are compact enough to cover on foot

## Pulmonías, "¡échale ganas!" and race-morning manners

Most of what you need here is a few habits.

- **Settle the fare first.** Pulmonía and auriga drivers don't run meters, so riders usually agree on the price before climbing in. They pay in pesos cash.
- **Cheer in Spanish.** "¡Échale ganas!" (give it everything) and "¡Vamos, vamos!" are what the crowd shouts. Calling the runner's name from their bib gets a grin every time.
- **Keep the runners' lane clear.** The seawall pavement is wide, but cones and tape mark the course. Spectators stay on the railing side.
- **Tip at the table.** In sit-down restaurants a tip of 10–15% is normal. Waiters bring the card machine to you.
- **Carry small notes.** Street stalls selling coconut water, fruit cups and tamales along the seafront are cash businesses.

Runners also tend to eat light and early the night before. A seafood marathon of a dinner is something people save for after the race.

## Where people stay and what they eat

The city splits into two clear bases. **Centro Histórico** and **Olas Altas** put you in the old town, with restored colonial buildings, Plaza Machado and the Teatro Ángela Peralta a few blocks inland. **Zona Dorada**, the Golden Zone, is the hotel and resort strip further north, closer to the beach and the newer restaurants.

Mazatlán is a shrimp port, and the menus show it. Post-race favourites include:

- **Aguachile**: raw shrimp cured in lime and chile, served with cucumber and red onion
- **Pescado zarandeado**: a whole fish split open and grilled over coals
- **Ceviche and tostadas** from the seafront carts
- **Pacífico beer**, which is brewed in Mazatlán

## After the finish line

Plenty of runners stay an extra day, because the city rewards slow legs. The **Catedral Basílica de la Inmaculada Concepción**, with its twin yellow towers, anchors the old town. Plaza Machado's café tables sit under trees a short walk away.

If your legs can handle stairs, the climb to **El Faro** on Cerro del Crestón gives a view over the whole coastline you just ran. It is a steep path rather than a stroll. Lower-effort options are boat trips out to the islands just offshore, or simply sitting at Olas Altas at sunset. Two or three days covers the race, recovery and the old town without rushing.
