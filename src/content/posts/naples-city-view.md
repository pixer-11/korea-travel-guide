## Why this view, and not the famous one

Every guidebook sends you to the terrace of Castel Sant'Elmo or the belvedere at San Martino for the classic Naples panorama, and both are worth the ticket. But a five-minute walk further along Via Tito Angelini, past the last souvenir cart and the last tour group, the street simply opens up on one side and Naples spreads out below you again — this time with no barrier, no entrance fee, and almost nobody else standing there.

Locals call this stretch of road, cut into the flank of the Vomero hill, one of the few remaining spots where the city drops away in a single uninterrupted sweep: terracotta rooftops, the grey dome of Vesuvius on the horizon, and the bay doing that flat, hazy, silver-blue thing it does in the afternoon.

## Getting there

The easiest route is the Montesanto funicular from the lower city up to Morgen station, followed by a ten-minute walk uphill along Via Morghen and onto Via Tito Angelini — the same road that leads to Castel Sant'Elmo's main gate. Alternatively, the Centrale funicular from Via Toledo drops you closer to Piazza Fuga, from which it's a similar walk through Vomero's quieter residential streets.

If you're already visiting the Castel or the Certosa di San Martino, you're a few minutes' walk from this viewpoint and it makes sense to fold it into the same outing rather than treat it as a separate trip. There's no dedicated bus stop right at the spot, so plan on arriving on foot regardless of which funicular you take.

## What you'll actually see

Unlike the paid terraces nearby, there's no railing installation, no gift shop, no plaque — just a natural pull-off in the road where the hillside drops steeply and the view is uninterrupted. On a clear day you can trace the curve of the bay from the port up past Mergellina toward Posillipo, with Capri sometimes visible as a pale shape on the horizon.

It's a scenic spot in the truest sense: nothing to buy, nothing to queue for, just the city laid out below you. Bring your own water and don't expect benches — this is a stop, not a park.

## When to go

Given how few reviews this place has relative to its rating, it's clearly not on the main tourist circuit yet, and that's exactly its appeal: you get the same rooftops-and-bay panorama tourists queue for at Castel Sant'Elmo, but without the ticket line. Late afternoon, an hour or two before sunset, is the sweet spot — the sun moves west over the bay and the light turns the rooftops amber-gold, while the haze that often sits over Naples at midday tends to clear.

Mornings can be hazier and the light flatter for photos. Avoid midday in summer, less because of crowds and more because there's no shade along this stretch of road.

## Pair it with Vomero

Because it's tucked into the upper reaches of Vomero, you're a short walk from Piazza Vanvitelli's cafés, Via Scarlatti's shopping street, and the quieter, more residential feel of Naples' hill district — a useful contrast after the density of the historic centre below. The Certosa di San Martino, with its Baroque church and museum of Neapolitan life, and Castel Sant'Elmo's star-shaped fortress are both close enough to combine in a single half-day loop that ends here.

## How to visit like a local

There's no ticket, no gate, and no set hours — it's a public roadside viewpoint, so you simply walk up and look. Because it sees so little foot traffic compared to the paid terraces nearby, there's no queue etiquette to worry about: you won't be jostling for space at a railing. The mistake visitors make is treating Vomero as a box to tick between funicular rides — locals treat it as a neighborhood to slow down in, stopping for a coffee standing at a bar counter (a euro or two, paid at the register first) before or after the walk.

Wear shoes that can handle uneven pavement and a real gradient; this is a hill neighborhood, not a flat old-town stroll. If you want the photo without a single stranger in frame, this is the spot the crowds haven't found yet — go before that changes.
