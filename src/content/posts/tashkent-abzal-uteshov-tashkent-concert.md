For Uzbek audiences, a Kazakh artist doesn't sound foreign. Both languages are Turkic, and the two countries share a long border and a lot of the same music on the radio. You can expect a room where plenty of people know the words and sing them back.

## Inside the Turkistan Concert Hall

Locally the venue is called **Turkiston saroyi**, the Turkiston Palace. It is one of Tashkent's main stages for pop concerts, touring artists from across the former Soviet space, and state cultural evenings.

This is a seated hall. The stalls rise towards the stage, a balcony sits above them, and rows are numbered. Because of that layout, where you sit matters more than how fast you get in. Your ticket shows a row and a seat, and ushers at the doors point people to them.

## Concert-night customs in Tashkent

Concert etiquette here has its own habits, and knowing them helps you fit in.

- **Flowers for the singer.** Across Central Asia, fans often walk up to the stage between songs to hand over bouquets. It's normal, and security generally allows it. Flower stalls near big venues do good business on concert nights.
- **Dress.** Audiences tend to dress up more than at a Western pop gig. Smart-casual is the norm, and many people treat the night as an occasion.
- **Phones.** Filming a favourite song is common. Holding a phone up for an entire set blocks the seats behind you, and people do notice.
- **Paying.** Uzbek som is the currency. Local cards (Uzcard, Humo) and cash cover most small purchases. Visa and Mastercard work at hotels and larger venues but not at every kiosk, so visitors often carry some som for snacks and flowers.
- **Language.** You'll hear Uzbek and Russian in the foyer, and staff at central venues usually speak Russian. "Rahmat" (thank you, in Uzbek) goes a long way.

## Late November around the show

Tashkent at the end of November is cold. Daytime is crisp and often bright, and the evening temperature can fall towards freezing. Concertgoers usually bring a proper coat. The cloakroom (garderob) is a standard part of any Tashkent hall, and checking your coat before you sit down is what most people do.

That weather suits daytime sightseeing. **Chorsu Bazaar**, under its large green dome, sells dried fruit, nuts and fresh non bread. The old-city quarter around **Hazrati Imam** has the Muyi Muborak library and the restored madrasas. Visitors who come for the concert often add a day or two in Tashkent. Some take the fast Afrosiyob train to Samarkand, about two hours away.

If you eat before the show, plov is the classic Tashkent meal, and it is traditionally a lunchtime dish. Many plov centres sell out by early afternoon, so concertgoers usually plan their plov for midday and eat something lighter, like shashlik or lagman, in the evening.
