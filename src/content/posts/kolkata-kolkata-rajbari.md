## A palace name on a south Kolkata side street

*Rajbari* is Bengali for a royal or zamindari mansion. Across Bengal, the word is shorthand for big households and long, unhurried meals. This Kolkata Rajbari sits at 56 Jatin Das Road, in the Lake Terrace pocket of Kalighat. It's a residential stretch of south Kolkata, a short walk from the lakes.

Google sums it up as a large, easygoing eatery serving curries, rice and other traditional food. That's the right way to think of it. You come here to sit down for a proper Bengali meal, not to grab a quick snack.

It is also one of the most-reviewed restaurants in the city, and the reviews are consistently strong. Expect company.

## Reading a Bengali menu

The order matters. Even if you only order a few plates, it helps to know the shape of a traditional Bengali meal. It runs in a set sequence, and a good menu usually follows it:

- **Something bitter first.** Shukto, a mild mixed-vegetable dish, is the classic opener.
- **Dal with rice.** Often served with a fried vegetable on the side.
- **Vegetable dishes.** Dishes like aloo posto, potato in poppy-seed paste.
- **Fish.** The centre of the meal in Bengal. Look for hilsa (ilish), prawns in coconut (chingri malai curry) or rohu in mustard.
- **Meat.** Kosha mangsho, a slow-cooked dry mutton curry, is the dish most visitors ask about.
- **Chutney, then sweets.** Mishti doi (sweet set yoghurt) or rosogolla close things out.

Check the current menu for what's actually on offer. It's also where prices are listed, and the overall level is mid-range. If a dish looks seasonal, especially hilsa, ask whether it's available that day.

## Eating the Kolkata way

Bengalis eat rice meals with the right hand, and nobody will blink if you do too. Mix a little rice with dal or gravy using your fingertips. Fish bones come out easiest by hand, too. Cutlery is always fine to ask for.

The usual local habit is to order dishes for the table and share them. Rice and a couple of curries go further than you'd think. Order in rounds instead of all at once, especially with a group.

On paying, UPI QR codes and cards are standard at mid-range Kolkata restaurants. Still, ask before you sit down if you're relying on a foreign card. Look over the bill for a service charge before you add a tip. If there isn't one, leaving a modest tip for good service is normal.

## Timing your table

At the moment the doors open at 12pm and close at 11pm, seven days a week. Hours can change, so check Google or call ahead before a special trip.

Because it's so popular, a wait at peak meal times is likely. Turning up right at noon opening usually beats the main lunch crowd. Weekend evenings are the obvious time families and groups go out, so a later weekday dinner is the safer bet. If you're a large party, ask whether they take bookings.

Give yourself a good hour to an hour and a half. A Bengali meal eaten in order isn't built for rushing.

## Getting to Jatin Das Road

The nearest metro is **Jatin Das Park** on Line 1, the Blue Line. From there it's a short walk west into the Lake Terrace lanes. **Kalighat** station on the same line is also close.

Cabs and app taxis know Jatin Das Road. For the driver, give the full address: 56, Jatin Das Rd, Lake Terrace, Kalighat. The lanes around here are narrow, so let the cab drop you at the door instead of hunting for parking.

## Before and after the meal

Lunch here fits well into a south Kolkata day. Pair it with any of these:

- **Kalighat Kali Temple**, one of the city's most important Hindu shrines, about a kilometre south-west. Visit before lunch, since mornings are when devotees come for darshan.
- **Rabindra Sarobar**, the large lake to the south. Lots of people walk its paths, and it's a good way to work off the mutton.
- **Lake Market**, nearby on Rash Behari Avenue, for fish, flowers and everyday Kolkata shopping.

If you finish late, Southern Avenue and the lake roads are the easiest place to pick up a taxi home.
