## Shells over the harbour

Kobe faces the sea, and this festival uses all of it. Shells go up over the harbour basin, so they reflect in the water, and the city's mountains catch the echo. The red lattice of Kobe Port Tower and the white frame of the Kobe Maritime Museum sit between you and the bursts.

It is one of the biggest fireworks displays in Kansai. For 2026 the festival runs over several October evenings, from the 19th to the 23rd, rather than a single summer night. Watching from the waterfront is free. That multi-night format matters more than it sounds, because the crowd is spread across five evenings instead of piling into one.

## Dates, tickets and what's confirmed

The announced dates are **October 19-23, 2026**. Free viewing from the waterfront is part of the festival's identity. Any reserved seating or paid viewing zones are listed by the organisers, so check there rather than relying on a resale site.

Confirm the timing, the nightly schedule and the ticket details on the official site before you travel. This guide does not list a start time or programme, because none was given to us and fireworks schedules shift.

Late October in Kobe means sunset falls around 5:30pm, so the show happens in proper darkness. Evenings by the water turn cool. Pack a light jacket even if the afternoon felt warm.

## Getting to the waterfront

Kobe's harbour is a short, flat walk from three rail hubs. Pick the one that matches your viewing spot:

- **Motomachi Station** (JR Kobe Line, Hanshin Main Line): the most direct walk down to Meriken Park and Port Tower, about ten minutes.
- **Sannomiya** (JR, Hankyu, Hanshin, subway, Port Liner): the main hub, with a 15-minute walk south to the harbour.
- **Harborland Station** and **Minato Motomachi Station** (Kobe Municipal Subway Kaigan Line): closest to the Harborland side and the umie mall.
- **JR Kobe Station**: an underground passage leads straight into Harborland.

From Osaka Station, a JR Special Rapid reaches Sannomiya in about 20 minutes. From Kobe Airport, the Port Liner runs to Sannomiya.

## Choosing your stretch of quay

The central waterfront is the heart of the viewing. That means the Meriken Park lawns around Port Tower and the boardwalks of Harborland across the water. Both face open harbour, so sightlines are wide.

Meriken Park has more open ground, which suits groups who want to sit. Harborland puts cafés, restaurants and toilets in umie and Mosaic right behind you, which helps on a cold night. Its main downside is the narrow walkways that clog when everyone leaves at once.

Confirm the designated viewing areas and any closures on the official site. Organisers often rope off parts of the waterfront for safety and launch operations.

## Watching the Kobe way

Japanese fireworks crowds run on a few habits. Knowing them makes the evening easier.

- **Bring a leisure sheet.** The thin plastic picnic tarp sold at any convenience store or 100-yen shop is what everyone sits on. Keep yours compact; sprawling over prime ground draws looks.
- **Top up your IC card first.** Load ICOCA or Suica before the show. After the finale, ticket machines get long queues and station staff often run one-way flows at the gates.
- **Take your rubbish with you.** Public bins are scarce in Japan, and on festival nights they fill fast. Carry a bag and bring the rubbish home or to your hotel.
- **Follow the staff.** Uniformed guides with light batons direct the crowd after the show. Walking the route they point to is faster than fighting it.
- **Eat before or after, not during.** Restaurants near the harbour fill at dinner time. A konbini picnic bought near the station saves you the wait.

Phones up for the first burst is normal. Talking loudly through the finale is not.

## Making a night of it

Five evenings means you can choose your night. If the forecast turns wet, you can switch to a different one. That flexibility is the festival's biggest practical advantage for visitors.

Come early and walk the waterfront in daylight. Kobe Port Tower, the Maritime Museum and the Earthquake Memorial Park are all in Meriken Park. The memorial keeps a section of quay left broken since the 1995 Great Hanshin earthquake. Nankinmachi, Kobe's Chinatown, is a few minutes north of Motomachi, and its takeaway stalls are an easy dinner.

After the finale, let the first wave of people drain away. Twenty minutes over a drink in Harborland usually beats queuing on a packed platform.
