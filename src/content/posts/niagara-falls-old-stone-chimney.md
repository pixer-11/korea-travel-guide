## A chimney with no house around it

There is no building here, only the chimney. It's a column of rough stone with a fireplace opening at its base, standing on its own in the open air on the New York side of the Niagara River. It has no roof, no walls and no ticket booth. You'll find a historical marker and a few minutes of quiet that the crowds at the falls never get.

What you're looking at is generally described as the oldest standing structure in western New York. French builders put it up around 1750, when this stretch of riverbank was the portage route that carried goods around the falls. Most people who come to see Horseshoe Falls never hear of it, which is why it stays so quiet.

## What it survived

The stone has outlived several empires. The French tied it to their posts guarding the portage. They burned those posts in 1759, when the British took the region during the French and Indian War. The chimney stood through the fire.

The British then built a new house around it. That house is linked to John Stedman, who ran the portage under British rule. Later the house went, and the chimney stayed. In the twentieth century it was moved twice to make room for the riverside industry that grew up on cheap hydro power. So the spot you stand on is not where it was first built, but the stonework itself is original.

## Up close

Give it a slow walk around. Things to look for:

- **The hearth opening** at the base. This was the working fireplace of the eighteenth-century building.
- **The masonry**: irregular stones set by hand. You can see where mortar fills the gaps between them.
- **The marker**, which sets out the French and British history in a few lines.
- **The scale**. It's taller than you'd guess from photos, and it shows how big the house that once wrapped around it must have been.

It takes 10–20 minutes, unless you're the kind of visitor who reads every word and photographs every course of stone.

## Crossing over from the Canadian side

Most visitors to Niagara Falls, Canada, forget one thing: this is in the United States. From the Clifton Hill and Fallsview area, the Rainbow Bridge is the direct crossing. You can drive it or walk it, and you clear US border inspection on the far end.

You'll need:

- A valid passport, or another document accepted for entry to the US.
- Any visa or travel authorisation your nationality requires. Check this before you leave your hotel.
- Patience at the bridge on summer weekends, when queues at the border build.

Once you're across, the chimney is a short drive east of downtown, out toward the industrial strip along the upper river. Put "Old Stone Chimney, Niagara Falls NY" into your map app rather than relying on street signs. Walking there from the bridge works, but it isn't a pleasant stroll.

## Seeing it without making a special trip

Treat the chimney as a stop on a wider day, not a destination by itself. Google lists it as open 24 hours, and that makes sense for an open-air monument. Still, come in daylight. The marker is hard to read in the dark, and this isn't a part of town to wander at night.

Locals don't hold special rituals here, and there's no visitor desk. A few good habits:

- **Don't climb on it.** The stonework is close to three centuries old, and the hearth isn't a seat.
- **Read the marker first**, then walk around. The details make more sense once you know the story.
- **Keep the ID you crossed with on you.** You'll show it again going back into Canada.
- **Go before or after the falls**, not at midday when you'd lose your spot in the crowds on Goat Island.

## Building a day around it

The chimney fits neatly into an American-side loop. **Niagara Falls State Park** is minutes away. Prospect Point puts you right beside the American Falls, and Goat Island gives you the edge of Horseshoe Falls from the US side.

If the chimney gets you interested in the portage-era history, drive about half an hour north along the river to **Old Fort Niagara** in Youngstown. That's where the French and British fought over the river mouth. Its stone "French Castle" dates from the same era. Seeing both on one day puts the chimney in context, a small survivor from the same contest over who controlled the Niagara.
