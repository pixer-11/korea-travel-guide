## A Monday night in Ate

Lima puts its biggest shows on the city's eastern edge. Martin Garrix plays Arena Monumental on December 7, 2026. It is a Monday, at the start of Lima's summer, and the night before a national holiday.

The venue sits in Ate, a long way from the clifftop districts where most visitors sleep. That distance shapes the whole evening: when people leave, how they get home, and what they eat on the way. The sections below follow the night in roughly that order.

## The Dutchman behind the decks

Garrix was a teenager from Amstelveen, near Amsterdam, when "Animals" broke out in 2013. Its stripped-back drop turned into a festival anthem around the world. He has topped the DJ Mag Top 100 poll several times since then.

His sets usually move between two sides of his catalogue:

- The big-room tracks that built his name, "Animals" among them
- Vocal crossovers such as "Scared to Be Lonely" with Dua Lipa and "In the Name of Love" with Bebe Rexha
- Records from STMPD RCRDS, the label he founded in 2016

Nothing here should be read as this night's setlist, which nobody has seen. It is simply the range his headline shows tend to cover, and it explains the crowd. You get club regulars and fans who know the radio hits standing side by side.

## Arena Monumental, the ground beside the stadium

Arena Monumental is the open-air concert ground next to Estadio Monumental. That is the large stadium in Ate that is home to Universitario de Deportes, one of Peru's best-supported football clubs. Its stands are visible from the concert area, and the complex fronts onto Avenida Javier Prado Este.

The space is a flat outdoor ground rather than a seated bowl. For a DJ headliner that means a standing crowd facing one main stage, with the lighting rig carrying much of the show. Lima rarely gets real rain, and December evenings are mild, so outdoor shows here are the norm.

## The 8 December effect

The calendar works in this show's favour. December 8 is the Feast of the Immaculate Conception, a public holiday across Peru. For many Limeños that makes a Monday-night concert feel like a Saturday.

That has practical effects. Fewer people in the crowd are watching the clock for a Tuesday-morning start, so the venue tends to empty more slowly. Restaurants and bars in Barranco and Miraflores often stay busy late on the eve of a holiday. Some banks and offices close on the 8th itself, which matters if you have errands planned.

## Crossing Lima to get there

Getting to Ate is the hard part of the night. Javier Prado is the main east–west artery from San Isidro towards Ate, and on weekdays it is among the most congested roads in the city. A Monday evening falls right in the commute.

Most visitors travel one of these ways:

- **Ride-hail apps** such as Uber, Cabify and InDrive, which are widely used across Lima
- **Registered taxis** booked through a hotel, which many foreign visitors prefer at night
- **Group transfers** arranged by hostels and some tour operators for large concerts

Fans coming from Miraflores or Barranco often allow well over an hour, because the drive is long and the traffic unpredictable. After the show, prices on the apps usually jump. Many attendees walk a few blocks away from the gates before requesting a car, where drivers can actually reach them.

## Tickets, gates and how a Lima concert crowd works

Tickets are sold through the promoter's official ticketing partner, which is also where ticket categories and conditions are set out. Outside big Lima venues, *revendedores* (touts) work the queues. Street resale is where most fake tickets come from, so local fans steer well clear of it.

A few habits are worth copying:

- **ID in your pocket.** Peruvians carry their DNI. Foreign visitors usually bring a passport or a clear copy, because ticket checks can involve ID.
- **Payment on site.** Card works at most official bars. Locals also pay with Yape and Plin, Peru's mobile wallets, which need a Peruvian bank account. Small soles notes help with street vendors.
- **Phone discipline.** Pickpocketing at crowded exits is a known problem at big Lima events. Regulars keep phones zipped away while they move through the gates.
- **The queue itself.** Lines form along the stadium perimeter, and security checks bags at the entrance. People travel light, because large bags slow everything down.

Outside, vendors usually sell grilled *anticuchos* and *salchipapas* (fries topped with sliced hot dog) to the departing crowd. A paper tray of salchipapas is a very Lima way to wait out the ride-hail surge.

## Where to base yourself, and what to do with the holiday

Most international visitors stay in Miraflores or Barranco. Both sit on the cliffs above the Pacific and have the city's densest choice of hotels and restaurants. San Isidro is a little closer to Ate along Javier Prado and is quieter after dark.

A free holiday on the 8th makes a slow recovery day easy. Along the Miraflores malecón, the clifftop path runs past Parque del Amor towards Larcomar. In Barranco, the Puente de los Suspiros and the streets around it are made for walking. Long, late lunches of ceviche are a Lima habit, and the cevicherías in both districts are full of families doing exactly that on a public holiday.
