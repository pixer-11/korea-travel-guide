Hac Sa means "black sand" in Cantonese (黑沙). The name is the whole pitch. On the far side of Coloane, past the casinos and the Cotai skyline, a long curve of darker sand meets the South China Sea. On a calm day you can hear the water from the bus stop.

## Black sand, gold sand and brown water

The beach is a mix of dark grey and golden grains. The original dark sand was famous, but it has thinned over the decades, and imported lighter sand now sits alongside it. Expect a two-tone shoreline, not a volcanic black beach.

The water deserves an honest word. This is the edge of the Pearl River Delta, and silt carried downriver turns the sea a murky brown-green. It is not dirty in the litter sense; it just isn't Thai-island turquoise. Come for the space, the pine-lined backdrop and the slow pace, and you won't be disappointed.

What you'll find along the shore:

- A wide, shallow-sloping stretch of sand, good for walking end to end
- Casuarina and pine trees shading the back of the beach
- Public barbecue areas and a campsite behind the sand
- Two parks close by, including the Hac Sa Reservoir Country Park trails inland

## The bus ride out to Coloane

Most visitors arrive by public bus, and the route is part of the appeal. Several lines terminate at or pass the Hac Sa stop, including the 21A, 25 and 26A from the Macau peninsula and the 15 from Taipa. Routes change, so confirm on the Macau Transport Bureau (DSAT) site before you set off.

The ride runs south over the bridges, through Cotai and into Coloane's greener hills. Allow roughly 45 minutes to an hour from central Macau depending on traffic. A taxi is quicker, though finding one for the return leg can take patience late in the day.

## Fires, pits and a Portuguese lunch

Food is half the reason people make the trip. Behind the sand, public barbecue pits let groups grill skewers, chicken wings and sausages in the open air. The campsite and picnic tables sit close by, so families tend to settle in for the afternoon.

If you would rather be cooked for, Fernando's sits at the back of the beach. It is a long-running Portuguese restaurant and has been a Hac Sa fixture for decades. It is popular, so expect a wait at weekends.

Coloane Village is a short bus ride back west. Its tiled lanes, the yellow Chapel of St. Francis Xavier and Lord Stow's Bakery, known for its Portuguese egg tarts, make an easy pairing with the beach.

## Claiming your patch of sand

A few habits make the day smoother here.

- **Bring your own barbecue supplies.** The pits are public, but fuel and food are on you. Supermarkets in Macau stock ready-made skewers and charcoal; buy before you board the bus.
- **Arrive early on public holidays.** Pits and shady spots under the trees go first, and turning up mid-morning usually beats the lunchtime groups.
- **Swim only between the flags.** Lifeguards cover the beach during the summer swimming season, and the flag colours tell you whether the water is open.
- **Carry an Octopus card or exact change.** Buses do not give change, and the Octopus card used in Hong Kong works on Macau buses too.
- **Pack your rubbish out.** Bins are provided, and leaving grill debris on the sand is frowned upon.

Dress is casual, but walking into Coloane Village straight from the beach in swimwear will draw looks. Throw on a shirt.

## When the beach is at its best

Summer, roughly May to October, is swimming season and the most lively time. It is also hot, humid and the peak of typhoon risk. Watch the Macau weather bureau for typhoon signals; the beach closes to swimmers when they go up.

Autumn and spring bring cooler, clearer days that suit a walk along the sand or a hike in the nearby country park. Winter is quiet and grey but pleasant for an hour's stroll and a long lunch.

## How long to spend

A half day is about right. Give it two or three hours if you are walking the beach and eating nearby. Plan a full afternoon if you are grilling, which tends to stretch into sunset. Combine it with Coloane Village and you have an easy, unhurried day away from the casinos.
