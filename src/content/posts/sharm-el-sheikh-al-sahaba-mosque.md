## Stone domes above the Old Market

Look up from the Old Market lanes and the minarets are already there. Al-Sahaba Mosque is built in pale, sand-coloured stone that turns honey-gold in late sun. Its design borrows from the Ottoman and Mamluk mosques of Cairo: a cluster of domes, slender minarets and carved arches along the façade.

It is one of the best-known sights in Sharm El Sheikh, and also one of the most reviewed. Visitors keep praising it, mostly for the craftsmanship. It is no secret, so plan around the crowds and don't expect to have it to yourself.

The name honours the Sahaba, the companions of the Prophet Muhammad. That tells you it is a working house of worship first and a photo stop second.

## What you find inside the gates

The courtyard opens out in front of the prayer hall, with polished stone underfoot and arcades along the sides. Up close you start to see the detail:

- geometric patterns cut into the stonework
- carved wooden doors and window screens
- a high central dome above the prayer hall, hung with chandeliers
- carpeted floors in the prayer hall, where everyone goes barefoot

After dark the whole building is floodlit. The domes glow against the sky over Sharm El Maya, and this is the view most visitors come to photograph. You can enjoy it from the square outside without going in.

Give it 30–45 minutes for the mosque itself. Allow longer if you are pairing it with a wander through the market.

## Dressing and behaving for a working mosque

Non-Muslim visitors are generally welcome outside prayer times, but the rules are firm and easy to follow:

- **Women:** cover your hair, arms and legs. A loose scarf and an ankle-length skirt or trousers will do.
- **Men:** wear long trousers and a shirt with sleeves. Beachwear and shorts are not appropriate.
- **Shoes:** take them off before stepping onto the carpet. Carry them, or leave them on the racks.
- **Photos:** ask before photographing anyone at prayer, and don't use flash in the prayer hall.

Each of the five daily prayers starts with the call from the minarets. When you hear it, stay near the back or step outside until the prayer ends. Friday's midday congregational prayer draws the biggest gathering of the week, so leave the interior for another day.

## Getting there from Naama Bay and the resorts

The mosque is in Old Sharm (Sharm El Maya), right beside the Old Market and a short way inland from Sharm El Maya bay and the port. Use the market as your landmark, because every taxi driver knows it. Many resort hotels in Naama Bay and along the coast also run evening shuttles to the Old Market.

Agree the taxi fare before you get in. Drivers here often don't use the meter, and settling the price first saves an argument at the end. The address code V78W+H39 works in Google Maps if you'd rather navigate yourself.

## Timing your visit around the crowds

At the moment the mosque is listed as open 10am–10pm daily. Mosques adjust visitor access around prayers and religious holidays, so check locally before making a special trip.

BestTime's forecast of typical foot traffic points to two quiet windows. The first is 10am–2pm, any day of the week. The second is 9pm–10pm, when the lights are on but the evening rush has passed.

Try not to arrive between 6pm and 9pm on weekends. That is when the square fills with tour groups and families coming over from the market.

The late window is the best of both. You get the floodlit domes and fewer people in your photos, and you're right by the market when you finish. During Ramadan the evening rhythm shifts around iftar and night prayers, so expect it to feel busier and more devotional.

## Pairing it with Old Sharm

The mosque fits neatly into an evening in Sharm El Maya. Walk the Old Market lanes for spice stalls, lanterns, perfume oils and shisha cafés, then come back to the square as the lights come up.

The Heavenly Cathedral, the Coptic Orthodox church known for its painted ceilings, is in the Hay El Nour area. Seeing both on the same afternoon shows you Sharm's two faiths side by side.

In the market, bargaining is expected, so start well below the first figure you're quoted. Carry small Egyptian pound notes for stalls and for tipping the shoe attendants at the mosque door.
