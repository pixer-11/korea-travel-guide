Knee-high walls of black, pitted basalt run alongside the path. Behind them sit fields of root vegetables, and beyond those the sea is very pale over sand and dark over lava rock. Ribbons tied to fence posts mark the way in two colours. That is what Jeju Olle Routes 19 and 20 look like, and it is where the Jeju Olle Walking Festival was set for November 5 to 7, 2026.

## Three days on the northeast coast

The festival was planned as a walk, not a stage show. There was no single venue. The "venue" was two connected stretches of the Jeju Olle trail network, walked on foot by everyone who registered.

Route 19 starts near Jocheon Manse Hill, the memorial to Jeju's part in the 1919 independence movement. From there it heads east past Hamdeok Beach and ends at Gimnyeong's small harbour. Route 20 picks up at Gimnyeong and follows the shore through Woljeong-ri and Pyeongdae-ri to the Jeju Haenyeo Museum near Sehwa. Each one is a full day's walk for most people.

One geographic note: the festival carried Seogwipo in its billing, but these two routes are on the northeast coast. Seogwipo's harbour town is on the far side of Hallasan, on the south coast. Many visitors to this stretch stay in Jeju City or in the beach villages along the route instead.

## Registration, not tickets

Entry was free, and walkers joined by pre-registering. The festival carried the Jeju Olle name, and registration and the programme for each edition sit with the organiser, not with a ticketing platform.

There was no box office and no seat to pick. The format gave registered walkers a start point, a route and some company for the day. Anyone walking the same path without registering is simply on a public trail.

## Blue arrows, orange arrows, and the pony

The waymarking system is the heart of any Olle walk. Jeju Olle trails use a small set of markers that most walkers learn within an hour:

- **Blue arrows** point in the standard direction of the route.
- **Orange arrows** point the other way, for walkers going in reverse.
- **Blue and orange ribbons** are tied to branches, poles and fences at junctions.
- **The ganse**, a stylised pony figure, marks the trail and gives Olle its emblem.

Stamp stations stand at the start, middle and end points of each route. Walkers with an Olle passport press a stamp into it at each one. Walking both 19 and 20 fills a page for each route.

## What the ground is like in November

The terrain is mostly gentle. The path follows farm lanes between stone walls, short stretches of beach and boardwalk, village alleys and coastal road edges. There are no long climbs, but the full distance adds up across a day.

On Jeju, November falls in tangerine season. Orange fruit hangs over walls along the island's rural lanes, and silver grass turns the open ground pale. The northeast coast takes the wind straight off the sea in autumn. Walkers usually wear a windproof layer even when the sun is out, plus shoes that can handle wet basalt.

Hamdeok, Woljeong-ri and Pyeongdae-ri break up the routes with cafés, convenience stores and toilets. The quieter farm sections between villages have little or nothing.

## Walking with the Olle crowd

Olle etiquette is mostly about the villages along the way. The trails cross private fields and run past people's front gates, so a few habits are taken for granted:

- **Walkers keep to the marked path.** Stone walls mark someone's field, and the produce inside belongs to the farmer, tangerines included.
- **Voices stay low in the alleys.** Many lanes run right past houses.
- **Greetings are normal.** A quick *annyeonghaseyo* to the haenyeo (women divers) or the farmers along the route is welcome.
- **Rubbish goes out with the walker.** Bins are scarce between villages.

Card payment and mobile pay work in most cafés and convenience stores along the coast. Small village shops and stalls are where cash still comes in handy. Korean walkers tend to set off in the morning and finish in the early afternoon, then eat in the end village before taking the bus home.

## Getting to the trailheads

The 201 bus is the key link. It runs the east coast road between Jeju City and Seogwipo and passes through Jocheon, Hamdeok, Gimnyeong and Sehwa. That means one line serves the start, the handover point and the finish of both routes.

A typical Olle day on this stretch goes like this: walkers ride the 201 to the starting point, walk, and ride it back from wherever they finish. Taxis also work the coast, and the island's taxi apps are widely used. A car is less use on a one-way walk, since the finish is a long way from where it was parked.

## Before and after the walk

The Jeju Haenyeo Museum, at the end of Route 20, explains the culture of Jeju's free-diving women. That culture is on UNESCO's intangible heritage list, and the museum made a natural end point for the festival. The coast around Woljeong-ri is lined with cafés looking out over the water.

Seongsan Ilchulbong, the tuff cone at Jeju's eastern tip, is a short ride further along the coast. Seogwipo itself, home to the Jeju Olle Tourist Center and the south-coast routes, is the gateway to the rest of the trail network.
