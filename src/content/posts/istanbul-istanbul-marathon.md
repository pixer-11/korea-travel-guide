## A race that starts on one continent

The start line is in Asia. The 2026 Istanbul Marathon, the event's 48th edition, was set for November 1, 2026. The start area was near the 15 July Martyrs Bridge, the suspension bridge across the Bosphorus that most people still call the first bridge.

The opening kilometres are on the bridge deck. On every other day it carries motorway traffic only, and walkers aren't allowed on it. On race morning it is handed over to runners. Below are the water, the ferries and the minarets of the European shore, and the field reaches Europe before most races have finished their warm-up loop.

## The route from bridge to Sultanahmet

Once across, the announced course followed the European side of the Bosphorus and worked its way into the old city. Two landmarks along the route make good orientation points:

- **Dolmabahçe Palace**, the long white Ottoman palace on the shore at Beşiktaş
- **Galata Bridge**, the low two-level bridge over the Golden Horn between Karaköy and Eminönü, usually lined with fishermen's rods

The finish was in **Sultanahmet**. That's the historic peninsula with the Blue Mosque, Hagia Sophia and the old Hippodrome square.

The bridge is the only real climb at the start. The approach rises from the Asian shore before levelling out over the water. Runners who push hard on it often pay for it in the later kilometres.

## Registering and confirming the details

Registration for every distance and entry category went through one place: the official Istanbul Marathon website (maratonistanbul.com), rather than reseller pages. The programme also included bib collection and a race expo.

International entrants usually collect race packs in person in the days before the race, showing the ID they registered with. Shorter distances and a non-competitive public run have traditionally been held alongside the marathon.

## Race-day logistics, Istanbul style

The city closes major roads for this race. That changes how everyone gets around on the morning.

- **Getting an İstanbulkart.** This rechargeable transit card works on the metro, Marmaray, trams, buses and ferries. Machines in any major station sell and top it up.
- **Crossing to the start.** The Marmaray rail tunnel under the Bosphorus links the European side to Üsküdar on the Asian side. It's the most reliable way to reach the start area when bridges and coast roads are shut.
- **Forget taxis.** With roads closed, a car can sit stuck in traffic while a start wave sets off.
- **Tram changes.** The T1 tram line runs through Sultanahmet, but parts of the finish area are usually taken over by the race, and the transport operator issues race-day notices.

In Turkish the race is the İstanbul Maratonu, and that's the name on the signs. Crowds along the route traditionally shout "Haydi!" (come on!). Shouting it back at the people cheering goes down well.

## Watching without running

Spectators get the best scenery. Galata Bridge is an easy place to stand: it has a pedestrian level, tram links on both ends and views of the Golden Horn. The waterfront near Dolmabahçe Palace is another good stretch, close to the Kabataş tram and ferry terminals.

Seeing the start means crossing to the Asian side by ferry or Marmaray. The bridge is closed to traffic, so the water and the tunnel are the ways across. Near the finish in Sultanahmet, crowds usually build up around the Hippodrome. Phone networks can be slow when thousands of people are in one square, so runners and supporters often fix a meeting point in advance.

## After the finish line

The finish sits in the middle of Istanbul's best-known sights, so a recovery walk doubles as a sightseeing walk. The Blue Mosque and Hagia Sophia are both working mosques. Visitors cover their shoulders and knees, women cover their hair, and everyone takes their shoes off at the entrance. A finisher's foil blanket doesn't count as covering up; a light layer from the bag-drop does.

Mosques close to visitors during the five daily prayers, and the times shift with the season. Basilica Cistern and the Grand Bazaar are within walking distance, though tired legs may not agree. Most cafés and shops around Sultanahmet take cards. Small change is handy for simit (the sesame bread rings sold from street carts) and for public toilets.

November in Istanbul is usually cool, and showers are common, so a dry layer is welcome after a race. The marathon remains one of the only ways to cross the Bosphorus on foot, something very few visitors ever get to do.
