## Ten days on a Kisama hillside

The 2026 festival runs December 1–10. Opening day is no accident: December 1 is Nagaland Statehood Day, and the festival has started on that date since it began in 2000.

The ground is the Naga Heritage Village at Kisama. It is a terraced hillside lined with morungs, the traditional bachelors' dormitories of Naga villages, rebuilt here in each tribe's own style. The thatch, carved posts and buffalo-skull decorations differ from one tribe's morung to the next.

The name comes from the great hornbill. Its feathers appear on the ceremonial headgear of many Naga tribes, and the bird runs through their folklore.

## What fills the days

The core of each day is the central arena. Tribal troupes in full ceremonial dress perform war dances, harvest songs and log-drum rituals in front of the stands.

Around the arena, attendees usually move between:

- **The morungs**, where each tribe serves its own cooking: smoked pork with bamboo shoot, dishes made with axone (fermented soybean), and rice beer.
- **Craft stalls** selling woven shawls, wood carving, beadwork and bamboo work.
- **Indigenous games and contests**, which in earlier editions included a raja mircha (ghost pepper) eating competition.

After dark, the action shifts into Kohima town. Earlier editions paired the daytime programme with a night carnival and a rock contest, which reflects how big a part music plays in Naga life.

## Reaching Kisama and staying nearby

Dimapur is the gateway. It has Nagaland's airport and its main railway station, and Kohima is roughly 74 km uphill by road, usually a three-hour drive or shared-taxi ride.

From Kohima, taxis and shuttles run the 12 km out to Kisama along the highway toward Imphal. Festival traffic slows the road at the start and end of each day. Visitors usually stay in Kohima's hotels and homestays, or in village homestays at Kisama and Kigwema, within walking distance of the gates.

Kohima sits at about 1,400 metres. December days are mild and clear, but nights turn properly cold, so attendees pack warm layers.

Indian citizens from outside Nagaland need an Inner Line Permit, which the state government issues through its online ILP portal. Foreign nationals follow separate registration rules, which the same state authorities set out. The Nagaland government's tourism department runs the festival and publishes its programme.

## Moving through the morungs politely

The morungs are hosted spaces, not stage sets. Visitors usually step in, greet whoever is tending the fire and eat at the benches, often from bamboo or leaf-lined plates.

Performers in ceremonial dress are generally happy to be photographed. Most visitors still ask first, and nobody handles the headgear: hornbill feathers and boar tusks carry real status.

Rice beer is offered in bamboo cups, and declining with a smile is fine. Small stalls lean on cash and UPI payments, and change runs short on busy afternoons.

Many visitors add a day or two either side of the festival for:

- **Kohima War Cemetery**, the 1944 battle site on Garrison Hill.
- **Kohima Village (Bara Basti)**, one of the largest Naga villages.
- **Khonoma**, a green village known for conservation.
- **Dzükou Valley**, reached on a day trek from Viswema or Zakhama.
