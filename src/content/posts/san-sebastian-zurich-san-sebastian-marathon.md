## A Late-Autumn Race on a Flat Bay

The 2026 race was set for November 22, 2026. That put the 47th Zurich Donostia San Sebastián Marathon at the tail end of the European autumn season, when the summer beach crowds have gone home.

This is one of Spain's oldest and most respected marathons. Its reputation comes down to one thing above all: the course is flat. It stays low along the coast and through the city's grid of streets, with no mountain passes to climb, so it draws runners chasing a personal best as well as first-timers.

The city has two names. San Sebastián is the Spanish one and Donostia is the Basque one. Signs, the race's official name and the shouts from the pavement use both.

## The Course: Sea Wall, River and Grid

The route stays close to sea level the whole way. It has long, straight stretches past the curve of La Concha bay, with the green hump of Isla Santa Clara sitting in the water. It also passes the wide streets of the 19th-century centre and follows the Urumea river, where it meets the Atlantic.

Flat does not mean easy. The open waterfront sections can catch a wind off the Bay of Biscay, and runners often tuck in behind others there.

Start and finish have traditionally been in the Anoeta sports area, south of the centre.

Watching costs nothing. Spectators simply line the route.

## November on the Bay of Biscay

This is a green, Atlantic city, and late November is cool and often damp. Rain is common at this time of year.

Runners typically start in a throwaway layer and keep a waterproof for afterwards, with dry clothes at bag drop for a quick change. The sea air feels colder than the thermometer once you stop moving.

Daylight is short at this time of year. The upside is that the city is back to its everyday rhythm, without the August crush on the promenade.

## Getting to Donostia

San Sebastián is compact, and most of it is walkable from the race area. Getting into town is the longer part of the trip.

- **San Sebastián airport** sits in Hondarribia, east along the coast. It's small, with mostly domestic flights via Madrid and Barcelona.
- **Bilbao airport** has far more international routes. Direct coaches run to San Sebastián in about an hour and a quarter.
- **Biarritz airport** in France is another option, crossing the border via Hendaye.
- **By rail**, Renfe trains arrive at Donostia's Estación del Norte, by the Urumea. The Euskotren "Topo" line links the city with Hendaye on the French border.

Hotel space in a city this size is tight on big race weekends. The Gros and Amara neighbourhoods are practical bases: both are flat and well connected.

## Pintxos After the Finish Line

San Sebastián is where race-day refuelling gets serious. The Parte Vieja (Old Town) is a tight grid of narrow streets just behind the harbour. Its bars line their counters with pintxos: small bites on bread, held together with a cocktail stick.

The Gros neighbourhood, across the river near Zurriola beach, has its own bar crawl and is usually less hectic than the old town. Either way, the evening after a marathon here tends to become a slow wander from one counter to the next.

## Cheering and Eating the Donostia Way

**On the course.** Spectators mostly gather along the promenade at La Concha and in the city-centre streets. That's where the energy is. Basque cheers sound different: "Aupa!" (come on!) and "Animo!", and "Zorionak" (congratulations) at the finish. Shouting them yourself goes down well.

**In the pintxo bars.** Walk up to the counter, catch the bartender's eye and point or ask. Many bars also have a chalkboard of hot pintxos cooked to order, which are worth asking about. Have one or two bites and a small drink, then move on. Hopping from bar to bar is the custom, not settling in.

**Paying.** Many bars let you keep a mental tally and settle up as you leave, telling them what you had. Cards are widely accepted, but smaller places sometimes prefer cash. Tipping is modest: rounding up or leaving small change is normal.

**Timing your meals.** Spaniards eat late. Lunch runs well into the afternoon and dinner rarely starts before 9pm, so the bars fill on that schedule. Straight after a race, pintxo bars are the easiest option, since kitchens often close between services.

**Getting around on race day.** Marathon days bring road closures along the course. Walking or the local Dbus city buses are the simplest ways around.
