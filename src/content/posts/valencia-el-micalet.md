## A tower that names a city's skyline

Stand in Plaça de la Reina and look up. The stone shaft of El Micalet rises from the corner of Valencia Cathedral, eight-sided and plain, with a belfry stage at the top. Valencians call it the Micalet; in Spanish it is the Miguelete.

Building began in 1381 and ran into the early fifteenth century. The name comes from the great bell inside, which was consecrated on the feast of Saint Michael. For six centuries it has been the old town's tallest marker. Anyone lost in the lanes of Ciutat Vella still uses it to get their bearings.

## The stone screw: roughly 207 steps

The climb is one narrow spiral staircase. A worn stone stair wraps around a central column, lit by slit windows that give you a glimpse of the plaza each time you turn.

There is no lift and no separate down staircase. People heading up and people coming down share the same tight curve, so you will press against the wall to let others pass more than once. Expect 10–15 minutes going up at an easy pace, with small pauses at the window slits.

A few things to know before you start:

- The steps are uneven and polished smooth in places, so wear shoes with grip.
- Leave large bags at your hotel; a backpack gets awkward on the turns.
- This is not a climb for anyone who struggles with tight, enclosed spaces.
- Bring water in summer, because the stairwell holds the heat.

## What the top terrace gives you

You come out among the bells, then onto an open terrace ringed by a stone parapet. It's the best free-standing view of the old town's layout.

Look down first. The domes and bell towers of Ciutat Vella crowd right up against the cathedral. Next door, the blue-tiled dome of the Basilica de la Mare de Déu dels Desemparats sits on Plaça de la Mare de Déu. Farther out, find the Mercado Central's roofline and the tower of Santa Caterina.

On a clear day the view runs out to the white curves of the City of Arts and Sciences and the line of the Mediterranean. Plan on 20–30 minutes at the top. Bring a camera with a decent zoom if you care about the rooftop detail.

## The bells and the Thursday court below

The tower is still a working belfry. Its bells strike the hours, and being beside one when it sounds is loud enough to make you flinch. Time your stay accordingly if you have sensitive ears or a small child.

Valencia keeps a long tradition of hand bell-ringing, and the cathedral bells are still rung by hand on feast days. Down at street level, a much older ritual happens on the cathedral's other side. Every Thursday at noon the Tribunal de les Aigües meets at the Gothic Apostles' Door on Plaça de la Mare de Déu.

This is the water court that has settled irrigation disputes among the farmers of the huerta for centuries. Proceedings are spoken in Valencian and over quickly. If you're climbing on a Thursday, go up mid-morning and be back down in the square before twelve.

## Climbing without the shuffle

Tickets for the tower are sold at its base, and they're separate from cathedral admission. Current prices are posted at the ticket point and on the cathedral's official site. Ask there too about the last entry time, which falls before the posted 5:45pm closing.

Arriving right at 10am usually beats the tour groups that work through the old town from late morning. A crowded stairwell means constant stopping to let people pass, so that early slot makes the biggest difference here. Late afternoon can also ease off as day-trippers leave, though you're racing the closing time.

Some etiquette that helps on a single shared staircase:

- Climbers coming down generally get priority on the narrowest turns; step to the column side and wait.
- Don't stop to photograph on the stairs themselves; save it for the window slits or the top.
- Keep voices low near the bells and on the terrace, as this is still part of a working cathedral.
- If you also visit the cathedral interior, cover shoulders and knees.

## Getting to Plaça de la Reina

The tower stands where Plaça de la Reina meets the cathedral, at the southern edge of the Ciutat Vella. The square is pedestrian-friendly and the old town has no metro station inside it.

The nearest metro stop is Colón (Lines 3, 5, 7 and 9), about a 15-minute walk north-west through the shopping streets. Many EMT city buses stop around Plaça de la Reina itself. Walking in from the Mercado Central takes under ten minutes, which makes the two a natural pairing.

## Building a morning around it

The tower works best as the anchor of a half-day in the old town. Once you've seen the streets from above, they make a lot more sense on foot.

A route that keeps the walking short:

- **Valencia Cathedral:** the chapel housing the Santo Cáliz, venerated as the Holy Grail, sits just inside.
- **Plaça de la Mare de Déu:** the basilica, the Turia fountain and the Apostles' Door, all one minute away.
- **Mercado Central:** the modernista market hall, best visited in the morning when the stalls are fullest.
- **La Llotja de la Seda:** the UNESCO-listed Gothic silk exchange, directly opposite the market.

Allow about an hour for the climb and the view, and three to four hours if you add the rest. Doing the tower first gives you the map in your head for everything after.
