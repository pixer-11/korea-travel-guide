Most of Seville's grand houses have been museums for a century or more. Palacio de las Dueñas hasn't: the Alba family still keeps rooms here, and you can feel it in the way the place is arranged, more like a lived-in home with some doors opened to the public than a stage set for tourists.

The building dates to the late 1400s, and the mix of styles shows it. Gothic brickwork sits under Mudejar tilework and Renaissance arches around the main courtyard, added and adjusted over five centuries rather than built to a single plan. Orange trees, palms and a fountain fill the courtyard, and the surrounding arcade is where most visitors linger longest, cameras up, looking at the tiled dado running around every column.

## What's actually inside

An audio guide is included with the ticket and it's worth taking; the rooms aren't heavily labeled otherwise.

The interior holds the Alba family's own collection of decorative arts, furniture and paintings, built up over generations rather than assembled for display. Look for:

- The small private chapel, still consecrated
- Family portraits and period furniture in the main salons
- Azulejo tilework and carved wooden ceilings throughout the ground floor
- A room dedicated to poet Antonio Machado, who was born on the premises in 1875

The Machado connection matters here more than at almost any other Seville palace. His father rented rooms in the building, and the poet's early childhood memories of the courtyard turn up in his later work. A modest exhibit marks the spot.

## Getting there and timing your visit

The palace sits in Casco Antiguo, Seville's old town, on Calle Dueñas, a short walk northeast of the cathedral and Plaza del Salvador, easily combined with a wander through the Alfalfa neighborhood. There's no dedicated parking; the historic center is largely pedestrianized, so most visitors arrive on foot from a hotel nearby or by taxi to the edge of the old town.

It's open every day of the week, 10am to 7pm, with no midday closure. Because it's a well-visited landmark rather than a quiet local spot, weekends fill up between 11am and 6pm; if you're coming on a Saturday or Sunday, try to arrive right at 10am or hold off until later afternoon. On weekdays, the calmest hour is 6pm to 7pm, right before closing, which also happens to be a good time to catch softer light in the courtyard.

Budget 60 to 90 minutes. It's a compact route compared to the Alcázar, so it doesn't demand a half day, but the audio guide rewards slowing down rather than rushing the loop.

## Reading the courtyard like the family does

The main patio is the heart of the visit, and it's designed to be walked around, not just photographed from one spot. Circle the full arcade rather than stopping at the first good angle; the tile patterns and carved capitals change as you go, and the far corners are usually less crowded.

Because rooms here are still used by the family, some areas rotate or close without much notice. If a room mentioned on the audio guide is roped off, it isn't a mistake, just the reality of a private home doubling as a visit.

Photography is generally fine in the courtyards; check signage before using a flash indoors, where furnishings and paintings are original pieces rather than replicas.

## Pairing it with the rest of the old town

Palacio de las Dueñas works well as a counterpoint to the Real Alcázar and Casa de Pilatos, both within easy walking distance and both grander in scale. Seeing Dueñas either just before or after one of them gives a useful contrast: this one still has someone living upstairs.

After the visit, the Alfalfa and Plaza del Salvador area nearby has tapas bars and small squares worth sitting in, a natural place to end up once you've done the loop through the palace's rooms and courtyards.
