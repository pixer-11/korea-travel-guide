## A trident on a desert slope

Out on the water, the shape rises out of a bare tan slope above the bay. A central stem branches into three arms, like a candelabra or a trident. It is cut straight into the hardened sand and lies on the peninsula's northern flank. It faces the sea, not the town.

The editorial record puts it possibly around 200 BCE. It has survived because this stretch of coast gets almost no rain. The same dryness means any footprint left on the slope can last for years. That is why people view it from a distance rather than walking up to it.

## The theories nobody has settled

No inscription explains it, so the origin stories pile up. You'll hear most of them from boat guides:

- **The Paracas culture:** the ancient people who lived on this peninsula, and the reason for the early date
- **A sailors' marker:** a landmark you can see from far out at sea, so a navigation aid
- **A plant symbol:** some read the arms as a cactus or a ritual plant
- **Later origins:** theories linking it to pirates or to the independence era in the early 1800s

Treat every version as a story, not a fact. That uncertainty is a big part of why it's worth the stop.

## Getting the boat right

Very few people visit on land. The boat is the visit. Here's how it works:

- **Book the night before.** Agencies line the El Chaco waterfront, and hotels sell the same Ballestas departures. They all stop at the Candelabro on the way out.
- **Go early.** Paracas gets strong winds, usually later in the day, and they can cancel afternoon sailings. A morning departure is safer and the water is calmer.
- **Expect a pier fee.** A boarding or reserve fee is charged at the dock, separate from your ticket. Ask the agency what's included rather than guessing.
- **Pick your side.** Ask the crew which side of the boat passes closest to the hill, then sit there. The boat only stops for a few minutes.
- **Dress for spray.** The boats are open speedboats, so bring a windproof layer and something to keep your phone dry.
- **Tip the guide.** It's normal to tip the guide who narrates in Spanish and English at the end, but nobody makes a fuss if you don't.

Bring a zoom lens if you have one. The geoglyph is huge, but the boat stays offshore.

## Building a Paracas morning around it

The Candelabro takes up maybe ten minutes of a roughly two-hour trip. The rest is the main event for most people. The Islas Ballestas have sea lions on the rocks, plus Humboldt penguins and huge seabird colonies on guano-covered arches.

Back on land by late morning, you have the afternoon free for the **Paracas National Reserve**. It covers most of the peninsula, with red-sand beaches and cliff viewpoints over the Pacific. Most people get around it on a half-day tour or by taxi from town.

Finish on the El Chaco waterfront, where seafood restaurants serve ceviche facing the pier you left from. The whole loop fits into one unhurried day. That suits the many travellers who stop in Paracas between Lima and Huacachina or Nazca.
