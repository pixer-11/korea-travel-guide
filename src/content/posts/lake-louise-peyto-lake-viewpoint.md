## A wolf's head in glacial blue

From the platform, the whole valley drops away below you. Peyto Lake lies far down in a cradle of spruce. Its milky turquoise water has a shape people compare to a wolf's head, with the snout pointing up the valley toward Peyto Glacier.

The colour comes from rock flour: fine silt the glacier grinds off the mountains and carries down in meltwater. The silt hangs in the water and scatters light, which is why the lake looks almost opaque from above. The lake and glacier are named after Bill Peyto, one of the early outfitters and guides in the Banff backcountry.

## Up the Icefields Parkway to Bow Summit

The viewpoint is off Highway 93 North, the Icefields Parkway. Head north from the Trans-Canada junction near Lake Louise and watch for the Bow Summit and Peyto Lake signs. Bow Summit is the highest point on the parkway, a little over 2,000 metres, so expect cooler air than in the village.

- **Drive time:** about 40 minutes from Lake Louise, about 1.5 hours from Banff town.
- **Park pass:** you need a valid Parks Canada pass on the Icefields Parkway. Current prices are on the Parks Canada website.
- **Fuel and signal:** there's no fuel and little mobile coverage on this part of the road. Fill up in Lake Louise.

## From the car park to the railing

Most visitors leave the car in the main Bow Summit lot and walk a paved path uphill through subalpine forest. It's short, roughly 10–15 minutes, but steady. At this altitude you may notice it in your breath.

The path opens onto a wide viewing platform built out over the slope. Expect to spend 30–45 minutes taking it in. If you want more, informal trails lead higher along the ridge for wider views over the Mistaya Valley. Stick to marked routes, because alpine meadow here recovers very slowly once trampled.

## Getting the platform to yourself

The car park is always open, but the people show up on a pattern. Day tours from Banff and Lake Louise tend to reach Bow Summit through the middle of the day. Arriving soon after sunrise or in the early evening usually means fewer elbows at the railing. In summer that is also when the low light picks out the ridgelines.

- **Arrive early or late.** The lot is small for how many people stop here, so a mid-morning arrival can mean circling for a space.
- **Don't block the front.** People step up to the rail, take their photos and move back. Do the same rather than setting up a tripod right at the front.
- **Keep the drone packed.** Flying drones is prohibited in Canada's national parks without a permit.
- **Leave wildlife alone.** Grizzly and black bears live along the parkway. Stay in your car if you see one by the road, and carry bear spray if you go past the platform.
- **Pack layers.** It's often several degrees colder at Bow Summit than at the lake, even in July.

## Which months for which colour

The lake is usually frozen and snow-covered until late spring. It typically thaws in June, but the colour is often muted at first. Glacial melt peaks in July and August, and that's when the famous blue is strongest.

September brings golden larches on the higher slopes and thinner crowds, though early snow is possible. In winter the parkway stays open, but conditions change fast. The viewpoint path isn't maintained as a summer trail then, so snowshoes or traction help. Check Parks Canada for current road and trail reports before you set out.

## Stops along the same stretch

Peyto Lake fits neatly into a parkway day, and a few other stops sit close by:

- **Bow Lake:** a few minutes south, with the red-roofed Num-Ti-Jah Lodge on its shore and Bow Glacier above.
- **Crowfoot Glacier viewpoint:** a roadside pullout just south of Bow Lake.
- **Waterfowl Lakes:** north of Bow Summit, with Mount Chephren reflected in the water.
- **Mistaya Canyon:** further north again, a short walk down to a narrow, water-carved limestone gorge.

Pair Peyto with Bow Lake on the way up and Mistaya Canyon at the turnaround. That makes an easy half day out of Lake Louise.
