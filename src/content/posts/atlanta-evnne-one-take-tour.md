## Getting across metro Atlanta

Atlanta is spread out, and most visitors underestimate how far apart things are. Hartsfield-Jackson International Airport (ATL) sits south of downtown, with its own MARTA rail station. From there, Red and Gold Line trains run north through downtown and Midtown.

MARTA covers the city's core well. It doesn't reach most of Gwinnett County, where Duluth and much of Atlanta's Korean community are based. If the concert hall is out in the suburbs, most fans end up driving or using rideshare.

Friday evening traffic on I-85 and I-285 is heavy. People coming from outside the Perimeter usually allow far more time than the map app first suggests. Rideshare pickups after the show tend to bunch up at the venue exits, so many people walk a few blocks before requesting a car.

## Buford Highway to Duluth: Korean food before doors

Atlanta has one of the largest Korean communities in the American South, so dinner before the show can carry on from what you're there for. Buford Highway runs northeast out of the city through Chamblee and Doraville. It's a long, plain-looking road of strip malls packed with Korean, Vietnamese, Chinese and Mexican restaurants.

Further out, Pleasant Hill Road in Duluth has its own run of Korean barbecue spots, bakeries, karaoke rooms and the large H Mart. Fans often:

- share grilled pork belly and banchan at a barbecue table before a show;
- pick up snacks and drinks at H Mart for the drive home;
- stop at a Korean bakery or café to meet up with friends and finish fan-made banners.

Korean barbecue restaurants in Gwinnett get very busy on Friday nights. Groups often eat in the late afternoon instead of at the dinner peak.

## Lightsticks, merch lines and fan chants

A K-pop show runs on unwritten rules, and newcomers pick them up quickly. Here's how these nights usually work.

**The merch line.** Official merchandise usually goes on sale at the venue before doors open. At K-pop shows this queue often forms hours ahead and can be longer than the line to get in. Fans who mainly want a lightstick or photocards tend to get in this line first and eat afterwards.

**Lightsticks.** EVNNE has an official lightstick. Many venues sync official lightsticks to the show by Bluetooth, so the whole room changes colour together. Phone flashlights come out for ballads instead.

**Fan chants.** Members' names and set call-and-response lines are shouted during specific songs. Fans learn them from guide videos the group's label posts. If you don't know them, cheering along at the chorus is fine.

**Freebies and trading.** Fan clubs often hand out free photocards, stickers or printed slogans outside the venue. Trading photocards in the queue is normal and friendly. Payment at the merch stand is usually by card.

**Phones.** Filming rules depend on the tour and the venue, and staff announce them. Phones held high block the view behind them, and regular concertgoers will tell you so.

## A late-January night out

January is Atlanta's coldest month. Days are often mild, but evenings can turn properly cold, and fans can spend a long time standing outside. People who have queued for merch before usually bring a warm layer they can tie around their waist or stash once they're inside.

After the show, Midtown and the Korean restaurants in Duluth stay open late enough for a debrief over food. Visitors from out of town often stay near the venue or along the MARTA line. That saves a long rideshare across the city after midnight.
