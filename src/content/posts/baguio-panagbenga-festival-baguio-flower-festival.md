## A month of flowers on a mountain city

Panagbenga is a Kankanaey word for "season of blooming." Baguio started the festival in 1995 as a lift for a city still rebuilding after the 1990 Luzon earthquake. The flowers became its symbol of recovery. The 2027 edition runs through February 2027.

February suits it. Baguio sits roughly 1,500 metres up in the Cordillera, so the dry season brings cool mornings and pine-scented air. Visitors from the lowlands usually pack a jacket.

The month is built around a few anchor events:

- **Grand Street Dance Parade:** schools and community groups in costume, dancing routines inspired by Cordillera traditions.
- **Grand Float Parade:** floats covered in real blooms, many of them sponsored by companies.
- **Session in Bloom:** Session Road shut to cars and lined with food and craft stalls.
- **Market Encounter:** stalls and evening activity in and around Burnham Park.

## Where it happens

Everything centres on downtown. The parades come down Session Road, Baguio's main commercial street, and finish at Burnham Park. The lake and rowboats at Burnham sit right beside the parade finish. The Baguio Cathedral, up its long stairway, overlooks the top of Session Road.

Most visitors walk once they reach downtown. On parade days the central roads close and taxis can barely move. Many people stay within walking distance of Session Road for that reason. Guesthouses in the Legarda Road area and around the Upper Session hills are a short walk downhill.

Crowds swell on parade weekends, and hotels across the city usually fill up then. Visitors who come midweek in February still get the flower displays, the Session Road stalls and far thinner streets.

## Tickets and getting up the mountain

Lining the parade route is free. In past editions, the ticketed part was grandstand seating at the Burnham Park end. Those seats give you a full view of each group's set routine. The official Panagbenga channels and the City of Baguio publish the parade schedule and how seats are sold. No prices appear here because none were provided.

Most visitors come by bus from Manila. Victory Liner, Genesis and Joy Bus run regular trips to Baguio. Private cars take TPLEX and climb by Marcos Highway or Kennon Road. Traffic on the mountain roads gets heavy on festival weekends. Travellers often take overnight buses to reach downtown before the roads close.

## Watching the parade from the kerb

The best kerbside spots fill hours before a parade. Families stake out the slope of Session Road with folding stools and umbrellas. The middle stretch of the road gives you a clear view down the hill. Near the finish, the crush around Burnham Park is thickest.

How people usually get through the day:

- **Payment:** street stalls mostly take cash in small notes, and GCash is common.
- **Water and snacks:** vendors walk the route, but queues at the downtown fast-food places get long.
- **Floats:** the flowers are real and often parked for public viewing after the parade. Look, photograph, and leave the blooms alone.
- **Strawberries and pasalubong:** the Baguio City Market off Magsaysay Avenue sells strawberry jam, ube jam and peanut brittle to take home.
- **Leaving:** people wait out the crowd at Burnham Park instead of joining the crush toward the jeepney terminals.

Baguio residents call the city the "Summer Capital." In February it fills with lowlanders escaping the heat, so the festival pace is slow and sociable. Treat parade day as a full day rather than a quick stop.
