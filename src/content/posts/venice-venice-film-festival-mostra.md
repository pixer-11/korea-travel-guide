## Why go

You come for the oldest film festival on earth, running since 1932, and for the strange, specific thrill of a red carpet laid out on a beach island rather than a city street. For eleven days every September, the Lido di Venezia trades its usual sleepy seaside pace for camera flashes, yacht taxis, and a genuine concentration of world cinema.

## Getting there

The festival is based on the Lido, a narrow barrier island a short vaporetto ride from central Venice, not in Venice's historic core itself. From Piazzale Roma or Venice Santa Lucia train station, take the ACTV vaporetto Line 1, 5.1 or 6 to the Lido S.M.E. stop. From there it's a walk or a quick bus (A or B) down Gran Viale Santa Maria Elisabetta to the Palazzo del Cinema, the festival's main venue on Lungomare Marconi.

Base yourself in Venice proper or on the Lido itself — hotels here fill fast in early September, so book well ahead if you're coming for the festival dates.

## What to see and do

The main event is the Palazzo del Cinema, home to red-carpet premieres, press conferences, and the main competition screenings judged this year by a jury led by Maggie Gyllenhaal. Alongside it, the Sala Grande and the newer PalaBiennale host additional programme strands, from Orizzonti to out-of-competition galas.

Even without a ticket, you can:

- Stand along the red carpet route outside the Palazzo in late afternoon and early evening, when arrivals cluster before premieres
- Browse the Lido's temporary festival village, with pop-up bars, merchandise stalls and screens showing highlights
- Catch a glimpse of the Excelsior Hotel nearby, the traditional base for visiting stars and press

Screenings themselves are open to the public for a portion of tickets each year, alongside accredited industry and press slots — worth checking well ahead if a specific film matters to you.

## When to go

The 2026 edition runs 2-12 September. Early in the run tends to be calmer before word spreads about buzzy titles; the final weekend brings the awards ceremony and heavier crowds chasing last-chance screenings.

September on the Lido is still beach-warm, so pack for heat and sun as well as evening layers for late premieres. Confirm exact daily schedules on the official Biennale Cinema site once the full programme drops, usually a few weeks before opening.

## How to visit like a local

Venetians and festival regulars treat the Lido crowd differently depending on the hour. Mornings are for badge-holders and industry screenings; the public red-carpet watching really builds from late afternoon onward, so arrive a couple of hours before a premiere if you want a decent viewing spot along the barriers.

Payment on the Lido during the festival is mostly card and mobile, same as the rest of Venice, though small kiosks and gelato stands may prefer cash for quick transactions. Tipping isn't expected beyond rounding up at cafés.

Public tickets for screenings, when available, are typically sold online through the official festival platform in tranches rather than all at once — the mistake first-timers make is assuming they can just walk up and buy one at the door. Check the official site as soon as the programme is announced.

Locals commute to the Lido by vaporetto rather than driving, and dressing for a premiere doesn't mean dressing for the beach walk there — bring shoes you can actually walk in from the dock. Above all, treat published dates, ticket windows and venue details as provisional until you've confirmed them on labiennale.org, since festival logistics shift from year to year.

## Nearby to combine with your trip

Pair a festival day with a vaporetto hop back into Venice for St Mark's Square or the Rialto Bridge, both under 30 minutes away by water. The Lido itself is worth an extra hour for its Liberty-style architecture and quiet beach clubs, a striking contrast to the festival's crush of photographers just streets away.
