## A casino turned gallery on the Luján

The building comes first, and it is meant to. It stands at the end of Paseo Victorica as a cream-coloured palace with a mansard roof, facing the Río Luján. It opened in 1912 as the Tigre Club, a casino and hotel for wealthy Buenos Aires families who came up the river for the weekend.

Gambling was banned in the 1930s and the club faded. After a long restoration it reopened as an art museum in 2006. Today the interior is as much a part of the visit as anything hanging on its walls.

## Inside the old ballrooms

You climb the front steps into rooms built for showing off. Expect painted ceilings, gilded mouldings, heavy chandeliers and a sweeping central staircase. The former salons now hold paintings and sculpture.

The collection covers Argentine figurative art from the late 19th century through the 20th. Many works show the Paraná Delta, the river and the people who lived along it. The landscapes link up with the brown water you can see from the windows, which is the reason to come here rather than a gallery in the city.

Plan on about 1–2 hours. That leaves time for the galleries, the upper floors and a slow look at the ceilings, which many visitors miss because they're looking at the walls.

## Getting to Paseo Victorica

Tigre is an easy day trip from central Buenos Aires. You have two rail options:

- **Tren Mitre (Tigre branch)** from Retiro to Tigre station, the terminus. The ride takes around 50 minutes.
- **Tren de la Costa**, a slower and more scenic line along the river. Its last stop is Delta station, near Parque de la Costa.

From either station, the museum is a riverside walk of roughly 20–25 minutes. You cross the bridge and follow Paseo Victorica, a promenade lined with rowing clubs and old houses. On the way you pass the Museo Naval de la Nación. Taxis and ride-hailing apps also work if your legs are tired.

## Doing the palace properly

This is one of Tigre's best-known sights, and it gets crowded. Weekends between 12pm and 6pm are its busiest hours, which means every hour it is open on Saturday and Sunday. If you can, come Wednesday to Friday after the 1pm opening. Don't plan a Monday or Tuesday trip around it, because the doors are currently shut both days.

A few habits help:

- **Check the official site before going.** Museum hours can shift with the season and on public holidays.
- **Look for the price at the entrance or on the museum's website.** Fees and free-entry arrangements change, so we don't print them here.
- **Ask about guided visits at the front desk.** Guides explain the history of the club, which is the half of the story the labels skip.
- **Learn a little Spanish.** Staff and signage are mostly in Spanish. A "buenas tardes" goes over well, and so does knowing that *sala* means gallery room.
- **Treat it like a historic house.** Don't lean on the banisters or the gilded surfaces. If you're unsure whether photos are allowed in a room, ask first.

## The terrace and the river after

Go back outside and walk around the building to the waterfront side. The view across the Luján, with rowing crews and *lanchas colectivas* (passenger launches) heading for the delta islands, shows you why the club was built here.

From the museum you can work your way back through town. Puerto de Frutos, the riverside market for wicker, furniture and crafts, is on the other side of the centre. Delta boat trips leave from the Estación Fluvial near the train station. If you want to see more of Tigre's buildings, the Museo del Mate is close by too.

## When to time your day

Because the museum doesn't open until midday or 1pm, use the morning for something else. Take an early boat into the delta or browse Puerto de Frutos, then reach Paseo Victorica after lunch. Spring and autumn are the most comfortable seasons for the riverside walk. In summer, the building's high-ceilinged rooms give you a break from the afternoon heat.

Leave enough time before the 6pm close to get around the whole collection. The last hour goes faster than you'd expect once the ceilings catch your eye.
