## Serangoon Road after dark

Serangoon Road is the spine of it all. For roughly ten weeks around the festival, arches and strings of lights span the road. Their motifs come from Deepavali itself: oil lamps, peacocks, lotus flowers. After sunset the whole stretch glows gold and magenta above the traffic.

Deepavali, the Hindu festival of lights, falls on 8 November 2026. The light-up runs from late September to early December, so you are not tied to a single night. That gives you about ten weeks to see the decorations and nearly the same stretch for the bazaars, temples and food around them.

## Dates, tickets and what is actually ticketed

The core experience is free and open: these are public streets. You walk under the lights, browse the stalls and step into temples without a ticket. Some programmed items are run separately, such as stage performances, opening ceremonies or guided heritage walks. Their timings and any booking requirements are set by the organisers each year.

Confirm timing, venues and tickets on the official site before you build a trip around a specific performance. For the street light-up alone, any evening in the window works.

A few calendar details worth knowing:

- **Deepavali day itself** is a public holiday in Singapore. In 2026 it falls on a Sunday, so the following Monday is the day off under Singapore's holiday rules.
- **The days just before Deepavali** are the peak shopping period, and the most crowded.
- **Late September and early December** sit at the quiet ends of the light-up period.

## The eve-of-festival bazaar crush

This is the part of Deepavali that belongs to Little India alone. In the final days before the festival, families come to stock up for the holiday. Expect them to buy:

- new clothes, especially saris and kurtas
- brass oil lamps and clay diyas
- jasmine and marigold garlands
- boxes of sweets and savoury snacks like murukku

The festive bazaar stalls cluster in the lanes off Serangoon Road, around Campbell Lane and Hastings Road. Stalls sit shoulder to shoulder under awnings, stacked with bangles, coloured powders for rangoli floor patterns and tins of snacks. On the last evenings before Deepavali, the lanes move at a shuffle.

If you want to feel the festival at full pressure, go then. If you want to actually look at things, go earlier in the light-up season.

## Getting to Little India

Take the MRT. Roads around Serangoon are congested in festival season, and parking is scarce.

- **Little India station** (North East Line NE7, Downtown Line DT12) is the obvious stop. Follow signs toward Buffalo Road and Tekka Centre, and you surface a short walk from Serangoon Road.
- **Farrer Park station** (NE8) sits at the northern end of Serangoon Road. Use it to walk the light-up southward without fighting the main crowd at the start.
- **Rochor station** (DT13) works for the southern end, near Sungei Road.

Walking the full lit stretch with a few stops takes one to two hours. Add an hour or more if you plan to eat and browse the bazaar properly.

## Temples, lamps and the festival's meaning

Deepavali celebrates the victory of light over darkness. Among Singapore's Tamil community, the story most often told is Krishna's defeat of the demon Narakasura. Homes light oil lamps, families wear new clothes, and many visit temple early on the morning of Deepavali.

Two temples on Serangoon Road anchor the district:

- **Sri Veeramakaliamman Temple**, dedicated to Kali, with a colourful gopuram (tower gateway) crowded with sculpted figures.
- **Sri Srinivasa Perumal Temple**, dedicated to Vishnu, further north toward Farrer Park.

The **Indian Heritage Centre** on Campbell Lane is useful context before you walk out into the lights. Its galleries cover the history of Indians in Singapore and the region.

## Eating your way through Deepavali

Little India runs on food in festival season. **Tekka Centre**, at the corner of Serangoon Road and Buffalo Road, is the district's hawker centre and wet market. It is the place for biryani, prata, thosai and teh tarik at hawker-stall level prices.

Along Race Course Road and the side streets, banana-leaf restaurants serve rice with curries and vegetables on a leaf, often eaten by hand. Sweet shops stack their counters with jalebi, ladoo and burfi, and many sell them boxed as gifts for the season.

**Mustafa Centre** on Syed Alwi Road is a vast department store, packed in the weeks before Deepavali. Locals go there for gift boxes, spices and festive clothing.

## Moving through the festival crowds

Little India is busy at the best of times, and in festival season it is packed. A bit of local behaviour makes the evening easier.

- **Greet people properly.** "Happy Deepavali" is standard. In Tamil, the community's main language here, it is "Deepavali Vazhthukkal". Locals mostly say "Deepavali" rather than "Diwali".
- **Dress for the temples.** Cover shoulders and knees, and take your shoes off at the entrance. Shoe racks are provided. Ask before photographing worshippers or priests during prayers.
- **Eat with your right hand** at banana-leaf places. When you finish, folding the leaf toward you signals you enjoyed the meal.
- **Pay small.** Cards and QR payments like PayNow and NETS are widely accepted. Some bazaar stalls and flower sellers still prefer cash, so carry small notes. Tipping is not customary at hawker stalls.
- **Mind the rangoli.** The coloured patterns on shop floors and pavements are decorations and blessings. Step around them, never across.

Arriving earlier in the evening, as the lights come on, usually beats the after-dinner surge, and weekdays are gentler than weekends. Keep bags in front of you in the densest lanes. Agree on a meeting point, such as the Tekka Centre corner, before your group splits up.
