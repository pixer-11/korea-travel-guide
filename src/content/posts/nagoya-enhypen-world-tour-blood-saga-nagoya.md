## Two nights to close the year

The calendar does a lot of the work here. ENHYPEN's World Tour "BLOOD SAGA" takes place at Vantelin Dome Nagoya on December 26 and 27, 2026, a Saturday and a Sunday. Most of Japan is easing into the year-end holidays by then.

That timing shapes everything around the shows. Trains fill with people heading home for New Year, hotels absorb both travellers and fans, and the city is lit for winter. For ENGENE, the fandom's name, it means two dome nights to finish 2026.

The group came out of the survival show *I-LAND* under BELIFT LAB. Japan has long been one of its biggest markets, and a dome booking reflects that scale.

## Inside Vantelin Dome Nagoya

This is a baseball stadium first. The dome opened in 1997 as Nagoya Dome and is the home ground of the Chunichi Dragons. Its silver roof is a fixed, closed shell, so the weather outside never reaches the seats.

For concerts the field becomes arena floor, and the stands curve around it in tiers. Floor seats give you the stage at eye level. The upper stands give you the full shape of the production and the lightstick sea, which at dome scale is half the show.

Distances are long from the back rows. Many fans bring compact binoculars, a habit at Japanese dome concerts generally.

## Getting to Dome-mae Yada

The subway is the obvious route. Nagoya Dome-mae Yada Station on the Meijo Line is the closest stop, a short walk from the dome along a covered pedestrian route. The Meijo Line is a loop, so it links directly with Sakae and connects with other lines heading to Nagoya Station.

Other approaches spread the load:

- **Ozone Station**: served by JR Chuo Line, Meitetsu Seto Line, the Meijo Line and the Yutorito Line, roughly a 10–15 minute walk.
- **Yutorito Line**: the elevated guideway bus has its own Nagoya Dome-mae Yada stop.
- **From the shinkansen**: Nagoya Station is on the Tokaido Shinkansen, and the trip to the dome by subway involves one change.

Driving is the weakest choice on a concert day. Fans overwhelmingly come by rail.

## Entry, ID checks and the merch line

Japanese dome concerts run on their own etiquette. Tickets for big K-pop tours in Japan are usually allocated by lottery, with fan club members getting the first rounds. Many tickets are digital and tied to the buyer's phone. The tour organiser publishes the ticketing route and entry rules on its official channels.

What attendees typically do on the day:

- **Carry photo ID.** Name checks at entry are common at Japanese concerts, and resold tickets are frequently refused.
- **Queue for merch in the daytime.** Official goods booths usually open hours before the show, and the lines move in long, marshalled snakes.
- **Keep the lightstick charged.** Official lightsticks are often synchronised to the stage during the show.
- **Stay seated when asked.** Japanese venues expect you to keep to your seat, and filming during the show is generally not permitted.

Staff hold signs and direct queues; people follow them quietly. Cheering is loud and fan chants are well rehearsed, but the queuing is orderly.

## The post-encore squeeze at Yada

The last song ends, and tens of thousands of people head for the same few stations. Dome staff usually release blocks of seats in stages, so leaving can take a while even before you reach the street.

Nagoya Dome-mae Yada absorbs the biggest wave and runs controlled entry to the platforms. Plenty of fans walk on to Ozone instead, where three rail operators share the crowd. Others wait it out: AEON Mall Nagoya Dome-mae stands right beside the dome, and its food courts and restaurants give you somewhere warm while the platforms clear.

For anyone catching a late shinkansen, that exit time is the real constraint. Many out-of-town fans stay overnight in Nagoya for exactly this reason.

## Eating and sleeping around a December dome weekend

Nagoya's food is distinctive enough to plan meals around. The city's cooking leans on dark hatcho miso and strong flavours:

- **Miso katsu**: pork cutlet under a thick, sweet miso sauce.
- **Tebasaki**: peppery fried chicken wings, a standard izakaya order.
- **Hitsumabushi**: grilled eel over rice, eaten in three stages, the last with broth poured over.
- **Kishimen**: flat, wide wheat noodles in a light broth, often eaten at station stands.

Visitors usually stay around Nagoya Station or Sakae. Both are on the subway network for the dome, and both have dense clusters of restaurants open late. Rooms tend to tighten on dome weekends, and late December adds holiday travellers on top.

## The day between shows

Fans attending both nights get a free afternoon in Higashi-ku. The Tokugawa Art Museum and the Tokugawa Garden are a short ride from the dome. The museum holds treasures of the Owari Tokugawa family, including scrolls from the *Tale of Genji*.

Further in, Osu's shopping arcades mix secondhand clothing, electronics and street snacks around Osu Kannon temple. Nagoya Castle, with its golden shachihoko on the roof, is on the Meijo Line too. Opening hours at these sites shift through the year and around New Year, and each publishes its own schedule.
