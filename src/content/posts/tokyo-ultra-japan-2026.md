Odaiba Ultra Park sits on reclaimed land jutting into Tokyo Bay, with the Rainbow Bridge and the city skyline as a backdrop to the main stage. For one weekend in September, this waterfront park becomes the regional anchor of the global Ultra Music Festival circuit, pulling in electronic music fans from across Asia and beyond.

## What Ultra Japan actually is

Ultra Japan is the Tokyo edition of the Ultra Music Festival franchise, the same brand behind Miami's original event and sister festivals around the world. It runs across two days, September 19-20, 2026, at Odaiba Ultra Park.

The 2026 Phase 3 lineup announcement includes Zedd, Alesso, Peggy Gou and Knock2 among the headliners, spread across multiple stages. Expect a mix of mainstage EDM, techno and bass-heavy sets, with production values (LED walls, pyrotechnics, elaborate stage design) that mirror Ultra's other global stops.

## Getting to Odaiba Ultra Park

Odaiba is an artificial island in Tokyo Bay, and the festival site sits within its waterfront park area, walkable from several transit stops. Your best routes in:

- **Yurikamome Line** (the elevated automated train) to Odaiba-Kaihinkoen Station, then a short walk.
- **Rinkai Line** to Tokyo Teleport Station, also a walkable distance from the park.
- Taxis and rideshares can drop off nearby, but expect heavy congestion around festival start and end times.

Driving is not recommended. Parking near the venue is limited and traffic on the bridges connecting Odaiba to central Tokyo backs up badly during peak festival ingress and egress.

## Tickets and what they cover

Ultra Japan sells single-day and two-day general admission passes, along with VIP and elevated ticket tiers that typically include separate viewing areas and shorter bar lines. Exact pricing, tiers and any early-bird windows are set and updated by the organizers directly.

Confirm timing and tickets on the official Ultra Japan site before making plans. Don't rely on resale platforms or unofficial vendors for entry tickets; counterfeit and duplicate QR codes are a recurring problem at large festivals of this size, and Ultra's own channel is the only place to verify a ticket's validity.

## Reading the set times like a local raver

Ultra festivals worldwide publish a detailed stage-by-stage schedule only a short window before doors open, and Ultra Japan follows the same pattern. Once that schedule lands on the official app or site, treat it as your day's blueprint rather than wandering in blind.

Mainstage headliners are almost always saved for the last two hours of each night, so if your priority is a specific act like Zedd or Peggy Gou, don't burn your energy early. Smaller stages tend to run tighter, more underground sets earlier in the afternoon, which is a good window to explore before the crowd thickens.

## Odaiba's day-into-night shift

What makes Ultra Japan distinct from Ultra's other stops is the setting itself: a park built for daytime leisure that transforms into a full-scale night festival against a bay view. Afternoon sets play out under open sky with the bay breeze cutting the September heat, while the same stages take on a different character after dark with lighting rigs replacing sunlight as the main visual.

September in Tokyo is still warm and humid, so daytime sets can feel intense; many attendees pace themselves, staying hydrated and finding shade near the park's edges between sets. Locals who've done multiple editions tend to treat the first few hours as a warm-up and save their energy for the after-dark stretch, when the temperature drops and the production lighting comes into its own.

## Where to stay and eat around Odaiba

Odaiba itself has hotels aimed at tourists and business travelers, many within walking distance or a short train ride of the park, making it convenient if you don't want to deal with late-night transit back to central Tokyo. Staying in areas like Shimbashi or Shinagawa, both a quick ride from Odaiba via the Yurikamome or JR lines, also works well and gives you more restaurant options for pre- or post-festival meals.

Odaiba's shopping malls, including Aqua City and DiverCity, sit near the festival grounds and have food courts and restaurants that stay busy with festivalgoers grabbing a meal before heading in. Convenience stores nearby are useful for grabbing water, snacks or basic supplies before entry, since festival food and drink prices run higher than what you'd pay outside the gates.

## Practical notes for festival day

A few things worth sorting out before you leave your hotel:

- Bag policies and prohibited items are set by the organizers and listed on the official site; check them so you're not turned away at security.
- Trains back from Odaiba after the festival ends can get crowded fast, so budget extra time if you're relying on the last trains.
- September weather in Tokyo can include rain, so a light poncho or waterproof layer is worth packing given the outdoor, waterfront setting.

Ultra Japan draws a large international crowd, and Odaiba's transit links, while efficient, are not built to move tens of thousands of people instantly. Building in buffer time on both ends of each festival day will save you more stress than almost anything else on this list.
