## Eleven days, four colours

Dubai goes red, green, white and black. From November 26 to December 6, 2026, the UAE marks its 55th Eid Al Etihad, the name now used for National Day. The programme mixes free concerts, heritage performances and light shows across Dubai, Sharjah, Abu Dhabi and Ras Al Khaimah.

The city dresses for it. Flags run along overpasses and lamp posts, mall atriums fill with tricolour displays, and tower facades along Sheikh Zayed Road light up in the national colours after dark. Children turn up to school in kandoras and abayas trimmed with flag ribbon.

## What December 2 actually marks

The date is the hinge of everything. On 2 December 1971, six emirates joined to form the United Arab Emirates: Abu Dhabi, Dubai, Sharjah, Ajman, Umm Al Quwain and Fujairah. Ras Al Khaimah joined in early 1972, making seven.

Two other days sit inside the celebration window:

- **Commemoration Day, 30 November**: the nation honours Emiratis who died in service. The mood is respectful rather than festive, and many people pause at midday for the minute's silence.
- **2 December**: the main day, with the official national ceremony broadcast on television that evening and fireworks in several emirates.

The 55th anniversary carries extra weight. Round-numbered years usually bring a larger programme than ordinary ones.

## Where the celebrations happen in Dubai

There is no single venue. Eid Al Etihad in Dubai is spread across public spaces rather than staged in one arena. Dubai's citywide festival programme appears on Dubai Calendar, the city's official events listing run by Dubai Festivals and Retail Establishment.

In past years, National Day fireworks in Dubai have lit up waterfront and heritage spots such as Al Seef on Dubai Creek, Dubai Festival City and the mountain town of Hatta. Malls stage their own shows, and Global Village, which runs through the cooler months, adds heritage performances and flag-themed nights.

For the old-Dubai side of things, the creek is the place to be. Al Seef is a short walk from Al Fahidi Station on the Metro Green Line. The Al Fahidi Historical Neighbourhood and its wind-tower houses sit right beside it.

## Tickets, free shows and the official ceremony

Most of it costs nothing. The concerts, heritage shows and light displays in the 2026 programme are free and open to the public. You don't need a ticket for fireworks over the creek or a performance in a mall atrium.

Ticketed exceptions do exist. Attractions that always charge, Global Village among them, still need a ticket on National Day nights. Prices appear on each venue's own website and at the gate.

The official national ceremony is a separate thing: a large televised production that most residents watch at home or on big screens in public spaces. The venue changes from year to year, so it doesn't fit into a standard Dubai itinerary.

## Flags on the bonnet: the car parade custom

The roads become part of the party. Emirati drivers, and plenty of residents, wrap their cars in flag decals, portraits of the rulers and slogans in the national colours. On the evening of December 2, slow-moving convoys of decorated SUVs fill roads such as Jumeirah Beach Road and The Walk at JBR, horns going.

Police in Dubai set rules on this every year, and they are enforced. Typically:

- No stickers on the windscreen or anything blocking the driver's view.
- Number plates stay fully visible.
- Nobody rides standing through a sunroof or sitting on a window ledge.
- Foam and silly-string spray cans are banned at public gatherings.

If you're watching rather than driving, the beachfront promenades are where most of the convoys pass.

## Joining in without getting it wrong

The welcome is real, and visitors are encouraged to join in. Wearing the colours is normal. A flag scarf or a pin is the easy way in, and supermarkets and Dubai Mall kiosks sell flags and accessories through late November.

A few things matter:

- **Respect the flag.** Don't let it touch the ground, don't sit on it and don't use it as a costume. Damaged flags are folded away, not thrown in a bin.
- **Commemoration Day is solemn.** On 30 November, people keep their voices down at public observances. Party outfits and music feel wrong that day.
- **Ask before photographing people,** especially Emirati women and families. Wide shots of performers on a stage are fine.
- **Stand for the anthem.** "Ishy Bilady" opens official performances, and everyone stands for it.

Heritage stages usually feature **Al Ayyala**, a dance listed by UNESCO in which two rows of men sway with thin bamboo canes to drums and chanted poetry. You'll often see **Al Razfa** too, its livelier relative. When the lines move, the audience generally stays behind the performers' space.

On the busiest nights, families often take the Metro to waterfront events rather than drive, because traffic around the fireworks sites jams up. The Red Line serves Jumeirah Lakes Towers and Dubai Marina for JBR, and Burj Khalifa/Dubai Mall for downtown.

## Beyond Dubai: four emirates, one long week

Dubai is only one stage. The 2026 programme runs across Sharjah, Abu Dhabi and Ras Al Khaimah as well, and each has its own character.

- **Sharjah**, next door, leans into heritage, with events in and around its restored old quarter. Traffic on the Dubai–Sharjah road gets heavy in the evenings.
- **Abu Dhabi**, about 90 minutes' drive away, is the federal capital. It tends to host the largest official gatherings and fireworks along the Corniche.
- **Ras Al Khaimah**, roughly an hour north of Dubai, sets its celebrations against the Hajar Mountains and the coast.

Early December is one of the best times of year to be outdoors in the UAE. Daytime temperatures are warm rather than punishing and the evenings are mild, which is exactly why so much of the programme runs outside after dark.
