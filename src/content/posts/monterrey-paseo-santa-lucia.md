## A river built on a founding spring

Monterrey never had a river walk. It built one. Paseo Santa Lucía is a canal of still, clear water roughly 2.5 kilometres long. It runs from the edge of the Macroplaza, in the city's Centro, east to the old steelworks at Parque Fundidora.

The name comes from the Ojo de Agua de Santa Lucía, the springs beside which Diego de Montemayor founded the city in 1596. The street on the address carries his name. The canal opened in 2007, built for the Universal Forum of Cultures that the city hosted that year.

## What the walk actually feels like

The canal sits below street level, so the traffic noise of Centro drops away once you walk down. Paved walkways line both banks. Footbridges cross overhead at intervals, and planted borders and fountains break up the stone.

As you head east, the downtown towers fall behind you. The jagged saddle of Cerro de la Silla rises ahead on clear days. At the Fundidora end, the rusted bulk of Horno 3, a former blast furnace, marks where you finish. The contrast between the two ends is the whole story of Monterrey in a single stroll.

## Riding the canal from Museo de Historia Mexicana to Horno 3

The canal carries boats as well as walkers. The main boarding point sits at the western end, beside the Museo de Historia Mexicana and the Museo del Noreste. Boats then glide east toward Parque Fundidora.

The boat suits you better than the walk if:

- you are visiting in summer, when Monterrey heat makes 2.5 km on foot hard going
- you are travelling with children or anyone who can't walk far
- you want to take photos of the bridges from water level

Boats run within the listed hours of 10am to 9pm, every day of the week. Check the current fare at the ticket booth by the landing.

## Getting to the Macroplaza end

Start at the western end. The address on Calle Diego de Montemayor puts you right behind the Museo de Historia Mexicana, on the east side of the Macroplaza.

- **Metro:** Zaragoza station on Line 2 opens onto the Macroplaza; walk east across the plaza to the museums.
- **On foot from Barrio Antiguo:** the old quarter's cobbled streets sit just south, a few minutes away.
- **From the other end:** Parque Fundidora station on Line 1 drops you near the eastern finish, if you'd rather walk the route in reverse.

Allow 40 minutes to an hour to walk the full length at an easy pace. That leaves no time for stops, so budget two to three hours if you want a museum at one end.

## Timing the light, the heat and the hours

The paseo is listed as open from 10am to 9pm, seven days a week. That schedule is the same on every day, Sundays included.

Monterrey summers are fierce, and the canal has stretches with little shade. From roughly May to September, a late-afternoon start makes sense. You walk as the sun drops and finish around dusk at Fundidora, before the 9pm close. In the cooler months, from November to February, a morning walk after the 10am opening works comfortably.

## Walking the canal without the hassle

This is a quieter attraction than the Macroplaza above it, and the habits are simple.

- **Pay at the booth.** Boat tickets are bought at the ticket office by the landing, not on board. Carry some pesos in case card payment isn't working.
- **Walk one way, ride or ride-hail back.** Most people do the route in one direction. From Fundidora, the Line 1 metro or a ride-hailing app gets you back to Centro.
- **Ask in Spanish.** "¿Dónde se toma la lancha?" (where do I catch the boat?) gets you pointed to the landing. "Lancha" is the word staff use for the boats.
- **Keep off the water's edge with small children.** The banks are open in places, without railings along every stretch.
- **Bring water.** Bring a full bottle on hot days. The walk between the two ends is long, and Fundidora's cafés are at the far end.

## Pairing it with both ends

The canal makes the most sense as a link between two of the city's main sights rather than a stop on its own.

At the western end:

- **Museo de Historia Mexicana**, next to the boat landing
- **Museo del Noreste**, connected to it, covering the history of northeastern Mexico and Texas
- **Macroplaza**, with the Faro del Comercio and the Palacio de Gobierno

At the eastern end:

- **Horno 3**, the converted blast furnace now home to the Museo del Acero
- **Parque Fundidora**, the old steelworks grounds turned into a park with lawns, paths and industrial relics

One route that works is a museum morning at the Macroplaza and an afternoon walk or boat ride east. End with sunset among the chimneys at Fundidora.
