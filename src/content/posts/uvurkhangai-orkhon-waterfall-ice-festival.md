## When the Orkhon stops falling

For two days in December, a waterfall becomes a wall you can climb. The Orkhon Waterfall, called Ulaan Tsutgalan in Mongolian, drops roughly 20 metres into a dark basalt gorge in the upper Orkhon Valley. By December the spray and the main fall have frozen into columns and curtains of blue-white ice.

The Orkhon Waterfall Ice Festival runs on December 5-6, 2026, and puts ice climbers on that frozen face. It is a small, remote event. There are no stadium seats, just a gorge rim, a frozen pool and a lot of cold air between them.

## Dates, tickets and the winter calendar

These are the essentials:

- **Dates:** December 5-6, 2026
- **Venue:** the frozen Orkhon Waterfall, Uvurkhangai province
- **What happens:** an annual ice-climbing festival on the waterfall itself
- **Season:** the festival opens Mongolia's winter festival season, which runs from early December to March

Entry and ticket arrangements come from the festival organiser. This guide does not carry prices or start times, because none are given in our facts. Many visitors come as part of an organised winter tour, and the operator handles transport, lodging and festival access as one package.

## The gorge and the climb

The setting is volcanic. Eruptions and earthquakes shaped this gorge roughly 20,000 years ago, and the walls are black basalt streaked with frost. In winter the Ulaan Tsutgalan river above the falls lies under snow, and the ice below builds outward in thick, ribbed layers.

Spectators usually split between two viewpoints. From the rim you look straight down on the climbers' ropes and the tops of the ice columns. A steep path leads to the base, where you see the full height of the frozen fall and hear axes biting into it.

Climbers work with ice axes and crampons, roped from anchors above. The pace is slow and deliberate, which suits watching. A single route can take a while, and you get time to see each placement.

## The long road to Ulaan Tsutgalan

Getting here is a big part of the trip. The waterfall sits well west of Ulaanbaatar, beyond Kharkhorin. The final stretch runs over unpaved valley tracks that snow and ice make slower in December.

Most visitors split the journey. A common route is Ulaanbaatar to Kharkhorin on the paved road, then on via the spa town of Khujirt towards the falls. In Kharkhorin, Erdene Zuu Monastery is the usual stop, with its long white wall of stupas. The whole area belongs to the Orkhon Valley Cultural Landscape, a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

## Dressing for the Khangai in December

The cold here is serious. In the Khangai mountains, December temperatures regularly drop well below minus 20°C, and the gorge floor sits in shade for much of the short day. Standing still while you watch is colder than hiking.

Experienced attendees usually pack:

- an insulated down jacket and a windproof outer layer
- felt-lined or heavily insulated boots with good grip for the icy path
- microspikes or light crampons for the descent to the base
- mittens over thin liner gloves, so phones and cameras stay usable
- a thermos, and spare camera batteries kept in an inside pocket

## Ger camps, drivers and winter manners

Rural Mongolia in winter runs on its own rhythm. Most festival-goers travel with a hired driver in a Land Cruiser or a Russian UAZ van. Few people self-drive these tracks in snow. Many summer ger camps in the valley close for the season, so beds near the falls are limited, and organised groups usually reserve them as part of the package.

If you are invited into a family ger, a few customs matter:

- step over the threshold, never on it
- move clockwise inside, and don't walk between the stove's central posts
- take an offered bowl of suutei tsai, salty milk tea, with your right hand or both hands, and at least sip it

Cash in tögrög is the norm outside the cities, and card machines are rare in the valley. Mobile signal comes and goes in the gorge. Drivers and guides usually agree on meeting points beforehand rather than counting on a phone call.
