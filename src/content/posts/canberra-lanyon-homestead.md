## Where the suburbs run out

Tuggeranong's last roundabouts drop behind you on Tharwa Drive. Then the paddocks open up, and to the west the Brindabella Ranges fill the horizon. Lanyon Homestead sits on the flat land by the Murrumbidgee River. It is one of the oldest working pastoral properties in the Canberra region, and it was here long before anyone drew a federal capital on a map.

Most visitors to Canberra never come this far south. That suits the place. It is a slow, low-key outing: a house, a garden, outbuildings and a long view of hills. It feels a long way from Parliament House, and the drive takes about the time of a coffee.

## A house older than the capital

European settlement at Lanyon goes back to the 1830s, on Ngunnawal Country. The main homestead you walk through today dates to the 1850s. It was built for the Cunningham family, who held the property for generations. Behind the house are older, plainer stone and timber service buildings, and these tell you how the station actually ran.

The ACT Government now looks after Lanyon as a heritage site. The interiors are furnished to show domestic life on a rural estate. Going through the rooms gives you a sense of scale: a colonial pastoral family lived well, but a long way from anywhere.

## Inside, outside, and the café

The house interior is mainly seen on a guided tour. That is the best reason to come, so plan your day around it. Tour times can change, so check the ACT Historic Places website before you set off.

What you can do on your own:

- **The gardens and lawns.** Mature trees and wide grass slope down towards the river flats. Bring a picnic rug.
- **The outbuildings.** They are worth a slow loop, because the working side of the station lives here.
- **The café.** It's on the grounds, so lunch doesn't mean driving back into town.
- **The view west.** On a clear day you can see the Brindabellas over the paddocks.

Plan on about two hours. Allow longer if you take a tour and eat at the café.

## Timing a visit around short hours

Lanyon keeps short hours. At the moment it opens Wednesday to Sunday from 10am to 3pm and is closed Monday and Tuesday. Like most heritage properties, its hours can change through the year and over public holidays, so check the official site for the current month.

The 3pm close catches people out. If you arrive at 2pm, you get a rushed lap and probably no tour. Get there by about 10:30am, take a tour, then eat lunch at the café. That covers everything without watching the clock.

Canberra's seasons change the place a lot. In autumn the exotic trees colour up. Winter mornings in the valley can be frosty, while summer afternoons on open lawns get hot. Bring a hat from November to March.

## Getting around Lanyon without a car-park scramble

Driving is the realistic option. Follow Tharwa Drive south through Tuggeranong, past Banks and Conder, and the turn-off is well signposted before Tharwa village. Public transport out here is thin, so don't count on a bus to match the 3pm close.

On site, a few habits make the visit easier:

- **Ask about tours as soon as you arrive.** Then you can plan the garden and café around the tour time.
- **Card is normal** in Canberra, including for café orders. Tipping is optional in Australia and nobody expects it.
- **Treat the interiors like a museum.** Keep your hands off the furniture and follow the guide's lead on photos inside.
- **Stay on paths near the outbuildings.** Some of them are old and fragile.
- **Check the official site for entry and tour pricing.** It is posted there and not repeated here.

## Making a day of the Tharwa end

Lanyon pairs well with the rest of Canberra's quiet south. Tharwa village is a few minutes on. Its old bridge crosses the Murrumbidgee, and the river banks are popular for a swim or a paddle when the water is safe.

Go a little further and you reach Namadgi National Park. Its visitor centre, south of Tharwa on the Naas Road, is the starting point for walks into the ranges you could see from the homestead lawns.

A good order for the day is the homestead in the morning, lunch on site, then the river or Namadgi in the afternoon. Light lasts longer than Lanyon's hours, so finishing out in the hills makes sense.
