Chang'an Avenue runs east to west past Tiananmen Square, ten lanes of asphalt with the Mao portrait on one side and the square's flagpole on the other. That is where the Beijing Marathon has traditionally lined up its field.

## A race that has run since 1981

The Beijing Marathon is one of the oldest city marathons in Asia. It was first held in 1981. In Chinese it is 北京马拉松 (Běijīng Mǎlāsōng), and runners shorten that to 北马, "Bei Ma".

It is a major international road race. Elite fields from abroad share the start with tens of thousands of amateur runners. In earlier years the programme also included a half marathon. More recently it has been a full-marathon-only event, and the distances on offer can change from year to year.

## From Tiananmen to the Bird's Nest

The course traditionally links the political centre of the city with its Olympic north. On recent editions it has headed west along Chang'an Avenue from Tiananmen. It then turns north through Haidian District, a stretch of universities and tech campuses. The last section crosses into Chaoyang and finishes near the Olympic Green.

That finish sits right by the 2008 Olympic venues. The National Stadium, known as the Bird's Nest, has a steel lattice shell. The National Aquatics Center, the Water Cube, is wrapped in bubble-patterned panels. Exact routing can change between years, so the official course map for each edition is the definitive version.

## Getting a bib

In most years places can't simply be bought. Entry is usually by ballot. Runners register online during a set window and successful applicants are drawn from the pool. Foreign runners enter with their passport details and show the same passport when they collect their race pack.

Fees, entry windows, qualifying rules and international places change from year to year. Race numbers are tied to the registered runner's identity, so a bib bought from a reseller is not valid.

## Shouting jiāyóu on Chang'an Avenue

Watching from the roadside is free and open to everyone. The useful word to know is **加油 (jiāyóu)**, literally "add oil". It is the all-purpose Chinese cheer, and roadside crowds traditionally shout it at every runner who goes past, not only at the leaders.

Tiananmen is the hardest place to spectate. Getting into the square area always means a security checkpoint with ID checks and bag screening. Foreign visitors need a passport, not a photocopy. On race mornings the area around the start is typically restricted further. Most spectators usually head for points further along the course, or for the finish near the Olympic Green, where there is more room to stand.

## Moving around on race day

Road closures usually shut down large parts of central and northern Beijing on marathon mornings. Taxis and ride-hailing cars get stuck or rerouted. The metro is the reliable option.

- **Near the start:** Tiananmen East and Tiananmen West stations, both on Line 1
- **Near the finish:** Olympic Green and Olympic Sports Center stations, both on Line 8
- **Security:** every Beijing metro station scans bags at the entrance, so getting in takes extra time

Some exits near the course are often closed on race mornings. Runners usually reach the start very early, since security screening for a field this size takes time.

## Paying, eating and practicalities

Beijing runs on mobile payment. Alipay and WeChat Pay both let international visitors link a foreign bank card, which makes buying a meal or a metro ticket much simpler. Cash is a useful backup, though small vendors near the course do sometimes take only mobile payment.

Mid-October is usually counted among the best times of year in Beijing. It is past the summer heat and before the winter cold. Mornings can be cool, so runners often wear a throwaway layer at the start. Air quality varies from day to day, and many runners follow an air-quality index app in the days before a race.

## Staying on after the finish

The Olympic Green is a good place to recover. It is a long, flat pedestrian boulevard with the Bird's Nest and Water Cube along one side. North of it is the Olympic Forest Park, which has wide paths and a lake for a slow walk on tired legs.

The start area connects to the old city. The Forbidden City sits directly north of Tiananmen and takes at least half a day. Jingshan Park, just behind it, has a hill with views over the palace roofs. Nanluoguxiang and the hutong lanes around Houhai are a short metro ride away and good for an evening walk. Entry to the Forbidden City is by advance reservation with a passport, through its official channel.
