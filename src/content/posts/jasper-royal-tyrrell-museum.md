## Badlands, not mountains

Get the geography sorted first. The Royal Tyrrell Museum is at 1500 N Dinosaur Trail in Drumheller. That is the prairie badlands of southern Alberta, a long way from the peaks around Jasper.

Coming from Jasper, you are driving the better part of a day. Most people pair it with Calgary instead. The payoff for the drive is real: this is Canada's national-calibre museum of palaeontology, set inside Midland Provincial Park, with eroded grey and ochre coulees running right up to the car park.

## What waits inside

The building is low and sits into the hillside. Inside, the galleries run in a loose chronological loop, from early life through the age of dinosaurs and on to the Ice Age. Fossils found in the surrounding valley give the place its name. Joseph Burr Tyrrell found an Albertosaurus skull in these badlands in 1884.

Highlights to look for:

- **Dinosaur Hall**, the big room of mounted skeletons, including tyrannosaurs and horned dinosaurs from Alberta's rocks.
- **Borealopelta**, the armoured nodosaur found in an oil-sands mine near Fort McMurray. It is preserved so completely that it still looks like an animal, not a skeleton.
- **The Burgess Shale exhibit**, with strange Cambrian creatures from the Rockies' famous fossil beds.
- **Black Beauty**, the dark-boned Tyrannosaurus rex skeleton.
- **The preparation lab window**, where technicians work on real fossils.

The exhibits are interactive in places, which keeps children going. Give it three to four hours if you read the panels. A family moving fast still needs two.

## Timing your visit

The museum is busiest on weekends from 11am to 5pm. That is essentially the whole afternoon, so on a Saturday or Sunday, aim to be at the door when it opens and see Dinosaur Hall first.

Currently the museum opens 10am–5pm Tuesday to Sunday and closes on Mondays. Museum schedules shift through the year, especially between summer and the off-season, so check the official site for this month's hours. A weekday in the shoulder season usually means fewer school groups and family crowds.

## Booking, eating and the dig programs

Buy tickets online through the official site before you set out. Admission prices are posted there too. Hands-on programs fill up first. These include guided fossil digs and casting sessions, and they run on a seasonal schedule, so book them well ahead.

The museum has a cafeteria, which matters here. Options right next to the building are thin, so eat on site rather than driving into town mid-visit. The gift shop is near the exit. Leave it until the end unless you want to carry a plush Albertosaurus around the galleries.

Behave as you would in any working research museum. Look but don't touch where the signs say so. You may not collect fossils anywhere in the badlands outside a supervised program, and Alberta law protects them.

## Getting to Drumheller

From Calgary, take Highway 9 northeast for about 1.5 hours. The road crosses flat farmland until the valley suddenly opens up in front of you. From Edmonton, allow roughly three hours. There is no practical public transport for a day trip, so plan on driving.

The museum is a few minutes northwest of downtown Drumheller on North Dinosaur Trail (Highway 838). Follow the brown signs from the town centre.

## Round out the valley

The museum sits on a scenic loop, so build the rest of the day around it:

- **North Dinosaur Trail** continues west to **Horsethief Canyon**, a viewpoint over the layered badlands.
- **The Bleriot Ferry**, a small cable ferry across the Red Deer River, links the north and south loops when it is running.
- **Downtown Drumheller** has the giant fibreglass T. rex billed as the World's Largest Dinosaur. You can climb inside it.
- **The Hoodoos Trail** off Highway 10 near East Coulee has mushroom-capped sandstone pillars along a short boardwalk.
- **Atlas Coal Mine** is a preserved mine site that tells the valley's other story.

Summer in the valley gets hot and exposed. Carry water on the trails and wear a hat for the viewpoints.
