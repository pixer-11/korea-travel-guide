## What Fabula actually is

Fabula is not trying to be a generic "modern Chinese" restaurant. It's a specific culinary argument: that Cantonese refinement and Ningbo's saltier, funkier coastal cooking can sit on the same plate as European technique without either side losing its accent.

The kitchen is run by chef Zee Zheng, who cut his teeth as sous chef at Taian Table, one of Jing'an's better-known contemporary Chinese dining rooms. Fabula picked up a Michelin star not long after opening, which in Shanghai's crowded fine-dining scene is a fast way to go from unknown to booked-out.

## Why Cantonese and Ningbo, together

Pairing these two regional cuisines is the interesting part. Cantonese cooking prizes restraint: clean stocks, precise steaming, ingredients speaking for themselves. Ningbo food, from the coastal city south of Shanghai, leans into fermentation, cured seafood and stronger, saltier flavors built for rice and cold winters.

Putting them on one tasting menu means the meal can swing between delicate and assertive within a few courses. Add European technique on top, plating, sauce work, maybe a cheese or butter element, and you get a kitchen that's clearly trained in French or Western fine-dining discipline but refusing to let it flatten the Chinese flavor base underneath.

## Finding it in Jing'an

Jing'an is one of Shanghai's more established central districts, known for Jing'an Temple, high-end shopping along Nanjing Xi Lu, and a dense cluster of serious restaurants that also includes Taian Table itself, Zheng's old kitchen. Fabula sits within this scene rather than off on its own.

## The tasting menu format, and what to expect walking in

Restaurants at this level in Shanghai almost always run a fixed or semi-fixed tasting menu rather than an à la carte board, and Fabula follows that pattern. Expect a sequence of courses built around whatever the kitchen judges is at its best that week, seafood and produce driven by the Cantonese and Ningbo backbone rather than a static printed menu you can special-order from.

Do not walk in expecting to swap dishes freely or order a smaller plate to share; a room of this caliber is built around the chef's sequence, not diner customization. If you have dietary restrictions, flag them when you book, not when you sit down.

## Ordering and etiquette at the table

Tipping is not standard practice at most Chinese restaurants, including higher-end ones, so you generally do not need to calculate a percentage on top of the bill; check whether a service charge is already built in when the check arrives. Payment by mobile (Alipay or WeChat Pay) is standard across Shanghai dining, though a room at this level will also take major cards.

If you don't speak Mandarin, don't worry about ordering in the traditional sense: a tasting menu format means the sommelier or server-led wine and tea pairing conversation matters more than menu navigation. Ask about a tea pairing alongside or instead of wine; Chinese fine dining has increasingly built serious tea programs to sit next to the wine list, and a kitchen working this closely with Ningbo and Cantonese ingredients is a good candidate for one.

## Where it fits if you're already eating your way through Jing'an

If you're treating this as one stop on a broader Jing'an tasting circuit, it pairs naturally with a visit to Jing'an Temple earlier in the day and a walk down Nanjing Xi Lu beforehand to build an appetite. Given its lineage from Taian Table, diners who've already eaten there will recognize some of the technical DNA, precision plating, a similar seriousness about sourcing, even as Fabula pushes further into the Ningbo side of the flavor spectrum.

Treat Fabula as a destination meal rather than a casual dinner you decide on same-day. Confirm hours, address and current pricing through official channels before you commit an evening to it, since none of those specifics are fixed enough to print with confidence months in advance.
