## Grey Brick on Travessa da Sé

The lane is narrow, and that helps. Travessa da Sé runs between Senado Square and the Cathedral, and halfway along it you reach a plain wall of grey brick with a dark timber doorway. That's the whole street front of Lou Kau Mansion. The house is behind it, laid out deep instead of wide.

Inside, the noise of the square drops away. The house was built around 1889 and has several courtyards. The rooms run one after another, each separated by an open light well. It's part of the Historic Centre of Macao, the UNESCO World Heritage listing from 2005, and it's one of the few Chinese domestic buildings in that group you can actually walk through.

## The Merchant Behind the Door

Lou Kau, also known as Lou Wa Sio, was one of the richest Chinese merchants in nineteenth-century Macau. His money came largely from trading and from government gambling concessions. His family later built the Lou Lim Ieoc Garden in the north of the peninsula, so this town house is the earlier, more compact half of the family's story.

In Cantonese the house is called 盧家大屋, Lou Ka Tai Ok, meaning "the Lou family's big house". In Portuguese it's Casa de Lou Kau. Street signs and taxi drivers may use any of these names, so keep the Chinese characters handy on your phone.

## Reading the Light Wells

This is a house you read from the floor up. It follows the Xiguan style of Guangzhou merchant homes: grey brick, upright timber screens, and halls lined up along one axis. Between the halls are open courtyards, called light wells, which bring air and daylight into the middle of a deep, narrow plot.

When you stand in a light well, look up before you look around. What to notice:

- **Carved timber screens and lattice** separating the halls, some of them pierced so air can pass through
- **Plaster mouldings and painted friezes** along the tops of the walls
- **Coloured glass windows** on the upper level, often called Manchurian windows
- **Western touches** in some of the decorative detail, a sign of Macau's mixed influences

The layout is the reason to come. A merchant family greeted guests in the front halls, while the private rooms sat further in and upstairs. Walking from front to back takes you from the public part of the house to the private part, one courtyard at a time.

## Getting to Travessa da Sé

You'll do the last stretch on foot. The Macau Peninsula's historic core is a walking area, and Travessa da Sé is too narrow for cars to drop you at the door. The simplest way in is to head for Senado Square, the black-and-white wave-patterned square off Avenida de Almeida Ribeiro.

From the square, follow the signs uphill towards the Cathedral (Sé). The mansion is in the lane on your way, and the walk takes a few minutes. Most city buses stop along Avenida de Almeida Ribeiro. Tell a taxi driver "Largo do Senado" and walk the rest.

## Moving Through the House Quietly

The house works as a self-guided walk. Expect a doorway, a front hall and then the courtyards one after another. The editorial description calls it open to the public for tours, so ask at the entrance if a guided visit is running that day. Check the official Macao Cultural Affairs Bureau site or the entrance board for current entry details.

A few habits that make the visit go smoothly:

- **Keep to the marked route.** Some rooms are roped off, and the timber floors and screens are original, so don't lean on them.
- **Photograph without flash.** The courtyards give enough natural light, and flash flattens the carving anyway.
- **Talk quietly in the light wells.** The hard brick bounces sound around, and a loud group fills the whole house.
- **Let groups go ahead.** If a tour is crowding a hall, step back into a courtyard and wait a few minutes.

Cantonese is the main language here, Portuguese is official, and staff in tourist areas generally manage basic English. A simple "m̀h'gōi" (thank you) at the door goes down well.

## Beating the Weekend Tide

The weekend pattern is clear. From 11am to 6pm on Saturdays and Sundays is the busiest part of the week, and that's every weekend hour except the first one. If a weekend is your only option, be at the door at 10am, when it opens, and you'll have the courtyards before the Senado Square crowds spread up the lane.

On weekdays you can be more relaxed, but plan around Monday, when the house is closed. The hours stay the same Tuesday to Sunday, 10am to 6pm, so leave enough time and don't turn up at 5:45pm. Mid-morning on a weekday is a sensible default, and the light wells get good daylight for photos then.

## Around the Corner

The mansion fits into a half-day walk around the historic centre. All of these are within a short walk:

- **Cathedral of Macau (Sé):** at the top of the same lane, on the Largo da Sé
- **Senado Square:** a few minutes downhill, with the Leal Senado Building and the Holy House of Mercy
- **St Dominic's Church:** the yellow Baroque church at the top end of the square
- **Ruins of St Paul's:** about ten minutes' walk north through the shopping lanes, with the Mount Fortress above

A good route is to start at Lou Kau Mansion at 10am, climb to the Cathedral, then go back down through Senado Square towards St Paul's. That way you see the merchant house before the busy midday hours.
