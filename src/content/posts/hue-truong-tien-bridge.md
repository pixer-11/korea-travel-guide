## Six spans and twelve arches

Count the arches from the riverbank. The bridge rests on six spans, and each one curves into two shallow steel arcs, so you get twelve humps of riveted truss. That rhythm is why a Hue folk song calls it "six spans, twelve arches". Locals still hum the line.

It is called Cầu Trường Tiền in Vietnamese, and it stretches a little over 400 metres across the Perfume River (Sông Hương). The walkways sit outside the trusses on both sides, so you walk with open water below and the steel lattice at your shoulder. Motorbikes stream through the centre lane, and their hum carries through the deck.

## A bridge that kept being rebuilt

The structure went up in the late 1890s under Emperor Thành Thái, in the years when French engineering was arriving in the imperial capital. It is often linked to Gustave Eiffel's firm. That attribution is popular rather than settled, so treat it as local lore.

Since then it has lost spans more than once. A major typhoon in 1904 twisted the steel, and fighting during the war years, especially in 1968, dropped sections into the river. The name changed too, from Thành Thái to Clemenceau and back again. The present name refers to the royal mint (trường tiền) that once stood near the northern end.

## Walking it properly

Use the footpaths only. The central roadway belongs to motorbikes, and nobody slows for a pedestrian standing in it to take a photo.

- **Pick your side:** the downstream (east) walkway faces the new town's riverside parks. The upstream (west) side looks towards the Citadel and the river bend beyond it.
- **Stopping:** step against the outer rail, because the paths are narrow and cyclists use them too.
- **Crossing the road at either end:** walk at a steady, predictable pace and let traffic flow around you. Don't dart or freeze.
- **Getting there:** most visitors arrive on foot from the Lê Lợi riverside hotels or by Grab bike or taxi. Ask for "Cầu Trường Tiền".

This is still a working city bridge rather than a ticketed sight. You won't find a gate, a fee or an official visiting window, just a steady flow of commuters.

## When the lights come on

Dusk is the reason most people come back for a second crossing. After dark, LEDs run along the arches and cycle through colours, and the reflection stretches across the slow water. The best view of all twelve arches together is from the south bank, not from the bridge itself.

Walk down to the riverside park along Lê Lợi street and look upstream. Dragon boats tie up along this stretch. In the evenings some take passengers out to hear ca Huế, the court-derived folk singing of the region. Prices are agreed at the wharf, so ask the boat crew directly.

## What waits at each end

The two banks feel like different cities. The south end opens onto Lê Lợi, with hotels, the riverside gardens and the backpacker streets around Phạm Ngũ Lão and Nguyễn Đình Chiểu a few minutes' walk away.

The north end puts you right beside Đông Ba Market. It is Hue's largest market, a crowded maze of fabric, conical hats, dried goods and food stalls. Keep going west along the river and you reach the walls of the Citadel. The Ngọ Môn gate of the Imperial City is roughly a 20-minute walk from the bridge.

## Fitting it into a Hue day

You don't need a separate trip for Truong Tien Bridge. Use it as the hinge between a morning in the Citadel and an evening on the south bank. Cross northbound to reach Đông Ba Market in the morning, when it is most active. Then cross back southbound in the late afternoon.

If you plan a boat trip upriver to Thiên Mụ Pagoda, the departures along the south bank put the bridge in your first view from the water. For photographs, try the riverside steps below the south end just after sunset, before full darkness flattens the sky behind the steel.
