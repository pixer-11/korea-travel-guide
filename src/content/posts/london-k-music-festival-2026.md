## Eight weeks of Korean sound

This festival doesn't fit into one weekend. K-Music Festival 2026 runs from September 28 to November 23, 2026, so it covers most of London's autumn. Concerts are scattered across the calendar and across the city, which means you can catch one show or plan several evenings around it.

The Korean Cultural Centre UK organises it every year. Its programme puts Korean contemporary acts and traditional musicians side by side. On a single night a hall might hear plucked zither strings, then electronics, then a voice that has trained for years to sound deliberately rough.

## What the programme covers

There are two strands, and the festival's interest lies in how they meet. One is contemporary music: Korean artists working in jazz, experimental, electronic, folk and crossover styles. The other is *gugak*, the umbrella term for Korean traditional music.

These are some of the gugak sounds the festival is built to showcase:

- **Gayageum**: a long zither with silk strings, plucked and pressed to bend notes
- **Haegeum**: a two-string fiddle held upright on the knee, with a reedy, almost vocal tone
- **Janggu**: an hourglass drum struck on both heads, the backbone of most ensembles
- **Pansori**: epic sung storytelling for a single vocalist and one drummer, often lasting a long stretch without a break

Many contemporary acts borrow from these instruments. Hearing both strands across a few nights shows you where the newer sound comes from. The full 2026 lineup, with dates for each act, is on the organiser's official site.

## Venues across London

The festival isn't tied to one stage. It uses multiple London venues, matching each act to a room of the right size. A solo gayageum recital needs a quiet, close space. A large percussion ensemble needs a full concert hall.

That makes the venue the first thing to look at on each listing. London's concert halls are spread across Zone 1 and beyond, so two festival nights can end up on opposite sides of the river. Each venue is named on that concert's listing on the organiser's site.

The organiser's own base is on the Strand at Grand Buildings, 1–3 Strand. It faces Trafalgar Square, a few minutes' walk from Charing Cross station (Northern and Bakerloo lines, plus National Rail) and Embankment.

## Tickets and getting around

Ticket links for each concert sit on the Korean Cultural Centre UK's official site, alongside the dates and venues. Because the shows are at different venues, the ticketing system can change from one concert to the next. Many London halls send e-tickets that you scan on your phone at the door.

Most festival-goers travel by Tube and pay by tapping a contactless bank card or phone at the barriers, the same as an Oyster card. Late in November the evenings are dark and cold by early evening. Regulars treat the walk from the station as part of the night, not a chore.

## In the hall with a gugak audience

Korean traditional concerts have their own etiquette, and it can surprise a London audience. In pansori especially, listeners call out short shouts of encouragement during the performance. This is called *chuimsae*, and the best-known cries are *"eolsu!"* and *"jota!"* ("good!"). Shouts come at the end of a phrase or after a high point. They aren't heckling: the singer expects them.

Some other things that are true of most nights:

- Applause between pieces is normal, but sanjo (a long improvised solo form) moves through its sections without stopping. Audiences usually wait for the final stroke.
- Bars at London venues almost always take card and contactless payment. Tipping at the bar isn't expected.
- Contemporary sets work like any gig: phones come out briefly, then go away.

If you can't tell whether a moment invites a shout, watch the Korean audience members around you. They usually set the pace.

## Building an evening around it

Most festival venues sit in parts of London that are busy after dark, so a concert is easy to fold into a longer night out. Around the Strand, Covent Garden and Soho are both a short walk north. Soho has the city's densest cluster of Korean restaurants, which is handy for dinner before a show.

If you're heading to a South Bank concert, the riverside walk across Hungerford Bridge from Embankment is a classic way in. With roughly eight weeks of programming, plenty of attendees pick one traditional night and one contemporary night. Hearing the two strands back to back is what the festival is designed for.
