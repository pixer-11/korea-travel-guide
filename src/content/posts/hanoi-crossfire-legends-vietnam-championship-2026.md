## A Monday final in Hanoi

The date is fixed: Monday, December 14, 2026. The CrossFire Legends Vietnam Championship 2026 is a national esports tournament held in Hanoi, and it pulls competitive gaming fans in from across the region.

CrossFire has a long history in Vietnam. The PC original, the Smilegate shooter sold locally as Đột Kích, was a fixture of the country's internet cafés for years. CrossFire Legends carries that tactical first-person shooting onto phones. For many Vietnamese fans in the crowd, this is the game they grew up on, rebuilt for the device in their pocket.

## Inside a national shooter final

Esports finals follow a familiar layout. Players sit in rows at the front of the stage wearing headsets, facing each other or the audience. Above them, a giant screen carries the spectator feed: kill feeds, minimaps, scoreboards between rounds.

The casting is in Vietnamese and moves fast, especially in close rounds. Seasoned fans read the screen as much as they listen. Round wins draw a jump in volume, and a clutch play from a single surviving player brings people out of their seats. Phones come up every few minutes to film it.

A first-time visitor who does not speak Vietnamese can still follow along. In a shooter, the score line and the shrinking number of players left alive tell most of the story.

## Getting into Hanoi and across the city

Most international visitors fly into Noi Bai International Airport, north of the Red River. The ride into central Hanoi usually takes 45 minutes to an hour by taxi or Grab, depending on traffic. Airport express buses also run into the Old Quarter.

Inside the city, Grab cars and Grab motorbikes cover nearly every trip. After an event lets out, hundreds of people open the same app at once, so many fans walk a block or two away from the doors before calling a ride. Hanoi's elevated metro lines serve a few corridors, but most event-goers still travel by road.

In mid-December Hanoi is cool and mostly dry. Evenings regularly drop into the mid-teens Celsius, so locals wear light jackets after dark.

## Cheering, paying and eating with the crowd

A few habits make the day go smoothly.

- **Payment:** Vietnamese fans pay for most things by bank QR code or e-wallet. Street vendors near any venue still prefer cash in đồng, so visitors usually carry small notes.
- **Cheering:** Team chants and handmade banners are normal. People stand for big moments and sit again quickly so the rows behind can see.
- **Merch and photos:** Players often meet fans after matches. People usually wait for staff to set up a signing line instead of crowding the stage edge.
- **Food:** Vendors on plastic stools near large venues sell bánh mì, xôi (sticky rice) and sugarcane juice. Fans often grab a meal between matches rather than queuing at a concession stand.
- **Language:** "Cảm ơn" (thank you) and "bao nhiêu?" (how much?) get you through most street purchases.

Hanoi has no tipping culture at street stalls. Rounding up a ride or leaving change at a sit-down restaurant is appreciated but not expected.

## Turning the trip into a Hanoi weekend

Since the final falls on a Monday, many travelling fans arrive over the weekend before. Stays around Hoan Kiem Lake and the Old Quarter put you within walking distance of the city's best street food. Most event-goers then reach the venue by Grab.

On the Friday, Saturday and Sunday evenings beforehand, the streets around Hoan Kiem Lake usually close to traffic for the walking-street nights. Families, buskers and street games take over the roads. The Temple of Literature, the Ho Chi Minh Mausoleum complex and the bia hơi (fresh draught beer) corners of Ta Hien street fill out the rest of a short visit.

For a second dose of gaming, PC cafés across the city still run CrossFire. Sitting down at one shows you where the Vietnamese scene started.
