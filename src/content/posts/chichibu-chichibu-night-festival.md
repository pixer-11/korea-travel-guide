## Lanterns Against a December Sky

Picture a wooden shrine on wheels, taller than the shopfronts it passes. It is lacquered and carved, hung with paper lanterns, and dragged on thick ropes by dozens of people in matching festival coats. Inside, drummers keep up the hammering yatai-bayashi rhythm while men balanced on the roof shout the pullers on.

That is the Chichibu Night Festival, or Chichibu Yomatsuri. It ranks alongside Kyoto's Gion Matsuri and Takayama's festival as one of Japan's three great float festivals. The difference is the season and the hill. Here the floats go up a steep slope in winter darkness while fireworks burst overhead.

## Dates for the 2026 Edition

The festival runs from December 2 to December 3, 2026. The first day is the eve, called yoimiya. Floats roll through town and the streets fill, but the scale is smaller.

December 3 is the main day. After dark the full procession sets off from Chichibu Shrine toward the hill. The fireworks go up over the town during the climb, so the light from above and the lanterns below arrive together. Most visitors who can only come once come on the 3rd.

## Shrine, Streets and Dango-zaka

The whole festival happens in central Chichibu, a compact town in a basin ringed by mountains in western Saitama. Everything you'll want is within walking distance:

- **Chichibu Shrine**: the starting point, with its carved and painted halls.
- **The old shopping streets**: the floats are pulled through these, and food stalls line them.
- **Dango-zaka**: the short, sharp slope that is the festival's climax.
- **Hitsujiyama Park**: higher ground east of the centre, popular for watching the fireworks.

The climb up Dango-zaka is the moment everyone waits for. Each float weighs many tonnes, and the pullers haul it up the incline in surges while the crowd roars and the drums speed up. The floats then rest at the otabisho, the temporary home of the shrine's deity for the night.

## Tickets and Where People Watch

The festival itself is a street event. Most people watch from the roadside along the procession route, with no ticket involved. Around the busiest stretches, the organiser sets up seating areas and controls access, and its official festival site is where seating details live.

Spots near Dango-zaka are the most sought after and fill first. People who skip the slope often watch from the shopping streets, where the floats pass at arm's length. Others head up to Hitsujiyama Park for a wide view of the fireworks over the rooftops.

## From Ikebukuro to the Mountains

Chichibu is about two hours from Ikebukuro on the Seibu line, ending at Seibu-Chichibu Station. From there it is a short walk into the centre. The Chichibu Railway's Chichibu Station and Ohanabatake Station sit even closer to the shrine.

Seibu runs limited express services, and seats on these are what festival-goers chase on the main day. Ordinary trains do the trip too, with a change along the way. Late in the evening the return trains to Tokyo are packed, so many visitors stay overnight in Chichibu or nearby Nagatoro instead.

## Lasting the Night on Dango-zaka

Early December nights in the Chichibu basin are properly cold. Standing still for hours is colder still. Regulars come dressed for it:

- Thermal layers under a heavy coat
- Hat, gloves and a scarf
- Disposable heat packs (kairo), sold at every convenience store
- Shoes with grip for the sloped, crowded streets

Police and festival staff run one-way crowd flows near the slope. Once a route is closed you follow the direction you are sent, even if your hotel is the other way. Pushing against the flow is the quickest way to annoy everyone around you.

The food stalls are a big part of the night: yakisoba, grilled skewers, and amazake (sweet sake) in paper cups. Many stalls are cash-only, so locals carry coins and small notes. Give the rope-pullers room, and never step between a float and its ropes. Those floats do not stop quickly.
