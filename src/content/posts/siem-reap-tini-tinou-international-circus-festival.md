## When the circus spills onto the street

For one week, the festival planned to take circus in Siem Reap out from under the canvas. Acrobats, jugglers and clowns from more than 10 countries joined the line-up alongside the Cambodian performers who work here year-round. The lineup announced over 100 artists. Some shows were ticketed, and the parades and street performances were free and in public.

Tini Tinou is ASEAN's only biennial international circus festival, so it comes round every other year. The 2026 edition was set for November 17 to 24. Siem Reap shared the programme with Battambang, the river town to the west where much of Cambodia's modern circus tradition was trained.

## Dates and the two-city split

The festival was planned across eight days, November 17–24, 2026, in two cities.

- **Siem Reap:** the base for most international visitors, close to Angkor and the airport.
- **Battambang:** Cambodia's second-largest city, roughly three to four hours west by road.

The festival is rooted in Battambang. It grew out of Phare Ponleu Selpak, the arts school and NGO there that trains young Cambodians in circus, music and visual art. Festival visitors often stay in one city. Others split their nights between the two to take in both halves of the programme.

## The free half: parades and street shows

The free events are what make Tini Tinou feel like a festival. The programme set parades and street shows alongside the ticketed performances, and a visiting troupe can turn a pavement into a stage for twenty minutes.

Shows like these suit families and anyone in town for the temples without an evening booked around a performance. Street audiences in Cambodia stand close, children push to the front, and nobody minds if someone drifts in halfway through. Small notes are handy for tipping street performers. Tips are welcome, though not required.

## Tickets, venues and the programme

Ticketed shows were spread across venues in Siem Reap and Battambang. Phare Ponleu Selpak, the Battambang school, was behind the programme, and the free street events filled the gaps between paid performances.

Siem Reap's year-round circus is Phare, The Cambodian Circus. It performs in a big top in town, and its artists are graduates of the Battambang school. The festival brings visiting companies into that same world. The programme centred on contemporary circus that tells stories through acrobatics, physical theatre and live music, rather than a traditional animal circus.

## Watching a show in Siem Reap

Siem Reap is small, and the way people get around shapes the evening.

- **Getting there:** most visitors take a tuk-tuk, either hailed on the street or booked through PassApp or Grab, which both work in Siem Reap. Fares for hailed tuk-tuks are agreed before setting off.
- **Paying:** US dollars and Cambodian riel both circulate. Small change often comes back in riel, so a mix of the two is normal.
- **Seating:** audiences at circus shows here are relaxed. Front-row seats at ticketed shows usually go to whoever comes first.
- **Etiquette:** applause mid-act is normal and the performers feed off it. Most companies don't allow flash photography during acts, for the artists' safety.

After a show, Pub Street and the Old Market (Psar Chas) are a short ride away, and that's where most of the late-evening food and drink is.

## Making the trip to Battambang

Battambang is a slower, quieter city than Siem Reap. Its riverside is lined with French-era shophouses, and the Sangkae River runs through the middle. Phare Ponleu Selpak's campus sits on the outskirts, which makes this the festival's home ground.

Buses and shared taxis run between the two cities along the main road, and the trip takes roughly three to four hours. A scenic boat route also runs across the Tonle Sap and up the Sangkae, but it is much slower and depends on water levels. Visitors who cover both cities usually travel by day and reach their evening show rested.

Battambang's other well-known sights fill the daylight hours:

- the bamboo train
- Phnom Sampeau and its bat cave
- Wat Ek Phnom

## Fitting it around Angkor

Mid-to-late November is a good time to be in Siem Reap. The rainy season is ending, the countryside is still green and the moats around the temples are full. The cooler, drier months are beginning, so evenings outdoors are pleasant for street shows.

A festival week fits easily around a temple pass. Visitors usually spend mornings at Angkor Wat, Bayon and Ta Prohm, rest through the hottest part of the day, and keep the evening for circus. Temples in the morning and a show at night is the usual rhythm.

The festival's purpose matters too. The Cambodian circus came out of a project to give young people a trade and a voice through the arts. Its graduates sharing a programme with international companies, in their own country, is the heart of what Tini Tinou is about.
