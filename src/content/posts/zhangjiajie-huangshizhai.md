## A table of rock above the pillars

Huangshizhai is a mesa, not a peak. Its top is a broad, wooded plateau with a footpath running around the rim. Railed viewing platforms stick out from the path over a drop full of quartz-sandstone columns.

The columns rise from the valley floor in rows, with pines clinging to their ledges. On damp days, cloud settles between them and only the tops show. That cloud is the scene the place is known for, and it comes and goes within minutes.

The name means "Yellow Stone Fortress." Legend links it to Zhang Liang, the Han dynasty strategist, and his teacher Huang Shigong, the Yellow Stone Elder. A Chinese saying puts it bluntly: come to Zhangjiajie without reaching Huangshizhai and you haven't really come.

## Getting to the trailhead

Huangshizhai sits in the southern part of the park, close to the main Zhangjiajie Forest Park entrance. That gate is outside the small town of the same name, separate from Wulingyuan's main tourist strip.

- **From Zhangjiajie city:** buses run from the central bus station to the Forest Park gate. Allow about an hour.
- **From Zhangjiajie West station (high-speed rail):** take a taxi or the bus link toward the Forest Park area.
- **Inside the gate:** follow the signs for the Huangshizhai cable car, or for the footpath that starts nearby.

One park ticket covers the core scenic area, and it is usually valid for several days. The cable car is ticketed separately. Current prices are posted at the gate and on the park's official site.

## Up by cable, down on foot

The cable car lifts you onto the plateau in a few minutes. On the way, the cabin rises past the cliff faces you'll be looking down on later.

The footpath is the other way up. It is thousands of uneven stone steps through forest, and most fit walkers need a couple of hours to climb it. Going down is kinder on the lungs but hard on the knees.

The most common plan is to ride up and walk down. You save your legs for the rim loop and still get the forest on the way out. Plan on three to four hours for the whole visit, plus time spent waiting for cloud to clear from a viewpoint.

## Walking the rim loop

The summit path is a loop, so you can go either way and end up back at the cable car. Signed platforms mark the main views. Among the best known are Liuqi Pavilion, a tiered pavilion near the high point, and Zhaixing Tai, the "Star-Picking Terrace."

Between platforms, the path runs under trees and opens onto the edge without warning. The rails are solid, but the ground can be slick after rain. The plateau top is fairly gentle, so most of the effort is the stretch from the trailhead up.

## Reading the gate, the stairs and the monkeys

A few habits make the day easier:

- **Carry your passport.** At the gate, foreign visitors' tickets are tied to their passport, often with a fingerprint scan. Expect to show both again at re-entry points.
- **Pay by phone.** Alipay and WeChat Pay are standard at kiosks and the cable car. Both now take foreign cards, so set one up before you arrive.
- **Ignore the sedan chairs unless you need them.** Bearers wait on the stair route offering lifts. Agree terms before you sit down, or wave them off politely.
- **Keep food out of sight.** Rhesus macaques work the trails here and will grab a dangling bag. Don't feed them.
- **Go early in the day.** Tour groups tend to hit the cable car once the coaches arrive from town. Getting there near opening usually means a shorter queue.

Opening hours change through the year, so check the park's official site for this month's times before you go.

## Pairing it with the rest of the park

Golden Whip Stream (Jinbian Xi) starts near the same Forest Park gate. It's a flat, shaded walk along the water below the pillars, a good contrast after a morning spent looking down on them.

Yuanjiajie, with its Avatar-associated scenery and the Bailong Elevator, is further north in the park, and so is Tianzi Mountain. They're best saved for another day on the same multi-day ticket. Huangshizhai is the quieter option of the three, and the place to see the columns without shoulder-to-shoulder crowds.
