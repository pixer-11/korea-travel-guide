## Eight days of tackle football at altitude

Canada and Mexico meet on Mexican soil. The 2026 Gridiron Nations Championship is an international tackle football competition, and it runs in Mexico City from December 12 to 19, 2026.

The thin air is the thing visiting teams talk about most. Mexico City sits at roughly 2,240 metres, which makes conditioning harder and keeps kicked balls in the air longer. December is also the heart of the dry season. Afternoons are usually clear and mild, and the temperature drops quickly once the sun goes down.

## A football city that is not only about soccer

Soccer gets the headlines, but American football has deep roots in Mexico City. University football is the core of it. The Pumas of UNAM play at Estadio Olímpico Universitario in Ciudad Universitaria, and their "¡Goya!" chant is known well beyond the campus.

Some useful background:

- **Estadio Azteca** hosted the first NFL regular-season game played outside the United States in 2005, and more NFL games after that.
- **ONEFA** is the long-running college league that feeds Mexico's national team.
- **The LFA**, Mexico's professional league, has had Mexico City teams since it started in 2016.
- **Canadian football** is played 12-a-side with three downs, so Canada's players come from a game that differs from the American version.

## Getting around in mid-December

The tournament opens on December 12, which is the feast of the Virgin of Guadalupe. Millions of pilgrims head to the Basílica de Guadalupe in the north of the city around that date. The Metro stations near it, La Villa-Basílica on Line 6 and Basílica on Line 3, are packed, and the roads around them close.

From December 16 the city also starts its posadas, the nine nights of Christmas parties that run up to Christmas Eve. Evening traffic gets heavier as a result. Getting around usually works like this:

- **Metro and Metrobús** for long trips across the city, paid with the rechargeable Movilidad Integrada card.
- **Uber and DiDi** for late nights after a game, since both are widely used.
- **Walking** around Roma, Condesa and Coyoacán, where the streets are flat and shaded.

## Game day in the Mexican stands

Mexican football crowds are loud and social. Vendors walk up and down the aisles selling beer, which people call *chelas*, along with snacks. Fans usually pay them in cash with small notes, and a few coins on top is a normal thank-you.

At the gates, security pat-downs are standard. Stadiums in Mexico commonly bar large bags, umbrellas and anything made of glass. Most fans carry just a phone, ID and a jacket. Inside the stands, fans often sing along with chants they have never heard before, and crowds tend to welcome the visiting fans.

Around town, cards are accepted in most restaurants. Tipping 10–15% is the normal practice. Street stalls and markets still work mostly in cash. A few phrases go a long way: "¿Dónde está la entrada?" (where is the entrance?) and "una más, por favor" (one more, please).

## Where fans usually stay

Most visitors base themselves in **Roma Norte** or **Condesa**. Both are leafy neighbourhoods with plenty of taquerías and bars, and both are close to Metrobús Line 1 on Avenida Insurgentes. **Coyoacán** in the south is calmer and close to Ciudad Universitaria. **Polanco** is the pricier choice, next to Bosque de Chapultepec and the Museo Nacional de Antropología.

On days without a game, fans often go to the murals at the Palacio Nacional in the Zócalo or take a trajinera boat ride at Xochimilco. December evenings also bring Christmas lights and seasonal stalls to many neighbourhood squares.
