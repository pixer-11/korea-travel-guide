## Sand, Camels and a Holy Lake

Pushkar does two things at once in November. On the dunes west of town, herders from across Rajasthan camp with their camels, horses and cattle. Their animals are trimmed, painted and hung with beads for trading. A short walk east, pilgrims fill the 52 ghats around Pushkar Lake.

The 2026 fair was set for November 17-24, closing on Kartik Purnima, the full moon of the Hindu month of Kartik. That is the most auspicious night to bathe in the lake, and the busiest one in town.

## How the Week Unfolds

The calendar of the fair moves from trade to faith.

- **Opening days:** the livestock trading is usually at its peak. Herders bargain between tents of patterned cloth, and the dunes are packed with animals.
- **Middle days:** the official programme on the mela ground takes over. Rajasthan Tourism has a history of staging camel races, moustache contests, turban-tying and folk music here.
- **Final days:** the crowds grow as pilgrims come for the Kartik Purnima dip at the lake.

Most visitors find two or three days enough. The start of the fair is the time for working camels rather than a show.

## Getting to Pushkar

Pushkar is a small town in the Aravalli hills. Nothing reaches the centre directly except road.

- **Rail:** Ajmer Junction, about 15 km away, has the best connections from Delhi and Jaipur. Taxis and buses cross the Nag Pahar ridge to Pushkar. Pushkar's own station has only a few trains.
- **Air:** Kishangarh Airport is the closest. Jaipur International Airport, about three hours by road, has many more flights.
- **On arrival:** the centre is a tangle of lanes around the lake. Cars stop on the edge of town, and the last stretch to the ghats and mela ground is on foot.

Accommodation fills fast for fair week. For 2026, RTDC and private operators planned tent camps near the grounds. The mela ground is open public space, and paid stands and camps post their own prices.

## Moving Through a Holy Town

Pushkar is a pilgrimage town first. Its rules apply on every day of the fair.

- **Food and drink:** meat and alcohol are banned within town limits, and most menus also leave out eggs. Rajasthani thalis, malpua and lassi are the standard fare around the market.
- **At the ghats:** shoes come off before stepping down to the water. Photographing bathers is not allowed, and on Kartik Purnima cameras stay away entirely.
- **The flower offer:** men on the ghats may hand visitors petals and start a puja. They then ask for a "donation" for the visitor's family. Refusing the flowers politely before anything starts avoids this.
- **Photographing herders:** asking first is customary. Some herders ask for a small tip in return.
- **Paying:** UPI and cards work in hotels and bigger restaurants. Herders, chai stalls and camel-cart drivers deal in cash.

Brahma Temple, one of the few in India dedicated to Brahma, sits a few lanes from the lake. A ropeway climbs to Savitri Temple on Ratnagiri hill, which looks down on the dunes where the fair's tents are pitched.
