Stand on the Plaza de Armas in Iquitos and look for the building that doesn't fit. It sits among plaster, stucco and tiled facades, and it is made of metal sheets held together with rows of bolts. That's Casa de Fierro, the Iron House. It's a kit building that crossed an ocean and then travelled up the Amazon, and today it stands at the corner of Jirón Putumayo.

The square around it is shaded by trees. Mototaxis loop past constantly. Most visitors photograph the cathedral and walk straight past the iron house, which is a big part of why it still feels like a quiet find.

## A house that arrived in pieces

Casa de Fierro dates from the rubber boom of the late 19th century. Rubber made Iquitos rich, and the barons spent their money on European status symbols. One of those was a prefabricated iron building, bought in Europe, broken down into panels and shipped across the Atlantic. It then went up the Amazon to a city that no road has ever reached.

The usual version of the story is that it was bought at the 1889 Paris Exposition and put together again in Iquitos. Whatever its exact route, the building is a physical record of how much money moved through this river port when rubber was king. Its owners were willing to import a whole house rather than build with what grew around them.

## The Eiffel question

The building's fame rests on one name: Gustave Eiffel, the engineer behind the Paris tower. Signs, guidebooks and local tellings credit the design to his workshop, and that attribution is part of the appeal. It's also worth knowing that historians have long debated it. No firm paper trail ties Eiffel personally to this house, and other prefabricated iron buildings of the era carry similar stories.

So treat the Eiffel connection as the legend the building lives with rather than settled fact. The real interest lies elsewhere anyway. This is an industrial European product, designed for quick assembly, put up on a jungle riverbank. Seeing that up close is what you came for.

## Reading the iron facade

Give the exterior some time before you go in. Here is what to look for:

- **The panels:** flat iron sheets fitted edge to edge, with the joins left in plain view.
- **The bolt lines:** rows of fastenings running along every seam, the clearest sign of a building designed to be taken apart and put back together.
- **The two levels:** a ground floor at street height and an upper storey wrapped by a balcony that looks onto the plaza.
- **The contrast:** compare it with the stone and plaster buildings around the square, then think about what metal does in Amazon heat.

That last point matters. Iquitos is hot and humid all year, and a metal skin is a strange choice for that climate. It tells you the house was bought as a statement, not for comfort.

## Eating inside the Iron House

Today the building is a restaurant, so seeing the interior usually means sitting down for a meal or a drink. At the moment it opens Monday to Saturday from 11am to 10pm. It is closed on Sundays, so a weekend visitor should come on Saturday.

Lunch is a natural fit with a morning spent around the plaza and the riverfront. An early evening table works too, once the worst of the heat has passed. Menus change and this guide hasn't been given current dishes or prices. Check the posted menu at the door before you sit down, and don't expect a museum. Be clear that you're coming for the building and the view of the square, then judge the food on its own merits.

## Getting a table and paying

The building is open to the street, so walking up and asking for a table is the normal approach. Here's what helps:

- **Say what you want:** "una mesa para dos" gets you seated. If you'd like the balcony side, point and ask "¿arriba?" (upstairs?).
- **Carry soles in cash:** cash is common across central Iquitos. Ask before ordering whether cards are accepted that day rather than assuming.
- **Tipping:** at sit-down restaurants in Peru, many diners leave roughly ten percent when service isn't already on the bill. Check the receipt first.
- **Photos:** shooting the facade from the plaza is fine at any time. Inside, you're in a working dining room, so be considerate of other tables.

Getting there is easy if you're staying downtown. Most central hotels are a short walk from the Plaza de Armas. From further out, flag a mototaxi and say "Plaza de Armas, Casa de Fierro". Agree the fare before you climb in, as there are no meters.

## The few blocks around it

Casa de Fierro works best as the first stop on a walk, not a trip of its own. Within a few minutes on foot you'll find:

- **Iglesia Matriz:** the cathedral of Iquitos, on the same plaza, with its clock tower.
- **Malecón Tarapacá:** the riverside promenade one block away, looking out over the Itaya and the Amazon floodplain.
- **The tiled mansions:** rubber-era houses along and near the malecón, faced with imported ceramic azulejos. They make a good comparison with the iron house, since both show what the boom bought.
- **Belén:** the market district south of the centre, reached by a longer walk or a short mototaxi ride. It's noisy and crowded, and it has none of the tidiness of the plaza.

Leave the iron house and walk to the malecón, then follow the tiled facades south. That loop takes an unhurried hour or two and covers the main story of rubber-boom Iquitos.

## When to stand in the plaza

The hours are short at both ends of the day. In the mornings the plaza is under the trees and the light falls on the facades. Late afternoon is cooler and suits an unhurried look at the metalwork. Then you can drift down to the malecón as the river turns gold.

If you only want photos, the exterior is there whenever you pass, Sundays included. If you want to go inside, keep to the restaurant's opening times and skip Sunday entirely. Either way, check the hours shortly before you go, as they can change.
