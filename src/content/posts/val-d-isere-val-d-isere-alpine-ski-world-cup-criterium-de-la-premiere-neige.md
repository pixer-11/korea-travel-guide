## A race that ends in the village

The finish line is in the middle of town. At most World Cup venues you need a lift or a shuttle to reach the race. In Val d'Isère the Face de Bellevarde drops straight down toward the village, and the finish arena sits at the bottom where the resort meets the slope. Racers come out of the last steep pitch, cross the line and stop a few hundred metres from the bakeries and ski shops.

This year the men race on December 12 and 13, 2026: one weekend, two technical disciplines. Giant slalom and slalom are both decided over two runs, called *manches* in French. The combined time sets the result, which is why the crowd stays put through the gap between runs.

## Why the "first snow" race matters

The name means "Criterium of the First Snow". The race dates back to the 1950s, when Val d'Isère began opening the European winter with an early-December competition. It has been a fixture of the circuit ever since and is one of the oldest names in Alpine racing.

The Face de Bellevarde was built for the men's downhill at the 1992 Albertville Winter Olympics. It is steep and sits in shade for much of the day, and early-season snow on it is often hard and icy. Technical skiers rate it as one of the toughest early-winter tests. December results here usually give the first reliable read on who is in form for the season.

## Getting to Val d'Isère

The resort is at the far end of the Tarentaise valley, close to the Italian border. There is one road in, and it ends here.

- **By train:** Bourg-Saint-Maurice is the closest railhead. From there it is roughly 30 km by road, past Sainte-Foy and the Lac du Chevril dam below Tignes.
- **By air:** Geneva, Lyon Saint-Exupéry and Chambéry are the usual airports, and transfer coaches run from them during the season.
- **By car:** the D902 climbs from Bourg-Saint-Maurice. Mountain rules apply in winter: snow chains or winter tyres are required on this road when conditions call for them.

On race weekend the whole village fills up. Visitors usually stay in Val d'Isère itself or in La Daille, a short shuttle ride down the valley. Some stay in Tignes and drive or take a bus over.

## Standing in the finish arena

The finish area is a wide snow bowl with grandstand seating and standing room behind the barriers. A big screen shows the top of the course, so you can follow each racer all the way down. The leader's box sits right by the line, and the reaction when a time drops to first is a large part of the atmosphere.

Ticket arrangements, grandstand access and spectator zones are set out on the event's official site and by the Val d'Isère tourist office. No prices are given here, so use those sources for current details.

Bring proper mountain clothing. In mid-December the valley floor sits at about 1,850 m, and standing still in the shadow of the Face gets cold quickly. Insulated boots matter more than a ski jacket.

## Watching the way regulars do

Experienced fans treat race day as a ski day with a show in the middle. Many watch the first run from the finish, ski the open pistes during the break and come back down for the second run. The Olympique gondola leaves from the village and goes up to Bellevarde, which makes that loop easy.

- **Make noise:** cowbells, flags and horns are normal along the barriers, and French racers get the loudest welcome.
- **Know the words:** the start is *le départ*, the finish is *l'arrivée*, and *première manche* / *deuxième manche* are the first and second runs.
- **Food:** fans typically eat at the stands near the finish or in village cafés during the break between runs. Seats are scarce at the peak of lunchtime.
- **Paying:** cards are widely accepted in the resort. Small cash is handy for stands that set up only for the weekend.

Plenty of spectators also watch from the side of the course partway up the Face. Where you can stand depends on the course marshals and the safety netting, so follow their lead. Never cross a race fence.

## Making a weekend of it

The race usually falls on one of the first open-skiing weekends of the season. The Espace Killy area, shared with Tignes, gives you plenty of terrain between runs and on the days either side of the race.

In the evening the main street through the village becomes the centre of the weekend. Après-ski bars and restaurants fill with fans, and in recent years the event has brought some racers into public prize-giving ceremonies in the village. If you want a quiet corner, the old church of Saint-Bernard-de-Menthon and the stone chalets of the old village are only a few minutes' walk from the finish.
