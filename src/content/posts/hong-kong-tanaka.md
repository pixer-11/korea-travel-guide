Wellington Street in Central is a street of contrasts: dai pai dong smoke drifting past glass-fronted wine bars, a century-old noodle shop a few doors from a private members' club. TANAKA sits inside that mix almost anonymously, its eight seats making it one of the smallest serious dining rooms in the district.

## What makes TANAKA different

This is not a general Japanese restaurant that happens to serve beef. TANAKA is built around a single ingredient: Matsusaka beef from virgin female Japanese black cattle, a designation prized in Japan for its marbling and considered rarer than standard Wagyu grades.

Chef Satoru trained in Tokyo before bringing that singular focus to Hong Kong. The format is omakase, meaning you don't order so much as receive a sequence the chef has decided on, course by course, cut by cut.

## The eight-seat counter

Eight seats is the entire restaurant. There's no overflow room, no bar to wait at, and almost certainly no walk-in availability on a given night.

That scale is the point. A counter this small lets the chef work each cut in front of you, adjusting sear and thickness seat by seat rather than firing plates for a full dining room at once.

- Book ahead, ideally days rather than hours in advance
- Expect a fixed or near-fixed omakase sequence rather than an à la carte menu
- Arrive on time; a room this size runs on a tight, sequential rhythm

## Reading a Matsusaka omakase

If you haven't eaten Matsusaka beef before, it's worth knowing what sets it apart before you sit down. The designation requires virgin female cattle, raised under strict feeding regimes in Japan's Mie Prefecture, which producers say results in exceptionally fine, evenly distributed marbling.

At an omakase counter like TANAKA's, that marbling is usually shown off across several different cuts and cooking methods in one sitting, sometimes seared, sometimes barely touched by heat, so you can taste how the fat behaves differently cut to cut. Go in expecting a tasting of contrasts rather than one showstopping steak.

## Finding it on Wellington Street

Wellington Street runs through the heart of Central, one of Hong Kong Island's oldest commercial streets and now a dense strip of restaurants and bars. The nearest MTR stop is Central Station, served by the Island and Tsuen Wan lines, with several exits feeding directly into the surrounding Central grid.

From most Central exits it's a short uphill walk. Because the restaurant is small and easy to miss among the street's larger, more brightly lit frontages, it's worth having the exact address ready on a map rather than hunting by eye.

## Booking a table at an eight-seat room

With only eight seats, TANAKA behaves more like a private booking than a drop-in restaurant. Reservations are essential, and given how quickly small omakase counters in Central tend to fill once word spreads, booking further out rather than closer to your date is the safer approach.

Confirm current hours, seating times and pricing directly with the restaurant before you finalize plans, since details at newly opened, small-format restaurants change faster than published guides can track. If a booking platform lists availability, treat the listed times as a starting point to verify rather than a guarantee.

## Counter etiquette for a chef-led sequence

Omakase counters run on a rhythm set by the chef, not the diner, and that's especially true in a room this small. A few habits make the meal work better for everyone at the counter:

- Eat each course as it's served rather than letting pieces sit, since beef temperature and sear are timed deliberately
- Keep strong perfume or cologne to a minimum, as it can interfere with delicate aromas at a close counter
- Ask questions about the cut or preparation between courses rather than mid-bite; chefs at counters like this are usually happy to explain what you're eating
- Follow the chef's pacing on sauces or condiments if offered, since they're often chosen to suit a specific piece of beef
