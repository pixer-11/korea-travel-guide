## Nine days, one very long glass roof

Most people come for the Christmas shopping. Artigiano in Fiera fills the halls of Fieramilano Rho-Pero from **December 5 to 13, 2026** with handmade goods from exhibitors around the world. Wool, ceramics, leather, carved wood, spices and cheeses all sit side by side, and stall after stall is run by the people who made what's on it.

The venue sets the scale. Massimiliano Fuksas designed the fair district, and a glass-and-steel canopy, nicknamed the *Vela* (sail), runs down its central spine. It rises and dips over the walkway like a frozen wave. The pavilions open off either side, each one about the size of an aircraft hangar.

## Dates, venue and tickets at a glance

Here are the basics.

- **Dates:** Saturday December 5 to Sunday December 13, 2026, which takes in two full weekends.
- **Venue:** Fieramilano Rho-Pero, in Rho, on the northwest edge of Milan.
- **Tickets and hours:** the fair's official site publishes ticket details and daily opening times.

This guide gives no prices or hours because we weren't given any. Exhibitor lists and hall maps also live on the official site. Daily times at big Italian fairs often differ between weekdays and weekends.

## Riding the red line to Rho Fieramilano

The metro is the simplest way there. Take **Line 1 (M1, the red line)** towards Rho Fieramilano and stay on to the end of the line. From central stops like Duomo or Cadorna, the ride is roughly half an hour.

One detail catches visitors out. Rho Fieramilano sits outside Milan's urban fare zone, so a standard city ticket doesn't cover it. Ticket machines sell fares to the Fiera, and staff at the turnstiles know the question well.

Regional and suburban trains also stop at **Rho Fiera** railway station, next to the complex. That's handy if you're coming from elsewhere in Lombardy or changing from the high-speed lines at Milano Centrale or Porta Garibaldi. Both stations lead straight onto the covered walkway, so you'll reach the halls without crossing a road.

## Eating your way around the world

For many regulars, the food matters as much as the crafts. The fair is known for its food halls, where regional Italian producers and international exhibitors serve hot dishes alongside jars, cured meats and sweets to take home.

Expect a mix like this:

- Italian regional food: cheeses, salumi, breads and Christmas sweets, often sold by producers from a single valley or town.
- International stalls: spices, teas and street food from the exhibiting countries.
- Seating: shared tables near the food areas. At lunch they fill up fast and turn over quickly.

People usually eat lunch either a little before or a little after the Italian midday peak. Eating at an off hour means less time standing with a tray and looking for a seat.

## The Sant'Ambrogio and Immacolata squeeze

The calendar makes this fair unusual. December 7 is the feast of **Sant'Ambrogio**, Milan's patron saint and a city holiday. December 8 is the **Immacolata**, a national public holiday. In 2026 these fall on a Monday and Tuesday, straight after the first weekend.

That turns the first stretch of the fair into a four-day holiday for most of Milan. Families, out-of-town visitors and coach groups all arrive on the same days. The second weekend, December 12–13, sees the last-minute Christmas crowd.

Wednesday to Friday, December 9–11, are ordinary working days with no holiday attached. Visitors who can pick their day usually find more room in the aisles midweek. That's reasoning from the calendar, not a measured count.

## Working the halls without losing the day

It's a long walk from end to end. People who leave happy usually go in with a rough plan.

- **Use the map.** The hall map and exhibitor list on the official site show where each country or region is. Regulars pick two or three areas they really care about first.
- **Carry less.** Coats get hot inside the pavilions, and the cloakroom or a light layer saves you from dragging a parka around. Many Milanese bring a folding shopping trolley for heavier buys like oil, wine and cheese.
- **Pay as the stall prefers.** Cards and contactless are common at larger stands, but small craft makers sometimes prefer cash. Some carry a mix of both for that reason.
- **Talk to the maker.** Asking how something was made is normal here, and often the best part of the visit. A simple *buongiorno* before you pick things up goes a long way.
- **Buy when you see it.** Pieces are often one-offs. Once you're three halls away, it's a long walk back to a stall you can't quite place.

Plan on a full day. Visitors often spend five or six hours and still miss whole pavilions.

## Pairing the fair with Milan in early December

The fair sits inside Milan's busiest week of the year. Around Sant'Ambrogio, the traditional **Oh Bej! Oh Bej!** market takes over the area around the **Castello Sforzesco**. It's a street fair of chestnuts, antiques and old Milanese stalls, with a very different feel from the halls at Rho.

December 7 is also the traditional opening night of the season at **Teatro alla Scala**, the *Prima*. It brings crowds and some evening fuss to Piazza della Scala, near the Galleria Vittorio Emanuele II and the Duomo.

Many visitors split their stay the same way. They give one day entirely to the fair, then spend the rest in the centre. Hotels near the red line, from Cadorna out towards Lotto and Molino Dorino, keep the trip to Rho a single ride with no changes.
