## Race day: 20 December 2026

The date is Sunday, 20 December 2026. That puts the Guangzhou Marathon in the closing weeks of China's road-racing season. By then much of the north has turned too cold for comfortable racing, while Guangzhou is still mild.

The field mixes Chinese club runners with international entrants. Plenty of them come up from Hong Kong and Macau, which are short train rides away. On the morning itself you get thousands of runners in matching race shirts, wide city avenues closed to traffic and spectators packed along the barriers.

The organiser publishes the start venue, course map, cut-off rules and registration details on its official race site. Those are the authoritative source for this edition. This guide covers the city around the race.

## A course built around the new city

Guangzhou's marathon has long been a showcase for Tianhe, the district of glass towers that grew up on the Pearl River's north bank. Earlier editions started and finished in that new town centre, around Huacheng Square and Haixinsha island. Runners covered long riverside stretches with the Canton Tower in view across the water.

What that means underfoot:

- **Wide, flat boulevards.** Guangzhou's central districts sit on the river plain, so you won't find much climbing.
- **Long river sections.** The Pearl River embankments give the race its open, breezy miles.
- **Skyline markers.** The Canton Tower's twisted lattice and the towers of Zhujiang New Town make easy landmarks when your legs are going.

The exact route can shift between editions. The official course map for 2026 is the one that counts.

## Getting a bib

Chinese city marathons have grown so popular that most big races allocate places by lottery, and Guangzhou has followed that model. Runners register online with their personal details, the draw takes place, and successful entrants are notified afterwards. Foreign runners normally register with their passport details.

Collecting your bib is a separate step. At major Chinese races, runners usually pick up their race pack in person at an expo in the days before the race. They bring the same ID they registered with, because the name on the bib is matched to the document.

That ID check matters. Bib transfers are generally not allowed at Chinese marathons, and identity checks at the start pen are common.

## Racing in a subtropical December

This is what sets Guangzhou apart from China's winter races. The city sits just south of the Tropic of Cancer. December falls in its dry season, when the summer humidity has gone and the mornings are cool rather than cold.

For runners, that usually means:

- Mornings cool enough that many runners wear a throwaway layer in the start pen
- Low rainfall compared with the wet months from spring to autumn
- Sun that can still feel warm by late morning, which matters for slower finishers

Most travelling runners pack for a spring race back home: singlet and shorts for the race, a light jacket for the evening.

## Reaching the start on the Metro

Guangzhou Metro is the obvious way in on race morning, because road closures shut down the centre to cars. If the start is in Tianhe's new town again, the closest stops are:

- **Zhujiang New Town Station** (Lines 3 and 5, plus the APM line), at the centre of the business district
- **Canton Tower Station** (Line 3 and the APM line), just south of the river
- **Tiyu Xilu Station** (Lines 1 and 3), near the Tianhe Sports Centre

The APM is a short automated line that runs underground along the central axis between Huacheng Square and the Canton Tower. It's handy for getting from one end of the start area to the other.

Most runners stay in Tianhe or along Line 3 so they can walk or take one short ride to the pens. Trains on race morning are full of people in running kit, so you don't need to worry much about finding the way.

## Cheering, paying and yum cha after the finish

Spectators along the course shout **"jiāyóu!"** (加油, literally "add oil"). It works as "keep going" for anyone flagging. Locals learn it as children, and runners recognise it in any accent. Once a group of spectators hears it from you, they usually join in.

Paying for things works differently from most of the world. In Guangzhou almost everyone pays with WeChat Pay or Alipay by scanning a QR code. Visitors can now link foreign bank cards to both apps, and most international runners set this up before they travel. Small food stalls in particular may not take cards.

After the race, Cantonese runners have a fixed habit: **yum cha** (饮茶, "drink tea"). It's a dim sum breakfast that can stretch into lunch. Dim sum restaurants pour tea as soon as you sit down. Diners tap two fingers on the table to say thanks when someone tops up their cup.

What runners usually order:

- **Har gow**: shrimp dumplings with translucent wrappers
- **Siu mai**: open-topped pork and shrimp dumplings
- **Cheung fun**: steamed rice-noodle rolls in sweet soy sauce
- **Char siu bao**: fluffy buns filled with barbecued pork

Popular dim sum halls fill up fast on Sunday mornings, so finishers often wait for a table. Many restaurants hand out a queue number at the door.

## Staying on after the race

Plenty of visiting runners give themselves a day or two in the city afterwards, mostly at a gentle pace.

- **Canton Tower**: the observation levels look straight down onto the river you just ran beside.
- **Shamian Island**: a leafy former foreign concession of colonial-era buildings, flat and quiet enough for a recovery walk.
- **Chen Clan Ancestral Hall**: a Qing-dynasty academy hall with dense wood, stone and ceramic carving, a short walk from Chen Clan Academy Station (Line 1).
- **Pearl River night cruise**: boats leave from piers along the river, and the skyline is lit after dark.
- **Beijing Road**: a pedestrian shopping street where glass panels in the pavement show road surfaces from earlier dynasties.

Hong Kong is a short ride by high-speed rail from Guangzhou South station or by intercity train from Guangzhou East. That makes it easy to add to a race trip, and many runners do.
