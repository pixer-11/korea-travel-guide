## Theatre night, Milan style

A theatre gig in Italy follows its own etiquette.

- **Seating:** Italian theatres usually split into *platea* (the stalls) and *galleria* (the balcony), and seats are numbered. Ushers guide you to your row. Fans typically stand during the big singalongs and sit down again for the ballads.
- **Tickets on your phone:** Most attendees bring the digital ticket and an ID. For high-demand Italian concerts the ticket can be tied to the buyer's name.
- **Before the show:** The Milanese *aperitivo* runs in the early evening, usually a spritz or Campari with snacks brought to the table. Bars near Porta Romana and along Corso di Porta Romana fill with concertgoers.
- **After the show:** Italians eat late, so plenty of kitchens keep serving after a concert crowd spills out. Pizza and a *cotoletta alla milanese* (veal cutlet) are the usual late orders.

## December in Milan around the shows

The residency overlaps with the busiest stretch of Milan's winter calendar.

On December 7 the city celebrates Sant'Ambrogio, its patron saint. That date traditionally opens the season at La Scala and brings the Oh Bej! Oh Bej! street market to the streets around the Castello Sforzesco. December 8, the Immacolata, is a national holiday, and Milanese families often put up their Christmas trees that day.

Through the month a Christmas tree and market stalls stand in Piazza del Duomo, and the Galleria Vittorio Emanuele II is decorated for the season. Fans coming from outside the city usually stay near Duomo or Porta Romana, since both are on M3. This holiday period also makes the city's hotels busy.

Visitors staying more than one night often pair a show with Leonardo's *Last Supper* at Santa Maria delle Grazie, which admits small timed groups. The Brera district is another common daytime choice.
