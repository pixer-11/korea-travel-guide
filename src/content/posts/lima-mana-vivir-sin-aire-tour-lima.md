## A stadium night named after a 1992 ballad

The tour takes its name from a love song. "Vivir Sin Aire" first appeared on Maná's 1992 album *¿Dónde Jugarán los Niños?*, the record that took the Guadalajara band from Mexican radio to stadiums across Latin America. More than three decades later, it is the title of a world tour.

The Lima date is December 2, 2026, at Estadio San Marcos. Expect a stadium full of people who know every word. In Peru, Maná belongs to the same shared playlist as family parties and long bus rides, so the crowd spans teenagers to grandparents.

## Estadio San Marcos and the campus around it

The stadium sits inside Ciudad Universitaria, the main campus of the Universidad Nacional Mayor de San Marcos. Founded in 1551, it is the oldest university in the Americas. The campus fills a large block between Avenida Venezuela and Avenida Universitaria, west of central Lima and toward Callao.

This is an open-air concrete bowl, not a covered arena. Lima stadium shows are usually split into two kinds of ticket:

- **Campo**: standing or floor zones on the pitch, often divided by distance from the stage
- **Tribunas**: the seated stands around the bowl, named by compass side (occidente, oriente, norte, sur)

The organiser publishes the seating map, zone names and ticket links for this date on its official sales channels. Maná also lists its tour dates on the band's official website.

## Getting to Avenida Venezuela

Most concertgoers arrive by car. Taxis and ride-hailing apps such as Uber, Cabify and InDrive are how most visitors get around Lima. They are also the usual way to reach San Marcos from the hotel districts.

How long the ride takes depends almost entirely on traffic. Avenida Venezuela and Avenida Universitaria are major arteries, and a stadium crowd on a weekday evening fills them quickly. Fans coming from Miraflores or Barranco usually allow far longer than the distance suggests, and many share rides with friends.

After the show, apps tend to surge and cars struggle to reach the gates. Plenty of people walk a few blocks away from the stadium before requesting a pickup. That gets them clear of the closures and the crowd.

## Moving through a Lima stadium crowd

A few local habits make the night easier.

**Paying.** Peru uses the sol. Cards work at most formal venues. Small outside purchases are often cash or Yape and Plin, the mobile wallets most Peruvians use, which visitors without a Peruvian bank account usually can't access. Carry some small notes for street vendors and bottled water.

**Tickets and resale.** Fake tickets are a real risk at big Lima shows. Local fans generally buy only through the official ticketing platform and treat social-media resellers with suspicion. The name on a ticket can matter at the gate, so keep the booking confirmation and your ID or passport together.

**At the gate.** Large Peruvian stadium shows usually ban umbrellas, glass and large bags at security. Fans tend to travel light: phone, ID, a light jacket. For floor zones, people who want to be near the stage typically join the queue hours before doors open.

**Language.** Stewards and vendors speak Spanish. A few phrases help: *¿Dónde está la puerta?* (where's the gate?) and *¿Cuál es mi zona?* (which is my zone?).

## The singalong is the main event

At a Maná show in Latin America, the crowd does half the singing. The band's catalogue is mostly anthems: "Rayando el Sol", "Oye Mi Amor", "Clavado en un Bar", "En el Muelle de San Blas", "Mariposa Traicionera", "Labios Compartidos", and the Santana collaboration "Corazón Espinado". Lima audiences know the slow songs as well as the rock ones.

Nobody has published a setlist for this date, and no song is guaranteed. The title track is the obvious candidate for a full-stadium chorus, though. Visitors who don't speak Spanish usually find it worth learning the chorus of "Vivir Sin Aire" beforehand. In practice, the stands carry the melody whatever you do.

## December in Lima

December is the run-in to the southern summer. The grey winter *garúa* (mist) has usually cleared by then. Rain is rare in Lima at any time of year, so an open-air stadium is a safe bet.

Evenings near the Pacific still cool down, and in the top tiers of the stands you sit in the wind. Most fans bring one light layer and nothing more. Daytime sun can be strong. Floor-zone fans who queue in the afternoon usually bring a cap and sunscreen.

## Filling the day before the show

The stadium's location makes for an easy day in west Lima.

- **Museo Larco**, in neighbouring Pueblo Libre, displays pre-Columbian ceramics and goldwork in an eighteenth-century mansion. It is one of the closest major museums to the campus.
- **Parque de las Leyendas**, in San Miguel next to the university, combines a zoo with the adobe huacas (ancient mounds) of the Maranga complex.
- **The Historic Centre**, east along Avenida Venezuela, has the Plaza Mayor, the Cathedral and the San Francisco monastery catacombs.

Most visitors base themselves in Miraflores or San Isidro for restaurants and safety, or in the Historic Centre to be closer to the stadium. San Marcos also sits nearer to Jorge Chávez International Airport than Miraflores does. That helps anyone flying out the following morning.
