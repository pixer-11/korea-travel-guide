## A river island turned into a listening room

Picture flat grass ringed by water. Jarasum sits in the Bukhangang (North Han River) beside Gapyeong town, a low, open island that spends most of the year as a campsite and riverside park. For three days in early October it carries stages, food tents and thousands of picnic mats.

The festival has run here since 2004. It grew into South Korea's best-known jazz event by doing something simple: putting international jazz outdoors, on a lawn, in autumn. You don't come here to sit in a numbered seat. You come to spread out on the grass with the river on both sides and the hills of Gapyeong behind the stage.

## Dates and tickets for 2026

The 2026 edition runs **October 9-11, 2026**, opening on Friday, October 9 and finishing on Sunday, October 11. That is a full weekend in Korea's most comfortable season, with clear days and cool evenings by the water.

The organiser runs an official festival website, and that is where the programme, ticket types and transport notes live. This guide does not list performers or prices, because none were supplied to us. The organiser's site is the only reliable source for the bill and for what each ticket covers.

## Getting to Jarasum from Seoul

Rail is the easy answer. Gapyeong Station sits on the Gyeongchun Line, which links to the Seoul subway network, and the ITX-Cheongchun express also stops there. Both run from the Seoul side of the line, and the express is the faster of the two.

From Gapyeong Station, Jarasum is roughly a 20-minute walk south-west through town toward the river. On festival days you follow the crowd carrying folding chairs. Taxis wait at the station, though queues form after the last sets, when everyone leaves at once.

Driving is possible via the Gyeongchun-ro road along the river. However, Gapyeong's two-lane streets clog on autumn weekends even without a festival. The train generally wins.

## On the grass: what a festival day looks like

The main field is lawn, not seating. People stake out space with:

- Picnic mats or thin waterproof groundsheets, because riverside grass holds dew
- Low folding chairs, kept short so the rows behind can see
- A fleece or light down jacket for after sunset, when river air cools fast in October
- A small torch for the walk back across the dark island

Food and drink stalls line the edges of the site, and plenty of attendees treat dinner as a long riverside picnic between sets. Days build slowly from afternoon into evening. The headline sets after dark are what most people stay for.

## Doing Jarasum the way regulars do

Regulars treat the festival as a weekend, not an evening. The Jarasum campsite sits on the island itself, and camping through the festival is a long-standing tradition among fans. Others stay in Gapyeong town or around Cheongpyeong, a few stops back toward Seoul.

Lawn etiquette matters more than at a seated concert. Korean audiences keep mats tidy and take rubbish back out. They also lower chairs and umbrellas once a set starts so the people behind can see. Chatting through quiet ballads draws looks.

Payment at food stalls is mostly by card, and Korean card terminals take international Visa and Mastercard almost everywhere. Tipping isn't part of Korean culture, at the bar or anywhere else. Weekend day-trippers on the Gyeongchun Line usually find the trains back to Seoul busiest straight after the closing set, so many linger over a meal in Gapyeong town first.

## Around Gapyeong between sets

The island sits close to some of Gyeonggi-do's most visited sights:

- **Nami Island (Namiseom)**: a short hop downriver, reached by ferry from the Gapyeong wharf, with tree-lined avenues that turn gold in October
- **Petite France**: a French-village-themed park on the road toward Cheongpyeong Lake
- **The Garden of Morning Calm**: a large hillside garden north of town, known for its autumn colour
- **Gapyeong Rail Park**: rail bikes on a disused section of the old Gyeongchun Line track

Most visitors fit one of these into a festival morning, since the music runs into the night.
