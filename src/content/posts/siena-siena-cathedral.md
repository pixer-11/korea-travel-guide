## Why go

You come around the corner from Via del Capitano and the facade just stops you: bands of green-black and white marble climbing toward a roofline crowded with gilded mosaics and pinnacles, the whole thing lit gold in late-afternoon sun. Siena Cathedral was built mostly between 1215 and 1263, and the striping isn't decoration for its own sake — black and white were Siena's civic colors, tied to the legendary horses of the city's mythical founders. Inside, the same stripes run the length of the nave in dark serpentine marble and travertine, so the whole building reads like a single, obsessive idea carried from floor to ceiling.

## What to see inside

The first thing that pulls your eye down, not up, is the floor: 56 marble panels inlaid and engraved with sibyls, biblical scenes, and allegories, worked on across two centuries by dozens of Sienese artists. Much of it is kept covered and uncovered only for a few weeks a year (typically around August), so check before you go if that's your priority. Off the left aisle, the Piccolomini Library glows with Pinturicchio's fresco cycle on the life of Pope Pius II, colors so saturated they look freshly painted. Nearby stands Nicola Pisano's pulpit, carved in the 1260s with dense, crowded relief scenes from the New Testament — take a slow lap around it, it rewards close looking. Look up in the dome for a starry blue coffered ceiling, and don't skip the crypt below and the unfinished "Facciatone," the wall of the never-completed nave extension, which you can climb for a rooftop view over the striped dome and the Tuscan hills beyond.

## Getting there

The cathedral sits on Piazza del Duomo in the heart of Siena's medieval center, a compact hill town where cars are largely excluded. From Piazza del Campo, the shell-shaped main square, it's a flat five-to-seven-minute walk uphill along Via dei Pellegrini or Via del Capitano. If you're arriving by train, Siena's station is down in the valley; take a taxi or one of the local city buses up to the historic center, since it's a stiff climb on foot with luggage. Drivers should park at one of the peripheral lots (Fontebranda or San Francesco are common choices) and walk in, as the center is a restricted traffic zone.

## When to go

This is one of the most-visited sights in Tuscany, and it shows — tour groups from the coach parks can fill the nave by mid-morning. Early opening time, or the last hour or two before closing, are noticeably calmer, with softer light through the windows and room to actually stand back and take in the facade without a wall of raised phones in front of you. Spring and fall bring the biggest crowds along with the best weather; a winter visit trades a few degrees of chill for a nearly empty piazza. Give yourself 90 minutes to two hours if you want to also see the Baptistery and crypt, which are usually included on combined tickets.

## How to visit like a local

Buy your ticket online before you arrive — the Opa Si Pass (covering the cathedral, crypt, baptistery, and Facciatone) is the standard way to see the full complex, and pre-booking avoids the ticket-office queue that forms on the piazza in peak season. Dress code is enforced: shoulders and knees covered for everyone, no exceptions, so bring a scarf or light layer even on a hot day. Bags are checked at a free deposit point near the entrance if you're carrying anything oversized. Because this is an active place of worship, keep voices low and phones on silent, and avoid visiting during a scheduled Mass unless you intend to attend respectfully rather than sightsee. The most common visitor mistake is treating it as a quick photo stop and rushing through — the marble floor and library alone deserve a slow half hour, so budget more time than you think you need and go early or late to get it.
