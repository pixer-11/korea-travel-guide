Two days, one field, and a crowd that knows every word. Playlist Rewind Festival takes place in Bandung on 14–15 November 2026, and the premise is simple: the songs you grew up with, loud through the speakers, with the crowd singing them back louder. This is a festival where the audience is half the performance.

## What a rewind festival sounds like

The pitch is nostalgia. Sets lean on the hits people already carry in their heads: the radio staples, the school-trip singalongs, the choruses that turn a field of strangers into a choir.

That changes how the crowd behaves. People don't come to discover a new band. They come to hear the second verse they half-remember, phone torch held up, friends' arms around shoulders.

Indonesian festival crowds are famous for knowing lyrics word for word, and a nostalgia bill makes the most of that.

## Dates, tickets and what is fixed

Here is what is set for this edition:

- **Dates:** Saturday 14 and Sunday 15 November 2026

Two-day festivals in Indonesia commonly sell single-day and full-weekend passes. Fans usually pick the day according to which headliner matters most to them.

## Secapa: a military field on the hill

Secapa AD is the Indonesian Army's officer-candidate school. Its grounds sit in Hegarmanah, a leafy, residential part of north Bandung near Jalan Setiabudi, and the big open field there has become one of the city's regular concert spaces.

The setting is part of the character. You're on a slope of the city that climbs towards Lembang, among old trees and quiet streets. It feels nothing like a downtown arena.

Expect an outdoor field. Ground conditions depend on the weather, so the shoes people pick for this venue have more to do with grass and mud than with style.

## November rain in the Bandung highlands

This is the one thing every out-of-town visitor underestimates. Bandung sits roughly 700 metres above sea level, and the northern districts around Secapa are higher still. Evenings get noticeably cooler than Jakarta.

Mid-November also falls in West Java's rainy season. Afternoon and evening downpours are common, and an outdoor field takes them in full.

Regular Bandung concertgoers tend to bring:

- a packable rain poncho (umbrellas block views and are often not allowed in crowds)
- a light jacket or hoodie for after dark
- shoes they don't mind getting muddy
- a zip bag for the phone

## Getting up to Hegarmanah

Most festival-goers come from Jakarta, and there are three common routes.

- **Whoosh high-speed rail:** the Jakarta–Bandung line runs from Halim to Tegalluar and Padalarang. From Padalarang a feeder train connects to Bandung Station, and from there it's a ride-hail north to Setiabudi.
- **Regular KAI trains:** Argo Parahyangan services from Jakarta's Gambir run to Bandung Station in the city centre.
- **By road:** the Cipularang toll road is the usual way. It feeds into Pasteur, and from there you head north.

Inside the city, Gojek and Grab are how most people move. Motorbike taxis (ojek) cut through traffic far better than cars do. On a festival weekend that matters, because Bandung's northern roads are narrow and already fill with Jakarta weekend traffic.

Locals know Jalan Setiabudi as a bottleneck on Saturdays and Sundays. Festival-goers coming from the centre usually allow much more time than the map suggests.

## Moving through the crowd at Secapa

A few habits make the weekend easier.

**Payment.** Indonesia runs on QRIS, the national QR payment standard, along with e-wallets such as GoPay, OVO, DANA and ShopeePay. Many local festivals are largely cashless at food and merchandise stalls. Visitors without an Indonesian e-wallet usually carry a card and a little cash as backup.

**Ride-hail pickups.** When the last act finishes, thousands of people open the same apps at once. Drivers often can't get near the gate. Experienced attendees walk a few streets away from the venue and request their ride from there, and groups usually agree on a meeting point among themselves.

**Getting in.** Indonesian festivals often swap e-tickets for wristbands at a separate redemption point. Bags are searched at the gate. Prayer areas (mushola) are a standard fixture at large Indonesian events.

**Singing along.** Join in. On a nostalgia bill, staying quiet stands out more than singing off-key.

## Around Setiabudi and Ciumbuleuit

The streets around the venue are good for eating before or after a set. Jalan Setiabudi and nearby Ciumbuleuit, home to the Parahyangan Catholic University campus, are lined with student cafés, warung and factory-outlet shops.

Bandung street food worth looking for on the way:

- **batagor:** fried fish dumplings with peanut sauce, a Bandung original
- **seblak:** spicy, soupy wet crackers, the city's current obsession
- **cuanki:** a light meatball-and-dumpling soup sold from carts
- **surabi:** small rice-flour pancakes, cooked over clay, with sweet or savoury toppings

Visitors who make a weekend of it often stay in the Setiabudi, Dago or Ciumbuleuit areas, so the trip back from the field is short. Others head up to Lembang's cooler hills on the free morning, or down to Jalan Braga in the old colonial centre. Braga's Art Deco shopfronts are a short ride south.
