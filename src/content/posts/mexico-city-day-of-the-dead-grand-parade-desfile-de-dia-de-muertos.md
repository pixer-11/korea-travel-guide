## Ten Years of Skeletons on Reforma

Paseo de la Reforma is built for spectacle. It is a wide, tree-lined boulevard of glass towers and bronze monuments, and on parade day it belongs to the dead. Giant skeletons several storeys tall sway above the crowd, worked by teams on the ground. Catrinas in wide-brimmed hats and lace walk beside them.

The 2026 edition is the 10th annual Desfile de Día de Muertos. It runs on **Saturday, October 31, 2026**, two days ahead of the holiday itself. There is no ticket and no entry fee. The parade is free, and you watch from the pavement like everyone else.

## The Parade That Started in a Bond Film

Unlike most of Mexico's Day of the Dead customs, this parade is new. The opening sequence of the 2015 James Bond film *Spectre* staged a huge Día de Muertos procession through the Centro Histórico. Back then the city had no such event. Visitors arrived the next year expecting one, and Mexico City launched its own parade in 2016.

The film origin shows in the style. The parade leans toward big set pieces:

- towering articulated calaveras (skulls and skeletons)
- alebrijes, the brightly painted fantasy creatures of Mexican folk art
- floats dressed in orange cempasúchil (marigold), the flower of the holiday
- dancers, musicians and costumed groups in catrina face paint

Ten editions in, it has become part of how the city marks the season. The quieter family rituals of the following days still run alongside it.

## Chapultepec to the Zócalo, Block by Block

The route runs west to east. It starts near Bosque de Chapultepec, the city's great park, and heads down Paseo de la Reforma. Along the way it passes the glorietas, the roundabouts with monuments at their centre, including the Ángel de la Independencia.

At the eastern end of Reforma the parade turns into the old centre and finishes in the Zócalo. That is the vast stone plaza framed by the Metropolitan Cathedral and the Palacio Nacional. It is a long route, several kilometres, so the whole column takes time to pass any one point.

Each stretch offers something different:

- **Reforma near Chapultepec:** the widest sightlines and the most room to spread out.
- **Around the Ángel de la Independencia:** a natural gathering point, with the monument behind the floats.
- **The Centro Histórico approach:** narrower streets and colonial facades, so the giant figures feel much bigger.
- **The Zócalo:** the finale. It is the most packed spot and the hardest to see from unless you are near the barriers.

## Getting to the Curb

The Metro is the most practical way to reach the route. Streets near Reforma are usually closed to traffic on parade day. Useful stations include:

- **Chapultepec (Line 1)** for the western start
- **Hidalgo (Lines 2 and 3)** near the eastern end of Reforma and the Alameda Central
- **Bellas Artes (Lines 2 and 8)** beside the Palacio de Bellas Artes
- **Zócalo/Tenochtitlan (Line 2)**, right under the finishing plaza

Stations directly on the route sometimes have exits closed or limited for crowd control. Many people get off a stop or two away and walk in. The Metrobús runs along Reforma too, but its service on the route is usually disrupted while the parade is moving.

## Holding a Spot Along the Barriers

Metal barriers line the route, and the front row goes to whoever got there first. Families usually stake out a curb section well before the parade reaches them. They bring folding stools, snacks and children already in costume. Behind them, the crowd is often several people deep.

What regular parade-goers tend to do:

- **Pick a stretch on Reforma rather than the Zócalo.** The boulevard is wide and the crowd spreads out. The plaza at the end fills to capacity.
- **Come in costume or face paint.** Catrina make-up is everywhere, and street artists along the route paint faces for anyone who stops. Nobody looks twice at a painted skull.
- **Carry small cash in pesos.** Street vendors work the crowd selling elotes, drinks, sugar skulls and paper flowers, and they rarely take cards.
- **Keep phones and wallets in front pockets.** Dense crowds on Reforma attract pickpockets, as in any big city gathering.
- **Let children sit on the curb at the front.** People generally make room for them, and asking politely in Spanish ("¿Puede pasar el niño?") goes a long way.

When the last float passes, the crowd usually walks along behind it toward the centre. That stream of people is the easiest way to reach the Zócalo afterwards.

Mexico City's government runs the parade, and its culture office publishes the route and start time for each edition.

## October 31 Is Only the Opening Act

The parade's timing confuses many first-time visitors. It falls on October 31, but Día de Muertos proper is **November 1 and 2**. November 1 traditionally honours children who have died. November 2 honours adults.

During those two days the city looks inward. Homes, markets and public buildings set up ofrendas: altars layered with marigolds, candles, photographs, pan de muerto and the favourite foods of the dead. The Zócalo often hosts a large public ofrenda installation around the holiday. Cemeteries fill with families decorating graves.

San Andrés Mixquic, in the borough of Tláhuac on the city's southeastern edge, is the best-known place for candlelit cemetery vigils. Xochimilco's canals also host Day of the Dead events. Many visitors combine the Saturday parade with these quieter rituals for a full long weekend.

## Making a Weekend of It

The parade route is ringed with places worth your afternoon. Bosque de Chapultepec holds the Museo Nacional de Antropología and Chapultepec Castle. At the other end, the Zócalo sits beside the Templo Mayor ruins and the Metropolitan Cathedral.

Between them, the Alameda Central and the Palacio de Bellas Artes sit right where Reforma meets the old centre. Pan de muerto, the sweet bread topped with sugar and bone-shaped dough, appears in bakeries across the city through late October and early November. Most visitors eat it more than once.
