## Why go

The pagoda you photograph from the West Lake shoreline isn't the original — it's a 2002 rebuild standing over the excavated bones of the real thing, and that layering of legend, ruin, and reconstruction is exactly what makes a visit worthwhile. The first Leifeng Pagoda went up in 975 AD, commissioned by a local king to mark the birth of his son, and stood for nearly a thousand years before collapsing in 1924 — partly because superstitious locals kept chipping bricks from its base, believing pagoda dust warded off illness. That collapse became folded into the legend of the White Snake, one of China's great folk tales, in which the pagoda traps a shape-shifting snake spirit beneath it forever. Today's structure, clad in copper and glass, was built directly over the excavated foundation, so your visit moves through actual archaeology before it moves through architecture.

## What to see

You enter at ground level into a dim, climate-controlled chamber built around the crumbled brick core of the 10th-century tower — worn, uneven, roped off but lit well enough to make out individual bricks laid a millennium ago. Glass panels let you look straight down into the foundation pit. From there, escalators carry you up through the new structure's eight modern floors (the copper exterior mimics the original five-story silhouette but the interior is subdivided further), each level lined with carved reliefs and painted panels retelling the White Snake legend in sequence — worth slowing down for even if you can't read the captions. The payoff is the top-floor viewing deck, where the railings frame a wide, uninterrupted view over West Lake, the humped shape of Su Causeway cutting across the water, and on clear days, the hills of Hangzhou rolling out behind you.

## Getting there

The pagoda sits at 15 Nan Shan Lu in Xihu District, on the southern shore of West Lake near Jingci Temple and the Su Causeway. There's no metro stop right at the gate, so most visitors arrive by taxi, bike-share, or on foot along the lakeside path — it's an easy 20–25 minute walk from the Leifeng Pagoda bus stop or from the Su Causeway if you're already exploring that side of the lake. Public buses (routes such as Y2 and K4) stop within a few minutes' walk. If you're combining it with other West Lake sights, plan to arrive from Jingci Temple or Hupao Spring, both nearby, rather than trekking across from the north shore.

## When to go

Set aside about 1.5 to 2 hours, more if you want to linger over the relief carvings on each floor. Early morning, shortly after opening, gives you the clearest light for photos over the lake and the thinnest crowds moving through the ruins chamber. Weekday afternoons are also noticeably calmer than weekends, when domestic tour groups tend to cluster around midday. Despite its postcard fame, Leifeng Pagoda draws a lighter crowd than West Lake's other headline stops, so even on a moderately busy day you're unlikely to feel jostled.

## How to visit like a local

Buy your ticket at the gate or through a Chinese ticketing app (Meituan or Ctrip both list it) — cash and mobile payment via Alipay or WeChat Pay are both accepted, and card machines are less reliable, so don't count on a foreign card working smoothly. There's no need to book far ahead; queues are short enough that walking up on the day works fine outside major holidays. Wear shoes with grip — the escalators inside are steep and the ruins chamber floor is uneven stone. The mistake most first-time visitors make is treating this as a quick photo-op from outside and skipping the interior entirely; the excavated foundation and the legend murals are the actual substance of the visit, not the exterior shell. If you want the view without the crowds, time your climb to the top deck for late afternoon, when the light softens over the lake and day-trip groups have started heading back toward their buses.
