Pisa is flat. For anyone weighing up a winter marathon, that one fact counts for a lot. The city sits on the Arno plain a few kilometres from the Tuscan coast, and its old centre has almost no climbs to wear down your legs.

The Pisa Marathon takes place on **December 20, 2026**. There is a full marathon and a half marathon, both run through the historic streets, and both finish near the Leaning Tower. Few races anywhere end in front of a monument this famous.

## A race that ends under the tilt

The finish is the reason people travel for this one. Piazza dei Miracoli, also called Piazza del Duomo, is a walled lawn holding four white-marble buildings: the cathedral, the baptistery, the Camposanto cemetery and the bell tower that leans.

Finishing near it means your last stretch runs toward a skyline you already know from postcards. The marble is pale against the grass, and the tower's lean is plain to see from the approach. Runners usually walk back into the square afterwards for the photo everyone wants: medal on, tower tilting behind them.

## December on the Arno

The date is a feature rather than a drawback. Late December is low season in Pisa. The day-trip coaches that fill Piazza dei Miracoli in summer are far fewer, so the city feels like a working university town again.

Winters on the Tuscan plain are usually cool rather than harsh. That suits distance running, but it also means the waiting is cold, both before the start and after you cross the line. Runners typically bring a throwaway layer for the start and pack something warm in their post-race bag.

The race also falls days before Christmas. Streets in the centre usually carry seasonal lights, and plenty of visitors make a short holiday of the trip, adding Florence or Lucca to the race weekend.

## Getting to Pisa and around the course

Pisa is one of the easiest Italian cities to reach.

- **By air:** Pisa International Airport (Galileo Galilei) is unusually close to town. The PisaMover shuttle train links it to Pisa Centrale in a few minutes.
- **By train:** Pisa Centrale is on the main Tyrrhenian coast line. Florence Santa Maria Novella is roughly an hour away on regional trains.
- **To the finish area:** Pisa San Rossore station sits a short walk from Piazza dei Miracoli. One stop from Centrale saves tired legs after the race.
- **On foot:** From Centrale to the tower, walk up Corso Italia, cross the Arno on Ponte di Mezzo and carry on through Borgo Stretto. It takes about 20–25 minutes.

Road closures for the race affect traffic in the centre, so most runners leave the car behind and walk or take the train. The organiser's official site publishes the course map, start location and timings.

## Where runners usually stay

The centre is compact, so nearly anywhere inside the old walls works.

- **Around Pisa Centrale:** The practical choice, with direct airport and Florence connections and a straight walk north to the river.
- **Along the Lungarni:** The riverside streets either side of the Arno sit between the station and the tower. They make a calm base for a pre-race evening walk.
- **Near Piazza dei Miracoli:** The closest you can be to the finish. Expect more souvenir shops and fewer dinner options open late in winter.

Some visitors stay in Lucca, about half an hour away by train, and come in on race morning. That makes sense only if the train timetable suits the start time.

## Race-morning manners, Pisan style

Italian race mornings run on espresso, and the bar routine catches visitors out.

- **Coffee:** In many bars you pay at the *cassa* (till) first, then hand your receipt to the barista. Drink it standing at the counter, the way Pisans do. Ask for *un caffè* for an espresso. A cappuccino before a race is normal; after lunch it gets looks.
- **Payment:** Cards and contactless are widely taken. Small bars sometimes prefer cash for a single coffee, so a few coins help.
- **Tipping:** Not expected in bars. In restaurants, rounding up is enough. A *coperto* (cover charge) on a bill is standard, not a scam.
- **Cheering:** Spectators shout *"Dai!"* or *"Forza!"* (come on, go on). If you have friends watching, teach them both.
- **Carb-loading:** The night before, a plate of pasta at a trattoria is the obvious move. Many Tuscan menus also carry *ribollita* (bread and vegetable soup) and *cecina*, the chickpea flatbread Pisa claims as its own.

Italian races generally ask for a medical certificate for competitive sport from all entrants, foreigners included. The organiser's official site sets out exactly what it accepts and how registration works.

## After the finish line

With the medal on, the city is small enough to see on stiff legs.

- **Piazza dei Miracoli:** The cathedral and baptistery are steps from the finish. Climbing the tower is a separate timed ticket and probably one to leave for the next day.
- **Piazza dei Cavalieri:** The Renaissance square of the Scuola Normale Superiore, with Vasari's graffito-covered Palazzo della Carovana. It is a ten-minute walk south of the tower.
- **Santa Maria della Spina:** A small Gothic church right on the Arno embankment, covered in marble spires.
- **Borgo Stretto:** A covered arcade street of cafés and pastry shops, and a gentle place for a recovery stroll.

Many runners spend the day after the race in Lucca, walking or cycling on its tree-lined city walls. Others take the train to Florence and treat the marathon as the opening of a Tuscan Christmas week.

## How long to plan for

Most international runners treat this as a long weekend. They arrive on the Friday or Saturday to pick up race numbers and walk the centre, run on Sunday the 20th, and leave Monday. Two nights is the minimum that feels comfortable. Three gives you an unhurried day to look at the tower without a race bib on.
