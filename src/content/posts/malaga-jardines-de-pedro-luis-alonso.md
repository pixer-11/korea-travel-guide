## What you're looking at

This is a geometric garden, not a wild one: box hedges trimmed into low patterns, gravel paths in a symmetrical grid, and terraced rose beds that step up toward the hillside behind the Ayuntamiento building.

A lily pond anchors the lower section, ringed by benches under palms and jacarandas. The roses are the headline; beds are planted in blocks of single colors, a style borrowed from formal French and Andalusian garden design of the early 20th century.

Behind the garden, the Alcazaba's fortress walls rise up the slope, giving you a strange contrast: manicured flowerbeds framed by Moorish battlements. It's a five-minute walk from the harbor, right where Paseo del Parque meets Calle Guillén Sotelo.

## Getting there and timing it

The address is C. Guillén Sotelo, 31, in Distrito Centro, directly in front of Málaga's city hall. It's a flat, five-to-ten-minute walk from Plaza de la Marina or the cathedral.

The garden never closes; it's open 24 hours every day of the week, though there's no lighting drama at night, just streetlamps and the building floodlights next door.

- Quietest weekend window: 7am-4pm
- Quietest weekday window: 9pm-11pm
- Busiest weekend stretch: 9am-10pm, when cruise groups and Alcazaba visitors pass through

If you're combining it with the Alcazaba or Gibralfaro, come early morning before the heat and the tour groups both arrive.

## Beating the crowds without losing the roses

Málaga cruise ships tend to dock mid-morning, and their groups walk this exact corridor between the port and the Alcazaba entrance. Weekend mornings before 9am, or after 4pm on weekdays before the evening paseo crowd, are your gaps.

It's rated as one of the better-loved green spots in central Málaga without being a locals-only secret; expect Spanish families and tourists mixed evenly on any given afternoon.

Because it's unenclosed and free, there's no queue, no ticket booth and no printed map: just walk in from any of the four corner entrances.

## Making the most of a short visit

Fifteen to twenty minutes covers it properly, longer if you sit by the pond. Bring nothing more than water and sun protection: shade is limited at midday since most trees are young palms rather than broad canopy trees.

Photographers do best in the first two hours after sunrise, when the rose colors read clearly and the path gravel isn't yet scuffed by foot traffic. Pair the visit with the adjacent Paseo del Parque, a much longer botanical promenade running parallel to the port, so you're not backtracking.

Skip trying to picnic here: benches are the only seating, and there are no tables or shaded lawns for spreading out.
