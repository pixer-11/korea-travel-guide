Iron columns hold up a roof of skylights over rows of wooden stalls. The hall is one long, high room, with produce, bread and cheese at the counters and plates of food passing between them to communal tables. This is Markthalle Neun, the ninth of the municipal market halls Berlin built in the 1890s. It still does the job it was built for: people come here to buy and eat food.

## A market hall that nearly disappeared

The building dates from 1891. It was part of a city programme to move food trading off the streets and into covered halls. Over the following century most of those halls closed or were converted. This one survived into the 2000s in a run-down state, with discount retail filling much of the floor.

In 2011 a local initiative took it over and rebuilt it around regional producers, independent food businesses and events. That history explains its mix today. Some stalls sell groceries you would carry home. Others sell lunch you eat standing up. The editorial line you'll see everywhere is accurate: international food vendors, shops and occasional community events, all under one roof.

## Getting to Eisenbahnstraße

The address is Eisenbahnstraße 42/43, in the part of Kreuzberg locals call SO36 or the Wrangelkiez.

- **U-Bahn:** Görlitzer Bahnhof (U1 and U3), then about five minutes on foot north up the side streets.
- **Alternative:** Kottbusser Tor (U1, U3, U8) is a longer but straightforward walk east along Skalitzer Straße.
- **By bike:** this is easy cycling country, and you'll find racks around the hall's entrances.

There's no point in driving. Street parking in the Kiez is tight and the U-Bahn drops you almost at the door.

## When the hall is calm and when it isn't

The schedule is simple: 9am to 8pm Monday to Saturday, with Thursday running until 10pm. **Sunday it is closed**, which catches out plenty of weekend visitors.

The weekday crowd figures show the quietest stretch running 9am–9pm. In practice, that means any hour the doors are open from Monday to Friday is comfortable. Saturday is the problem. Try not to arrive between 11am and 7pm, when the aisles fill and the tables are hard to come by. If Saturday is your only day, come at the 9am opening for coffee and bread or slip in for the last hour.

Thursday is a special case. The late opening is built around the evening street-food session that made the hall famous. Thursday daytime is calm, but the evening is lively by design. Check the official site for what's running that week.

## How the hall works once you're inside

There's no single kitchen and no table service. You walk the aisles, pick a stall, order at its counter and carry your food to a shared table. Expect to sit beside strangers, because that's how the long tables are meant to be used.

A few habits will make it smoother:

- **Payment:** many stalls take cards, but Germany still runs on cash in small places. Keep some euros on you for counters that don't.
- **Pfand:** drinks often come in glasses or cups with a deposit. Return them to the counter you bought from and the deposit comes back.
- **Tipping:** rounding up or leaving the coins is normal at a counter. Nobody expects a restaurant-style percentage.
- **Clearing up:** take your plates and glasses back rather than leaving them on the table.
- **Language:** English works at almost every stall. "Einmal, bitte" (one, please) and pointing will get you the rest of the way.

Prices are posted at each stall. Look at the boards before you queue, because some counters are snack-sized and others are a full meal.

## The shopping side of the hall

Many visitors come only for lunch. The producer stalls are what set the hall apart. Bread, cheese, vegetables, meat and dry goods are sold to people doing their weekly shopping. On a weekday morning it's the neighbourhood's own market, and some visitors miss that side of it.

If you're staying somewhere with a kitchen, shop here instead of at a supermarket. Even without one, a loaf and some cheese make a picnic for later. Opening times for individual stalls vary within the hall's hours, so the earlier you come, the more you'll find open.

## Around the Wrangelkiez afterwards

The hall sits in one of Kreuzberg's most walkable pockets, so plan to leave on foot.

- **Görlitzer Park:** a big, scruffy green space a few minutes south, popular for sitting in the sun.
- **Lausitzer Platz:** the square with Emmaus Church, just around the corner.
- **Oranienstraße:** bars, bookshops and late-night food a short walk north-west.
- **The Spree and Oberbaum Bridge:** walk east through the Wrangelkiez to the river and the double-decker brick bridge into Friedrichshain.

An hour covers a meal and a lap of the stalls. Allow two or more if it's Thursday evening or you're shopping as well as eating.
