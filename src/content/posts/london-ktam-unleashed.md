## Nine days, two cities

KTAM Unleashed runs from 6 to 14 November 2026. The whole thing is built around the UK's K-pop fan communities rather than one headline act. Rising Korean artists perform alongside special guests, and the programme is split between London and Brighton.

That fan-community framing sets the tone. A large share of the crowd already knows the choreography, the chants and each other from fandom group chats. Newer fans get to see artists early in their careers, among people who have followed them from the start.

## London and Brighton: where it happens

The festival spans two very different cities. The specific venue for each date sits with the organiser's listings rather than in this guide, which holds only the dates, the two cities and the format.

London gives the event its reach. It has the biggest concentration of K-pop fans in the country and the transport links to pull in people from the rest of the UK. Brighton, on the Sussex coast, is a compact seaside city with two universities and a young population.

November matters in both places. Sunset comes at around half past four in mid-month, so most attendees head to evening shows in full darkness. On Brighton's seafront the wind comes straight off the Channel, and a waterproof layer does more work than a statement jacket.

## Doing the London–Brighton run

The two cities sit about an hour apart by rail. Trains to Brighton leave from several London terminals:

- **London Victoria:** Southern and Gatwick Express services, the most direct option from the West End.
- **London Bridge, Blackfriars, Farringdon and St Pancras:** Thameslink services running north to south through the city.

Brighton station sits at the top of the hill. From there, Queen's Road runs downhill to the Clock Tower and the seafront in roughly a 15-minute walk. Weekend engineering works on the Brighton Main Line sometimes swap trains for replacement buses, which adds real time to the journey.

Late-evening timetables thin out on both lines. Fans travelling back to London after a Brighton show often stay the night on the coast instead.

## Show-night habits of UK K-pop fans

K-pop crowds in the UK have their own etiquette. Much of it will feel familiar from Seoul, and some of it is very British. This is what regulars usually do:

- **Lightsticks:** fans bring the official lightstick of the group they support, and turn them on together during the set.
- **Fanchants:** these are learned in advance from fan-made guides, so the crowd shouts members' names in unison at the right beats.
- **Freebies:** small handmade gifts like stickers, keyrings or printed photocards get swapped in the queue, usually from a tote bag.
- **Photocard trades:** people arrive with binders and trade lists, and swaps happen in person.
- **Payment:** UK venues are largely card-only now, and contactless covers bars, merch and cloakrooms.

For standing shows, the most dedicated fans usually queue for hours, and queues often self-organise with numbers. Queue-jumping is the one thing that reliably turns a friendly British crowd cold. Filming rules vary by artist and venue, and fans generally follow whatever the stage crew announces.

## Korean food between shows

London's Korean food scene runs deeper than most visitors expect. **New Malden**, in south-west London, has one of the largest Korean communities in Europe. It is a short South Western Railway ride from London Waterloo, and its high street has Korean restaurants, bakeries and supermarkets.

Closer to the centre, the streets around **Tottenham Court Road and Soho** have a cluster of Korean barbecue, fried chicken and pocha-style bars. Fans often meet there before a London date. Korean supermarkets in both areas stock snacks, drinks and the occasional album shelf, which is handy for last-minute freebie supplies.
