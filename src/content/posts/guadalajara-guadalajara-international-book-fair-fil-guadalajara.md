## Nine days, one roof, a lot of books

Expo Guadalajara turns into a city of shelving. For nine days, from November 28 to December 6, 2026, the convention halls on Avenida Mariano Otero fill with publishers' stands. There are stacked tables of new releases, author talks in side rooms, and aisles where school groups, booksellers and novelists share the same carpet.

This is the 40th Feria Internacional del Libro de Guadalajara. The University of Guadalajara founded it in 1987, and it has grown into the largest Spanish-language publishing event anywhere. Literary agents come to trade rights. Readers come to meet the writers they have only seen on spines.

## Italy at the 40th

Every edition hands one country the guest-of-honour pavilion, and for 2026 it is Italy. That means a dedicated national space on the fair floor and a programme of Italian writers, translators and publishers. Italian titles in Spanish translation also get a much bigger presence than they normally have on Mexican shelves.

For a visitor, the guest pavilion is usually the most designed part of the fair. It works as a showcase for one literary culture rather than a sales hall. It is a good place to start if you want a thread to follow through the noise, and Italian authors' sessions tend to fill quickly.

The anniversary adds weight too. A 40th edition is a milestone for an institution that Mexican readers treat as part of the calendar, alongside the holidays it runs into.

## How the fair floor works

The layout rewards a little orientation. Expect these zones:

- **Publisher stands**: large Spanish, Mexican and Latin American houses alongside small independent presses.
- **The guest-of-honour pavilion**: Italy's space for this edition.
- **FIL Niños**: the children's area, with reading and activities aimed at families.
- **Presentation rooms**: book launches, panels and readings running through the day.
- **The professional area**: rights trading and industry meetings for registered publishers, agents and librarians.

The FIL Literary Award in Romance Languages is traditionally presented around the opening. It is one of the larger literary prizes in the Spanish-speaking world, and the winner's presence shapes the first days of coverage.

Most people spend at least half a day on the floor. Serious readers and industry visitors often return across several days, picking sessions from the printed and online programme.

## Getting to Expo Guadalajara

Expo Guadalajara sits southwest of the historic centre, near the Plaza del Sol shopping centre in the Zapopan–Guadalajara commercial belt. It is not within walking distance of the cathedral district. Most visitors arrive by taxi or ride-hailing apps, which operate widely across the city.

Several large hotels cluster around the Expo. Out-of-town authors and publishers usually stay there to avoid daily cross-city traffic. Visitors who stay in the Centro Histórico or the Chapultepec–Americana neighbourhoods typically budget extra travel time, because evening traffic on the main avenues is heavy.

Flying in, you land at Guadalajara International Airport (Miguel Hidalgo y Costilla), south of the city. It is a straightforward drive to the Expo area.

## Tickets and where the details live

The University of Guadalajara runs the fair. Its official site, fil.com.mx, is where the dates, the venue information, the full programme and current ticket terms are kept. Entry prices and any discount categories are posted there and at the Expo ticket windows. This guide does not quote a figure.

Professional accreditation for publishers, agents and librarians is a separate process from general admission. It is also handled through the fair's site.

## Working the aisles at FIL

A few habits separate seasoned FIL-goers from first-timers.

- **Carry a sturdy tote or a small rolling bag.** Regulars come out with far more than they planned, and hardbacks get heavy across nine halls.
- **Pay both ways.** Big publishers' stands usually take cards. Small presses and independent stalls are more likely to want pesos in cash.
- **Ask for the fair price.** Many stands mark books down for the event, so asking "¿tiene descuento de feria?" is normal rather than pushy.
- **Join signing queues with the book already bought.** Lines for popular authors form at the stand or room after a session. Readers usually hold the title open at the title page.
- **Mark the programme.** People circle sessions in the official programme and plan their route between rooms, because the halls are long.

Weekdays bring large school groups, especially in the children's area. The weekends draw families from across Jalisco. Adults chasing particular talks often look for sessions that fall outside the main school-visit hours.

A few words of Spanish go a long way. "¿Dónde está la firma?" (where is the signing?) and "¿Lo tiene en español?" cover a lot of ground.

## When the halls close: Guadalajara in late autumn

Many visitors pair the fair with the rest of the city. Late November and early December is dry season in Guadalajara: clear days, cool evenings and Christmas lights going up.

Close-by and worth the evening:

- **Centro Histórico**: the cathedral, Plaza de Armas and the Hospicio Cabañas with its José Clemente Orozco murals, a UNESCO World Heritage Site.
- **Chapultepec and Colonia Americana**: cafés, bars and bookshops along Avenida Chapultepec.
- **Tlaquepaque**: the craft town to the southeast, with pottery workshops and mariachi around El Parián.

For food, eat what Guadalajara is known for. Try a torta ahogada, a crusty birote roll drowned in tomato and chilli sauce, and a bowl of birria. Both are easy to find across the city, from market stalls to sit-down fondas.
