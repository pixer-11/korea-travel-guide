## Where the talks stand

This is a negotiation, not a calendar entry. In November 2026, Buenos Aires was in advanced talks to bring Formula 1 back to Argentina. Officials expected a decision by March 2027. Renovation works were already underway at the Autódromo Oscar y Juan Gálvez, the circuit that hosted every earlier Argentine Grand Prix.

That is everything verified. The facts behind this guide include no race date, ticket price, promoter or session schedule, so none appear here. What follows sticks to what doesn't change: the circuit, its past, the trip to get there and how Buenos Aires works around a big motorsport weekend.

## A flat circuit at the city's southern edge

The Autódromo sits in Villa Riachuelo, in the far south-west of the city proper, just inside the Avenida General Paz ring road. The ground is flat parkland near the Riachuelo, the river that marks the city's southern boundary. Expect wide skies and long sightlines, but no hills or natural banking to watch from.

The circuit opened in 1952. It is named after Oscar and Juan Gálvez, two brothers who dominated Argentina's Turismo Carretera road racing. The track is a network of loops, and organisers combine them into several numbered layouts. Any Formula 1 return would use whichever configuration the renovation produces, so this guide makes no claims about lap length or corners.

## Fangio's shadow, Colapinto's crowd

This section is about why the talks matter so much locally. Argentina hosted world championship races in four stretches: 1953–1960, the 1970s, the early 1980s and 1995–1998. Juan Manuel Fangio won his home Grand Prix four years in a row from 1954 to 1957. The last edition was in 1998, won by Michael Schumacher for Ferrari.

Interest grew again with the rise of Argentine driver Franco Colapinto, who reached the grid in 2024. His arrival brought a wave of light-blue-and-white flags to circuits around the world. Argentine motorsport crowds already sing and chant like football terraces at domestic touring-car meetings. A home Grand Prix would draw on that same culture.

## Getting to Villa Riachuelo

The circuit is a long way from the neighbourhoods visitors usually stay in. Palermo, Recoleta and San Telmo are all well over half an hour away by road, and longer in weekend event traffic. Most people travel in one of these ways:

- **Taxi or ride-hailing**, heading south-west via the Autopista Dellepiane or General Paz
- **Colectivo (city bus)**, paid with a rechargeable SUBE card, not cash
- **Organised coach transfers**, which big events at the Autódromo often run from central points

At domestic race meetings, the roads around the gates jam in the hour before the main race and again straight after the chequered flag. Spectators who stay for the podium or a post-race choripán often get out faster than those who leave on the final lap.

## Grandstand habits: choripán, mate and paying your way

Argentine race days run on a few habits that surprise first-time visitors. The food is grilled, the drink is shared and payment comes in several forms.

- **Food:** the circuit staple is the choripán, a grilled chorizo in crusty bread, usually topped with chimichurri. Grills behind the stands do most of the cooking.
- **Mate:** groups pass a single gourd of yerba mate around with one metal straw. Accepting a turn is friendly. Wiping the straw is not.
- **Paying:** cash, cards and Mercado Pago QR codes are all common in the city. Small food stalls lean towards cash or QR.
- **Tipping:** in restaurants, Porteños usually leave around ten percent, often in cash even when paying the bill by card.
- **Dinner timing:** restaurants fill from about 9pm onwards. A table at 7pm is often empty because locals haven't arrived yet.

A few words of Spanish go a long way at stalls. *Un chori, por favor* gets you a sandwich, and *¿Se puede pagar con QR?* asks whether QR payment works.

## Where race visitors base themselves

Nobody stays near the circuit for the nightlife. Visitors usually split between a few central neighbourhoods:

- **Palermo:** leafy streets, parrillas and the city's densest cluster of bars
- **Recoleta:** grand apartment blocks and the famous cemetery
- **San Telmo:** cobbled streets and the Sunday antiques fair on Plaza Dorrego
- **Puerto Madero:** the converted docklands, with river-facing hotels

One common mix-up: Argentina's MotoGP round has been held at Termas de Río Hondo in Santiago del Estero province. That is more than a thousand kilometres from Buenos Aires and has nothing to do with this circuit.

## Seeing the Autódromo without Formula 1

The circuit is in regular use. Domestic series including Turismo Carretera and TC2000 race here, and those weekends show the venue as Argentine fans know it. You'll find packed paddocks, smoke from the grills and flags hung over the fences.

The city has also hosted single-seaters away from the Autódromo. Formula E ran the Buenos Aires ePrix on a street circuit in Puerto Madero from 2015 to 2017. That race gave a generation of local fans their first look at a modern world-series event. It is also part of why talk of a Formula 1 return gets the reaction it does.
