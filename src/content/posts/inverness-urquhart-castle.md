## A ruin on a headland

The road gives you almost nothing. From the A82 you see a car park and a low visitor centre set into the slope. The castle stays out of sight until you're through the building and standing above the water.

Then the ruin opens out below you. Grey stone walls run the length of a rocky promontory that juts into Loch Ness, with the Grant Tower standing at the far end. Behind it the dark water stretches north and south between steep, wooded hills.

## What you're actually walking through

This is one of the most-visited castles in Scotland, and the setting explains most of that. The site has been fortified since the medieval period. The Grant family were granted it in 1509, and much of the stonework you see dates from their time. In 1692 government troops blew up the gatehouse so that Jacobites could not use it, and the castle was never rebuilt.

The visit runs in a set order:

- **The film**: a short screening inside the visitor centre ends with the screen lifting away to show the castle through the window. Catch it before you head down.
- **The path down**: a sloped walkway drops to the ruin. It is steady but steep in places, so allow time for the climb back up.
- **The trebuchet**: a full-size replica siege engine stands on the grass near the approach.
- **The Grant Tower**: climb the narrow stair for the highest view over the loch. It's the part of the visit people queue for.
- **The shoreline**: small paths lead down to the water at the foot of the walls.

## Getting to Drumnadrochit

The castle is on the A82, about two miles south of Drumnadrochit village and roughly 14 miles southwest of Inverness. If you're driving, it's a straight run along the loch's north shore. The car park is on site, and in the afternoon it can fill.

You don't need a car. Scottish Citylink coaches on the Inverness to Fort William route stop at the castle entrance. Several Loch Ness cruises from the Inverness end also call at the castle's own jetty, so you can arrive by water and leave by bus.

## Beating the afternoon coaches

Timing matters more here than at most castles. On weekends the quietest window is **10am to 11am**. Try not to arrive between **1pm and 4pm on weekends**; that's when coach tours and cruise passengers overlap and the tower stair backs up.

Doors open at 9:30am every day of the week and close at 4:30pm. Last entry comes before closing, so don't plan a late-afternoon drop-in. That rule holds in every season the current schedule covers.

A few habits make it smoother:

- **Book a timed ticket online** with Historic Environment Scotland, which runs the site. Prices are on its official website, and members of Historic Scotland or English Heritage should check it for their entry terms.
- **Pay by card**: the ticket desk, shop and café all handle it routinely.
- **Do the tower first** if you arrive early. Go down while the visitor centre is still quiet, then watch the film on your way out.
- **Walk away from the jetty**: when a cruise boat docks, its passengers move up from the water's edge as one group. Head to the opposite end of the site and let them pass.

## How long and what to expect

Two hours covers it comfortably: the film, the ruin, the tower and a coffee. Photographers who want the light on the water should add another half hour. The ruin is fully exposed on its headland, and wind comes straight off the loch, so pack a layer even on a mild day.

The café sits in the visitor centre with windows facing the castle. It's the obvious place to wait out a shower before you head down. Inside the ruin there's no shelter beyond the tower itself.

## Around the loch afterwards

Drumnadrochit is the natural next stop, a few minutes up the road. The village has the Loch Ness Centre, which covers the loch's geology and the long history of Nessie hunting. You'll also find places to eat and the start of local walks into Glen Urquhart.

Keep driving south on the A82 and you reach Fort Augustus at the loch's southern tip. There the Caledonian Canal climbs through a flight of locks in the middle of the village. Heading back north, Inverness and the River Ness are about half an hour away, so the castle fits neatly into a half-day loop.
