## A plaza built for millions

The plaza is enormous, and it has to be. It is called the Plaza Mariana, and the space is laid out for the millions of pilgrims who reach this corner of northern Mexico City every year. Families walk in carrying framed images of the Virgin. Some pilgrims cross the last stretch of stone on their knees.

Around the edge you'll see the history of the shrine in stone and concrete. The circular modern basilica, with its sweeping green roof, sits beside the older baroque church. That older church leans visibly because it has sunk into the soft lakebed soil. Behind both rises Tepeyac hill. Tradition holds that the Virgin appeared to Juan Diego here in 1531.

## Inside the new basilica

The modern church was designed by architect Pedro Ramírez Vázquez and finished in 1976. Its round, open floor lets thousands of people see the altar at the same time, with no columns in the way. The roof slopes down like a vast tent. Mass is said many times a day, so a service may well be going on while you're inside.

The reason everyone comes hangs behind the altar. It is the tilma, Juan Diego's cloak, which carries the image of the Virgin of Guadalupe. To keep the crowds moving, you see it from moving walkways set into the floor below the altar. These walkways carry you past slowly, and there's no place to stop.

- Follow the signs down the side of the altar to reach the walkways.
- Ride them more than once if you like. People often do.
- You can't use flash, so set your phone before you get on.

## The Old Basilica and the hill

Walk back across the plaza to the Antigua Basílica. It was completed in the early 1700s and is now called the Templo Expiatorio a Cristo Rey. Stand at the door and you can feel the floor tilt. The Capuchinas church next to it leans too, for the same reason.

From here, stairs and garden paths climb Tepeyac hill. Halfway up are sculptures of the apparition scene and waterfalls. At the top is the Capilla del Cerrito, which marks the place where the apparitions are said to have happened. The view takes in the plaza and the sprawl of the north of the city.

On the way down, look for the Capilla del Pocito. This small 18th-century baroque chapel has domes covered in blue and white Talavera tile, and it's one of the finest pieces of architecture in the whole complex.

## Moving through a working shrine

This is a working place of worship before it is a sight, and the people around you are mostly pilgrims. The basilica draws very large crowds all year. Plan your visit around that.

- **Dress for a church.** Shoulders covered and no hats inside is the norm.
- **Keep the aisles clear.** If you see pilgrims on their knees, give them room. Don't photograph them up close.
- **Entry to the basilica needs no ticket.** The Museo de la Basílica, with its walls of painted ex-voto thank-you panels, has its own entrance. Its price is posted there.
- **Go early on a weekday.** Arriving early usually puts you ahead of the tour groups and the big parish pilgrimages, which tend to fill weekends.
- **Watch your belongings.** Packed crowds attract pickpockets, especially on the walkways and at the Metro.

Stalls along the approach streets sell rosaries, candles and Guadalupe images. Many pilgrims get theirs blessed after Mass. Small vendors often take cash only, so carry some pesos.

## Getting to La Villa

The basilica stands at Fray Juan de Zumárraga No. 2, in the Villa Gustavo A. Madero district. The simplest route is Metro Line 6 to La Villa-Basílica station. From there it's a short, signposted walk with the crowds.

If you're coming from the historic centre, take Line 3 north to Deportivo 18 de Marzo and change to Line 6 there. Taxis and ride-hailing apps work too, but traffic on the northern avenues can be slow at rush hour.

## Timing around 12 December

The feast of the Virgin of Guadalupe on 12 December is the biggest day here. Pilgrims walk in from across Mexico for days beforehand. On the night of 11 December, crowds sing the traditional *Las Mañanitas* to the Virgin. Seeing it is unforgettable, but the plaza and nearby streets become extremely packed.

If you want room to look around, come any other time of year and give yourself a full morning. Two to three hours is enough for the basilica, the Old Basilica, the hill chapels and a stop at the Capilla del Pocito.
