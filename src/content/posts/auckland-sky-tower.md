## A Needle Over the Harbour City

Auckland is a low-slung city, so the tower stands out. From most of the isthmus you can see the concrete shaft and its pod, 328 metres to the tip. It opened in 1997 and is still the tallest freestanding structure in the Southern Hemisphere. Locals use it as a compass: if you can see it, you know which way downtown is.

It stands at the corner of Victoria Street West and Federal Street. It belongs to the SkyCity complex, which also holds the casino, hotels and a row of restaurants. You go in at street level through a ground-floor lobby. A lift then carries you up the shaft in well under a minute, and a glass panel in its floor shows the shaft dropping away beneath you.

## What You See From 186 Metres

Your first stop is the main observation level at 186 metres. Floor-to-ceiling windows run all the way round, and some sections of the floor are thick glass. Through them you look straight down onto the roofs and traffic of Victoria Street.

On a clear day you can see for about 80 kilometres. Go round the windows and pick out:

- **Waitematā Harbour** and the arch of the **Auckland Harbour Bridge** to the north
- **Rangitoto Island**, the symmetrical volcanic cone guarding the Hauraki Gulf
- **Maungawhau / Mount Eden** and **Maungakiekie / One Tree Hill**, two of the city's many volcanic cones, to the south
- The yachts in **Viaduct Harbour**, almost directly below

Above that is the Sky Deck at 220 metres, the highest public level. It is smaller and quieter, with seamless glass that makes the panorama feel less framed. Allow an hour to circle both levels properly, more if you stop at the information panels or the café.

## The Ledge at 192 Metres

This is the part people come back talking about. Two adventure activities run from a level just above the main deck. Both are booked separately from standard entry.

**SkyWalk** sends you out onto a ledge around the outside of the pod. It is about 1.2 metres wide, with no handrail. You wear a full-body harness clipped to an overhead line, and a guide coaxes you into leaning out over the city. It takes a little over an hour, including kit-up.

**SkyJump** is the one the editorial blurbs call bungee. Strictly, it is a wire-guided base jump: you drop 192 metres on a cable at up to about 85 kilometres per hour. Guide wires keep you from swinging, and you land on a target at the base, beside the street, where friends can watch you come down.

Watching from inside is free entertainment of its own. Jumpers fall past the observation windows in a blur of orange jumpsuit.

## Dinner That Turns

The tower's dining sits in the pod. The best known is **Orbit 360° Dining**, a revolving restaurant that makes a slow full turn while you eat, roughly once an hour. That is long enough for the view to change from the harbour bridge to the volcanic cones between courses.

The pricing is on the high end. The appeal is the window table, not a bargain. Booking ahead is essential for dinner, and you should ask about any dress expectations when you reserve. A meal booking usually includes your ride up, so it can replace a separate deck ticket. Confirm that when you book.

## Getting Up Without the Queue

A few habits make the visit smoother here:

- **Buy tickets online first.** The ticket desk in the lobby is where waiting happens. Current prices and any combo deals with the SkyWalk or SkyJump are on the official Sky Tower site.
- **Check the weather and cloud base, not just the sun.** Low cloud can wrap the pod completely. If the top of the tower is hidden when you look up from Queen Street, wait.
- **Arrive early or go in the evening.** Reasoning from how the city runs, the morning opening tends to beat the cruise-ship and tour-coach groups. Weekday evenings tend to be calmer than weekend afternoons.
- **Pay by card.** Contactless works everywhere in the complex. Tipping isn't expected in New Zealand, though you can leave something for exceptional service at Orbit.
- **Expect security screening** for SkyWalk and SkyJump. Loose items such as phones, sunglasses and hats are locked away, because nothing may fall onto the street.

## Timing the Light

Auckland's sunset moves a long way through the year. In midwinter it comes around 5pm, so a late-afternoon visit gives you daylight, dusk and the city lights coming on in one trip. In midsummer the sun sets late, close to the tower's current closing times. Check the official site for this month's hours and last-entry time before planning around sunset.

From outside, the tower itself is worth watching after dark. The shaft and pod are lit in colours that change for holidays, sporting events and causes. The view from Albert Park or the waterfront is often better than you'd expect.

## Around the Base

You are in the middle of downtown, so the tower is easy to combine with other stops. **Federal Street**, at its foot, is a short pedestrianised dining strip. **Auckland Art Gallery Toi o Tāmaki** and the old trees of **Albert Park** are about a ten-minute walk east.

Walk downhill and you reach **Viaduct Harbour** and **Wynyard Quarter**, with their waterfront bars and the marina you just saw from above. The main rail and ferry hub, **Waitematā (Britomart) station**, is roughly a 10–15 minute walk away at the bottom of Queen Street. From there, ferries cross to Devonport and out to Rangitoto, if the view gave you ideas.
