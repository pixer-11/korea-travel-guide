## A museum about you

This museum's subject is the visitor's own body. The permanent exhibition is built as an adventure into the human body, the self, thought and feeling. You move through it as a series of themed rooms rather than a timeline of objects behind glass.

Its home is a long, pale 1930 building by the architect Wilhelm Kreis on Lingnerplatz. It looks like a government ministry and holds displays on digestion, sex, ageing and death. That contrast is part of the appeal. The building also has a darker past, and the museum tells that history openly, which most science museums never have to do.

It is one of Dresden's most-reviewed museums and consistently well rated. Expect company, especially on weekends and during school holidays.

## The Transparent Man

The museum's best-known exhibit is a life-size human figure you can see straight through. The Transparent Man (*Der Gläserne Mensch*) was first shown here in 1930. His skin is a clear shell, and inside it the skeleton, organs, nerves and blood vessels are laid out in their real positions.

He became an export product. The museum built further transparent figures, including a Transparent Woman, and sent them to exhibitions around the world. For decades they shaped how ordinary people pictured their insides. The figure in Dresden is a design object as much as a medical model, and it still pulls a crowd.

You will find him inside the permanent exhibition. Many visitors photograph him and walk on. Stay a few minutes and look at how the circulatory system is threaded through the body. That detail is what made the figure famous.

## Walking through "Adventure Human"

The permanent exhibition is called *Abenteuer Mensch*, or "Adventure Human". It is the core of any visit. Its rooms go beyond plain anatomy and cover how bodies are fed, made, aged and remembered:

- **The transparent human**: the glass figures and the history of putting the body on display
- **Living and dying**: life cycles, ageing and death, treated without euphemism
- **Eating and drinking**: digestion, nutrition and what culture does to appetite
- **Sexuality**: frank and explicit, so look ahead if you are with younger children
- **Remembering, thinking, learning**: the brain, memory and perception
- **Movement**: muscles, bones and how the body is used
- **Beauty, skin and hair**: appearance, grooming and the norms around them

Most rooms combine real historical objects with interactive stations. You might weigh things, test your senses or listen to recordings. Pace yourself. The people who come out disappointed are usually the ones who rushed the first room and ran out of energy by the fourth.

The museum also runs large temporary exhibitions alongside the permanent rooms. Check what is on before you go, because a strong special show can easily add an hour.

## The Five Senses: the children's floor

The kids' section is the Children's Museum, themed around the five senses. It is built for touching. Children test sight, hearing, smell, taste and touch at hands-on stations, and nobody asks them to stand back from a display case.

It works best for roughly primary-school ages. Older children often prefer the main exhibition. A family can split up easily: one adult stays with the younger ones among the senses while the other goes through *Abenteuer Mensch*, then you swap.

## Built on mouthwash money

The museum began with a businessman, not a doctor. Karl August Lingner made his fortune from Odol mouthwash and organised the International Hygiene Exhibition in Dresden in 1911. Its success led him to found the museum in 1912. The square outside, Lingnerplatz, carries his name.

The aim was public health education, explaining the body to ordinary people. Under National Socialism the institution was turned to "racial hygiene" propaganda. That history is part of what the museum presents today rather than something it hides.

After the war, in East Germany, the museum carried on producing teaching models and transparent figures. Knowing this backstory changes how the glass figures read. They are anatomy lessons, and they are also evidence of who got to define a "healthy" body.

## Getting to Lingnerplatz

The museum is at Lingnerplatz 1, on the southeast edge of Dresden's old town. The Großer Garten, the city's largest park, begins right behind it.

- **On foot from the Altstadt**: from the Frauenkirche or the Altmarkt it is an easy walk southeast, roughly a quarter of an hour, past the Rathaus.
- **From Dresden Hauptbahnhof**: the main station is also within walking distance, heading east.
- **By tram**: Dresden's transport operator, DVB, has a stop named after the museum, "Deutsches Hygiene-Museum". Check the DVB journey planner for the current lines.

Use the German name when you ask for directions or search maps: *Deutsches Hygiene-Museum*. Locals usually just say "das Hygiene-Museum".

## Timing the doors and the Großer Garten

The schedule is simple, but it catches people out. The museum is **closed every Monday**. From Tuesday to Sunday it opens from 10am to 5pm. If you only have a Monday in Dresden, plan this visit for another trip.

Do not treat 5pm as an arrival deadline. With the permanent exhibition, the Transparent Man and the children's floor, you realistically need two to three hours. Arriving after about 2pm means rushing the later rooms. Arriving close to 10am gives you the full day, and an early start on a weekday usually beats the weekend family crowd.

A few habits make the visit smoother:

- **Leave big bags and coats at the entrance.** German museums generally expect this, and gallery staff will send you back if you bring a backpack in.
- **Ask about English material at the desk.** The exhibition is presented in German, and English support is available for international visitors.
- **Check the current ticket prices on the official museum website or at the ticket desk.** Do the same for combined family tickets and special-exhibition entry before you set out.
- **Keep your voice down in the heavier rooms.** The sections on death and on the museum's Nazi-era history are meant to be read slowly.

Afterwards, go straight out into the Großer Garten. Its long avenues and the Baroque Palais in the middle are the obvious way to clear your head after three hours of organs and nerves. The two together make a natural half-day on the quieter side of the old town.
