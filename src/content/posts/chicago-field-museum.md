## Why go

The moment you step through the north doors into Stanley Field Hall, the scale of the place hits you before you've read a single label. Skylights three stories up throw pale light down onto a cast of a titanosaur skeleton stretched almost the length of a football field, its neck curving up toward a mezzanine lined with totem poles. This is the Field Museum's great trick: it doesn't ease you in, it opens with a room built to make you feel small. Somewhere off to the side, in her own gallery now, is Sue — the largest, most complete T. rex skeleton ever found, 40 feet of fossil that the museum bought at auction in the 1990s and has anchored its identity around ever since. That single specimen, plus the sheer range of what's under this roof — Egyptian mummies, an underground "Underground Adventure" bug tunnel, taxidermied lions, meteorites, gemstones — is why the museum pulls well over 30,000 visitor reviews online with a rating that hovers near the top of the scale for big-city natural history museums.

## Getting there

The museum sits at 1400 S Lake Shore Drive, on Chicago's Museum Campus, sharing the lakefront green space with the Shedd Aquarium and Adler Planetarium. If you're downtown, the free Museum Campus/CTA #146 bus or the #130 (seasonal) will drop you close to the entrance; by car, there's a large paid parking garage right on the campus, though on warm weekends it fills fast. The Roosevelt Road Metra stop and the CTA Red/Orange/Green Line's Roosevelt station are both about a 15–20 minute walk east across the park — pleasant in good weather, less so in a January wind off the lake, so budget a rideshare if it's cold.

## What to see

Give Sue real time — the skeleton has its own second-floor gallery now with context on how she was discovered in South Dakota and prepared for display. From there, the Egypt exhibit on the ground floor recreates a mastaba tomb you actually walk through, dim and low-ceilinged, with two dozen real mummies in the surrounding cases. Upstairs, the Grainger Hall of Gems and the meteorite collection reward a slower pace — cases of raw and cut stones lit like jewelry-store windows. Families gravitate to the Crown Family PlayLab and the walk-through "Underground Adventure," where the corridors are scaled up so you feel insect-sized among oversized roots and beetles. Set aside at least three hours for a first visit, four if you want the special ticketed exhibit that rotates through the west galleries — check what's currently showing before you go, since these change throughout the year.

## When to go

Crowd patterns here are fairly predictable. On both weekdays and weekends, the calmest window is right at opening through roughly midday — specifically 9am to 6pm sees steadier, lighter traffic on weekdays, and the same holds true early on weekends. The crunch comes on weekends between 11am and 4pm, when school groups, families, and tour buses converge at once and the main hall and Sue's gallery both bottleneck. If your schedule allows, a weekday morning is the single best time to see the big-ticket exhibits without shuffling behind a crowd.

## How to visit like a local

Buy tickets online before you arrive — the general admission line at the door can be slow, and a phone ticket lets you walk straight to the entrance turnstile. Most exhibits accept tap-to-pay and mobile wallets throughout the building, including at the café counters, so you rarely need cash. Skip the coat check line by traveling light in warmer months; in winter, checking a heavy coat is worth the few minutes since the galleries run warm. The mistake most first-time visitors make is trying to see everything in one sweep — the building is genuinely enormous, spanning four public levels, so pick two or three halls (Sue, Egypt, and one hall of your choice) rather than racing corridor to corridor. If you're visiting more than one Museum Campus attraction in a day, the combined City Pass-style tickets can save money, but confirm current pricing and what's bundled before you buy, since offers change.
