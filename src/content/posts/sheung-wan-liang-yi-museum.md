Most visitors walking Hollywood Road are chasing antique shops and the smell of dried seafood drifting up from Des Voeux Road. Liang Yi Museum sits behind an unmarked, almost fortress-like facade at 181-199 Hollywood Rd, and unless you already know it's there, you'll walk straight past it.

That anonymity is deliberate. This is a private museum, founded by collector Peggy Lee, and it doesn't chase footfall the way its neighbours do. Inside, four floors hold one of the world's more significant private collections of classical Chinese furniture, alongside a separate collection of European gold and silver vanity cases from the early 20th century.

## Why this museum runs differently

Liang Yi isn't a drop-in attraction, and that's the first thing to understand before you plan a visit. It's open Monday to Friday, 10am to 6pm, and closed entirely on Saturdays and Sundays, which rules out the museum for anyone treating this as a weekend Hollywood Road stroll.

Entry is by appointment only, arranged in advance through the museum, not bought at a door. There's no ticket counter to queue at, no signage shouting for attention, because the museum was never built for casual walk-ins.

## Booking your visit

Because it operates on private-museum rules, you need to plan this one before you land in Hong Kong, not after.

- Contact the museum through its official website or by email to request a visit
- Visits typically run as guided tours at set times, with group sizes kept deliberately small
- Confirm current hours and tour slots before you go, since a private museum can shift its schedule with little public notice
- Build your Hollywood Road day around the confirmed slot, since walk-ins on the day are unlikely to be accommodated

## The furniture collection

The core of Liang Yi is Ming and Qing dynasty furniture, much of it made from huanghuali and zitan, dense hardwoods prized for centuries in Chinese cabinetry. These are the woods that give classical Chinese pieces their weight and near-black sheen, and seeing them assembled at this scale, rather than as a single piece in a hotel lobby, is the point of the visit.

Cabinets, daybeds, and scholar's chairs are arranged so you can walk around them, not just view them from behind rope lines. The joinery on these pieces was built without nails or glue, using mortise-and-tenon construction refined over hundreds of years, and the museum's lighting is set up specifically to let you see those joins.

## Where East meets vanity case

The second collection, upstairs, is a hard swerve from Ming dynasty hardwood: European vanity cases, mostly gold and enamel, mostly early 1900s. It sounds like an odd pairing on paper, but it reflects the founder's own two collecting passions rather than an attempt at a unified theme.

These cases were made by houses like Cartier and Van Cleef & Arpels for a clientele that treated a compact, jewelled case as both tool and status object. Fitted interiors held powder, lipstick, a comb, sometimes a tiny clock, all behind an enamelled or gem-set lid. It's a smaller, glinting counterpoint to the dark hardwood downstairs, and most visitors say the contrast is what stays with them.

## Getting there through Sheung Wan

Liang Yi sits on Hollywood Road between Aberdeen Street and Wa In Fong, in the stretch of Sheung Wan known for its antique dealers. The nearest MTR stop is Sheung Wan Station, roughly a 10 to 12 minute walk uphill via Cochrane Street or Ladder Street.

Central Station, on the Island and Tsuen Wan lines, is a similar walking distance if you're coming the other way. Taxis and ride-hailing apps can drop you directly on Hollywood Road, which is worth doing if you're pressed for time before your appointment.

Plan roughly 1 to 1.5 hours for a full visit, more if the tour runs at a leisurely pace through both floors. Since the museum is closed weekends, pair a weekday visit with nearby Man Mo Temple or the antique shops that line Hollywood Road and Upper Lascar Row, both an easy walk away.

## Visiting without disrupting the quiet

Liang Yi's under-the-radar status isn't an accident, and it's worth respecting once you're inside. Because tours run in small groups on a fixed schedule, arriving late can mean missing the group entirely rather than just catching up.

Photography policies can be restrictive given the value and fragility of the pieces, so ask your guide before raising a phone or camera. Bags may need to be checked or minimised before you enter the galleries, since visitors move closely past furniture with no barriers.

Dress and behave as you would in a private home rather than a public gallery: this is someone's collection, displayed on their terms, and the small scale of each visit is what makes it worth the advance planning.
