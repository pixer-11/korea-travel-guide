Calle Ignacio Allende narrows as it runs south from the Frida Kahlo Museum, and the market fills most of a block along it. Step under the roof and the light drops. Aisles run in a grid, stalls are packed shoulder to shoulder, and painted signs hang above each counter. The place has been trading since 1921. It feels like a working neighbourhood market that also happens to feed a lot of visitors, not like a tourist attraction.

## Two Floors Under One Roof

The market spreads across two storeys. Ground-level aisles carry most of the everyday trade: fruit pyramids, chiles sold by weight, butchers, flower sellers and the food counters people come for.

Further in, the stalls turn to apparel, artesanía and souvenirs. Expect embroidered blouses, alebrijes, lucha libre masks and Frida-themed everything, a nod to the neighbour up the road. Give yourself at least an hour if you want to eat and browse. Plan on 30 minutes if you only want tostadas.

This is one of the most-reviewed markets in Mexico City and it is consistently well rated. So don't expect to discover it. It is busy, and that business is part of what keeps the food turning over fast.

## The Tostada Counters

Tostadas are the reason most people find their way here. A tostada is a flat, fried corn tortilla, crisp enough to shatter, piled high with toppings. The best-known counter is Tostadas Coyoacán, and the stools along it are often all taken.

The format is simple. Trays of toppings sit lined up behind the counter, and you point or name what you want. Classic choices include:

- **Tinga de pollo**: shredded chicken in a chipotle and tomato sauce
- **Pata**: pickled pig's trotter, tangy and soft
- **Ceviche** and **camarón**: seafood options, lime-bright
- **Pulpo**: octopus
- **Mole**: chicken under dark, complex sauce

Most tostadas come finished with crema, crumbled cheese and lettuce. Salsas sit on the counter for you to add. Eat over the plate and lean forward. A tostada breaks the moment you bite it, and nobody here pretends otherwise.

## Beyond the Tostadas

Other counters are worth a pass once the tostada is done. Look for quesadilla and antojito stalls where tortillas are pressed and griddled to order. Juice and agua fresca stands sell glasses of jamaica (hibiscus), horchata and seasonal fruit blends.

Snack sellers in and around the market sell esquites, which is corn off the cob served in a cup with mayonnaise, lime and chile. These are a standard Coyoacán street snack.

Seasons change the stock as well. In the weeks before Día de Muertos (1–2 November), Mexico City markets fill with sugar skulls, papel picado and marigolds. Around December, look for piñatas and posada supplies.

## Ordering at the Counter

Market food stalls in Mexico City follow a rhythm of their own. Here is how to slot in:

- **Grab a free stool, then order.** Nobody hands out menus. The vendor will ask "¿Qué le damos?" ("What can we get you?"). Reply with the number and the topping, as in "Una de tinga, por favor."
- **Say "con todo"** if you want the standard garnish. Say "sin crema" or "sin picante" if you don't.
- **Pay at the end.** At most counters you tell them what you ate when you're done. Saying "¿Cuánto es?" settles it.
- **Carry pesos in small notes.** Some larger stalls take cards, but cash in small denominations is what keeps things moving. Check prices on the boards or ask before you sit.
- **Tipping is modest and optional** at counter stools. Leaving your coins is a friendly gesture.
- **Give up your stool once you've eaten** when people are waiting behind you. Turnover here is quick.

In craft aisles, polite haggling over several items is normal. Prices on food are fixed.

## Timing Your Visit Around the Hours

The market opens at 7am every day. From Thursday to Sunday it closes at 6pm. Wednesday is shorter, with a 5pm close. Monday and Tuesday listings are less clear, so assume daytime trading and plan for mid-afternoon at the latest.

Coyoacán as a whole fills up with Mexico City families and visitors at weekends. That makes a weekday morning the sensible choice if you want a stool without circling. Food stalls take a while to set up after the doors open. Late morning is a good middle ground: the counters are fully stocked and the lunch rush hasn't started.

Many smaller stalls start packing up well before the official closing time. Don't count on a late-afternoon tostada.

## Getting There and Walking Coyoacán

The market is at Ignacio Allende s/n, in the Del Carmen part of Coyoacán. The nearest Metro stop is Coyoacán (Line 3), but it isn't close: allow a walk of around 20 to 25 minutes through residential streets. Plenty of visitors take a ride-hail car to the centre of Coyoacán instead.

What's nearby makes the walk worthwhile:

- **Museo Frida Kahlo (Casa Azul)**: about three blocks north on Allende and Londres. Tickets sell out, so book ahead on the museum's official site.
- **Jardín Centenario**: a few blocks south, with its coyote fountain and café terraces.
- **Plaza Hidalgo and the Parroquia de San Juan Bautista**: right next to the Jardín, with a 16th-century church on the square.
- **Museo Casa de León Trotsky**: a longer walk north-east, worth adding if you have the afternoon.

## Building a Coyoacán Half-Day

The market fits neatly into a walking loop. Book a morning entry at the Casa Azul, then walk down Allende to the market for tostadas. Go on to the Jardín Centenario for coffee and a churro under the trees.

That order puts you at the market around lunchtime, when the food counters are in full swing. You also end in the plazas, where street performers and weekend craft stalls collect. If you'd rather shop than queue, flip it around. Start at the market as it opens, then pick up the museum later in the day.
