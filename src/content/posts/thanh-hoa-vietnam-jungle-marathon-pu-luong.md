Limestone towers rise straight out of rice paddies. Thai and Muong stilt houses sit on the slopes between them, and a single valley road follows the Luong river below. This is the course of the Vietnam Jungle Marathon: Pu Luong, a nature reserve in the western hills of Thanh Hoa province where the path is often dirt, stone or a raised paddy wall.

## What the race is

It is a trail race, not a road marathon. Most of the distance is on footpaths through the Pu Luong Nature Reserve.

The event belongs to the Vietnam Trail Series and runs with the support of the Thanh Hoa Provincial People's Committee. That backing matters out here. Village roads, reserve trails and aid points all depend on cooperation from the local authorities and communes along the route.

The series organiser publishes the race date, distance options, cut-offs and registration links on its official site. This guide gives no day of its own. The exact edition date comes from the organiser.

## The ground under your shoes

Pu Luong has been a protected reserve since 1999. It straddles Ba Thuoc and Quan Hoa districts and covers a little under 17,000 hectares of karst ridges, forest and farmed valleys.

That landscape decides how the race feels. Expect:

- **Steep, short climbs** up limestone spurs, often on stone steps or rooty forest paths
- **Paddy-wall running**, narrow and exposed, where a wrong step means mud
- **Village lanes** of packed earth or concrete between stilt houses
- **Stream and river crossings** in the lower valleys

Limestone turns slick after rain, and the forest sections hold their moisture. Trail runners here usually wear shoes with an aggressive lug, and many carry poles for the climbs.

## Water wheels and terraces: the Pu Luong signature

The reserve is famous for two things, and both sit close to the trail.

The first is its bamboo water wheels. Tall wheels lashed together from bamboo turn in the Luong river and its streams, lifting water into irrigation channels. The best-known cluster stands near Ban Cong village, a short walk from the main valley road.

The second is the rice terraces. They step down the hillsides around villages such as Ban Hieu, Kho Muong and Ban Uon. Their colour depends on the season: water-filled after planting, bright green as the rice grows, and gold in the run-up to harvest, which usually falls in late May–June and again around September–October.

## Getting to Pu Luong

There is no airport or railway inside the reserve. Everything ends on a mountain road.

- **From Hanoi:** about 160–180 km, usually 4–5 hours by car or minibus, often routed via Mai Chau in Hoa Binh province. Most homestays arrange private transfers or shared vans.
- **From Thanh Hoa city:** the city sits on the North–South railway, and Tho Xuan Airport serves the province with domestic flights. From either one, the road climbs west toward Ba Thuoc in roughly 3 hours.
- **Within the reserve:** motorbike taxis (xe ôm) and lodge vehicles cover the gaps between villages. There is no public transport worth relying on.

Runners from Hanoi typically travel up the day before the race, because the road is slow and the final stretch winds through the hills.

## Sleeping on stilts in Ba Thuoc

Accommodation in Pu Luong means homestays and small eco-lodges, not hotels. Most are clustered in Ba Thuoc district, near villages such as Ban Don, Ban Hieu and Ban Kho Muong.

A traditional Thai stilt house has one large wooden room upstairs. Mattresses and mosquito nets are laid out along the floor, and the space below is used for storage, animals or a family's motorbikes. Meals are usually shared trays of rice, local greens, stream fish and pork. Some newer lodges add private bungalows and pools looking over the paddies.

On a race weekend, beds near the start fill with runners and their families. Many participants stay two or three nights to see the valley without a bib on.

## Stilt-house manners and race-weekend habits

Pu Luong runs on village rhythms, and runners who fit in have a better weekend.

- **Shoes off at the ladder.** Stilt houses are entered barefoot. Muddy trail shoes stay on the ground floor.
- **Cash is how the valley pays.** Small homestays, roadside stalls and xe ôm drivers mostly deal in Vietnamese đồng notes. ATMs are scarce in the reserve, so visitors usually withdraw money in Hanoi or Thanh Hoa city.
- **Rice wine comes with dinner.** Hosts often pour rượu cần or corn wine at the evening meal. A polite single toast is normal, and on the night before a race "một chén thôi" ("just one cup") is understood.
- **Greet the villages you run through.** The course crosses people's front yards and fields. A "xin chào" to someone in a paddy costs you nothing.
- **Mind the livestock and the crops.** Buffalo, dogs and chickens share the trails. Runners keep to the marked line rather than cutting across planted terraces.

Mornings in the valley start very early, with roosters and motorbikes well before dawn. Many runners simply go to bed when the homestay does.

## Before and after the start line

Pu Luong has plenty for the days around the race, and most of it is on foot.

- **Ban Hieu waterfall:** a series of travertine pools below a Thai village, popular for a soak after a hard day on the legs.
- **Kho Muong cave:** a short walk from Kho Muong village, with stalactite chambers and a Muong settlement on the approach.
- **Pho Doan market:** a weekly highland market where ethnic minority communities trade produce, cloth and livestock.
- **The Ban Cong water wheels:** easy to reach by bicycle along the valley road.

Lodges rent bicycles and arrange local guides for treks between villages. For runners, these walks are a gentle way to shake out their legs on the same kind of trails they raced on.
