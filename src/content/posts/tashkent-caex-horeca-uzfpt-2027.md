Picture a hall split into aisles of booths. On one side, packaging machinery sits under spotlights. On the other, there are tasting counters and stacks of branded cartons. That is the basic layout of CAEx Horeca & UzFPT 2027. It combines a food products show with a fair for packaging, processing technology and the HoReCa trade (hotels, restaurants, cafés) under one roof in Tashkent.

## What the fair covers

It runs for three days, February 4–6, 2027. This is the 3rd edition of the Specialized International Exhibition of Food Products, Packaging, Processing Technologies, and the HoReCa Industry. The full title tells you who it is for. UzFPT covers the production side: food products, packaging and processing. Horeca covers the hospitality buyers who purchase from those suppliers.

That pairing matters in Uzbekistan. The country grows huge volumes of fruit, vegetables and cotton-belt produce, and drying, canning and export packaging are serious industries. A fair that brings processors and hospitality buyers into the same hall serves both ends of that chain.

The typical crowd includes:

- Food manufacturers and distributors looking for regional buyers
- Packaging and processing equipment suppliers
- Restaurant, hotel and café owners sourcing kit and ingredients
- Importers scouting the Central Asian market

## The venue: CAEx Exhibition Center

The fair is held at the CAEx Exhibition Center, a dedicated trade-show venue in Tashkent. The name stands for Central Asia Expo. Exhibition centres like this one use a large open hall layout. Each stand is numbered on a floor plan, which you pick up at registration.

Most international visitors fly into Tashkent International Airport, the main air gateway to Uzbekistan. In the city, taxis booked through apps such as Yandex Go are the usual way to reach the venue, and drivers know CAEx by name. The Tashkent Metro is cheap and fast for crossing the centre, and many of its stations are lavishly decorated.

## Tickets, registration and timings

Trade fairs in Tashkent generally register visitors by badge, not by selling public tickets. The organiser publishes visitor registration, exhibitor lists and daily opening times on its official channels. Prices and hours are not part of what we know about this edition, so we have left them out here.

For professionals, the first morning usually brings the official opening and the busiest round of meetings. The final day tends to be quieter on the floor. Many exhibitors use it to finish deals and pack down.

## Doing business on the show floor

Uzbek business culture is warm, and it moves at the pace of a conversation. Expect a firm handshake between men. With women, it is normal to wait and see whether she offers her hand first. Some people greet with a hand placed over the heart, and returning that gesture goes down well.

Some practical points:

- **Language:** Russian is the common business language, and Uzbek is the state language. Exhibitors bring interpreters or bilingual staff when they are serious about the market.
- **Follow-up:** Telegram is the dominant messaging app in Uzbekistan. Contacts swap Telegram handles as often as email addresses.
- **Tea:** being offered tea (choy) at a stand is an invitation to sit and talk, not a formality to refuse.
- **Payment:** the currency is the Uzbek som. Cards work at hotels and larger restaurants, while bazaars and small cafés lean on cash.

## February in Tashkent

February is Tashkent's winter. Days are cold and grey, often hovering just above freezing, and there are occasional rain or wet snow spells. Exhibition halls are heated, but taxi queues outside are not. Visitors typically carry a proper coat and leave it at the cloakroom.

Winter is also low season for leisure tourism. Hotels near the centre are less stretched than in spring or autumn, when the Silk Road crowds arrive.

## Eating like it's your trade

For a food-industry visitor, the city itself is part of the research. Chorsu Bazaar sits under a large blue-green dome in the old town. It has lanes of spices, dried apricots, raisins, nuts and the round, stamped non bread sold from stacked trays. It is the clearest picture of what Uzbekistan produces and how it is sold.

For lunch, plov is the dish to study. The Central Asian Plov Centre, also called Besh Qozon, near the Tashkent TV Tower, cooks it in enormous kazans over fire. Plov is traditionally a midday meal, and it is best before the pots run low. Samsa from tandoor ovens and lagman noodles fill the gaps between meetings.
