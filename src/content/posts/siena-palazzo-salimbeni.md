Most people walking Via Banchi di Sopra glance right, catch a wall of dark stone battlements rising above a quiet square, and keep moving toward the Campo. That's the mistake. Palazzo Salimbeni is one of the few places in Siena where you can stand still for a few minutes and feel the medieval banking city rather than just the tourist one.

The palace is the headquarters of Monte dei Paschi di Siena, founded in 1472 and generally considered the oldest bank in the world still operating today. The building itself is older still: a fortified 14th-century noble residence, later remodeled in a neo-Gothic style in the 19th century to give it the crenellated, castle-like silhouette it has now. Brick, stone trim, narrow mullioned windows, the kind of severe civic architecture Siena does better than almost anywhere in Tuscany.

## The square, not the interior

This is worth saying plainly: Palazzo Salimbeni is a working bank headquarters, not a museum you walk into. There's no general public entry to wander the halls.

What you're visiting is Piazza Salimbeni, the enclosed square the palace anchors along with two adjoining palazzi, Palazzo Tantucci and Palazzo Spannocchi. Together they form one continuous, harmonious facade that's rare in a city built organically over centuries.

In the middle stands a 19th-century statue of Sallustio Bandini, the Sienese economist, positioned so it's framed by the palace battlements behind it. It's a compact, self-contained scene: good light in the morning, almost no crowds even at Siena's busiest times of year.

## Why the hours look strange

The listed opening hours, 9:00am to 12:30pm every day, throw people. This isn't a sight with a ticket booth and a closing time; the square is a public piazza that's always physically accessible, but that 9am to 12:30pm window is when the space feels "open" in the sense of being an active part of the streetscape.

Shops along Banchi di Sopra open, foot traffic through the square picks up, and the light hits the facade at its best angle. Come after lunch and the same buildings feel shuttered and dead, since this is when Sienese businesses traditionally close for several hours.

Plan around the same rule you'd use for any Sienese piazza: mornings work, mid-afternoon doesn't.

## Reading the facade

Spend your ten minutes actually looking rather than just photographing. A few details reward it:

- The crenellations along the roofline are Guelph-style, square-cut rather than swallow-tailed, a small but genuine clue to medieval Sienese political allegiance.
- The ground floor windows are noticeably heavier and more fortress-like than the finer upper stories, a holdover from the building's original defensive purpose.
- Look for the Monte dei Paschi crest near the entrance, a reminder this is still a functioning institution and not a restored relic.
- The join between Palazzo Salimbeni and the two flanking palazzi is subtle; it takes a second look to see where one building ends and the next begins.

## Bandini in the square

The statue itself is a small piece of Sienese civic pride. Sallustio Bandini was an 18th-century economist and priest whose writing on free trade and agrarian reform predates much of the Enlightenment economic canon usually credited to others.

Putting him in front of a bank headquarters is not an accident. The pairing of economist and bank makes the square one of the few spots in the city center that's explicitly about Siena's financial history rather than its religious or artistic one, which is most of what the rest of the town offers.

## Fitting it into a Banchi di Sopra walk

Palazzo Salimbeni earns its place as a five-to-ten-minute stop on the way somewhere else rather than a standalone destination. It sits directly on Via Banchi di Sopra, the pedestrian shopping street that runs from Piazza del Campo up toward the Basilica di San Domenico and the house of Saint Catherine of Siena.

Coming from the Campo, it's a flat, easy walk of about five to seven minutes, no stairs, unlike much of the rest of hilly central Siena. Combine it with:

- A coffee at one of the bars along Banchi di Sopra before continuing north
- The walk up to Basilica di San Domenico, another ten minutes on foot
- A loop back down toward the Duomo if you're doing a full old-town circuit

## Photographing the palace

Photography is the main activity here, and the square rewards a bit of technique. Because the piazza is enclosed on three sides, midday sun gets blocked by the buildings themselves for long stretches, so morning light angled from the east tends to hit the facade more evenly than you'd expect in an open piazza.

Stand near the statue and shoot back toward the palace to get Bandini in the foreground with the crenellations behind him. Try the opposite angle too, from the mouth of Banchi di Sopra, which compresses all three palazzi into a single unbroken wall of stone, the effect that makes this square distinctive in the first place.

Since this is a well-known stop on the standard old-town walking route rather than a quiet back corner, expect some company even during the mornings the square works best; it's rarely crowded the way the Campo gets, but you won't have it entirely to yourself.
