Diego Alary made his name on Top Chef, but SaltoBar is the first place in Paris that's entirely his. It sits in the 11th arrondissement, a district better known lately for natural wine bars and chef-driven openings than for grand dining rooms, and this one has quickly become one of the names people trade at dinner parties.

The kitchen works around fire. That's the whole premise: contemporary Iberian cooking, pulling from both Spain and Portugal, built on live-flame technique rather than the usual battery of induction burners and combi ovens. Expect the char, smoke and blistering that only direct heat gives food, applied to a menu that draws on two coastlines rather than one.

## What makes it stand out

This isn't a quiet neighbourhood bistro that happened to get noticed. SaltoBar arrived as one of a handful of chef-led openings that have defined the current wave of buzzy Paris restaurants, the kind of place where the chef's television profile and cooking style both do the drawing.

- A solo debut from a chef previously known for collaborative or TV kitchens
- Fire-cooked cuisine as the technique, not just a garnish word
- Iberian focus, Spanish and Portuguese, rather than standard French bistro fare
- Located in the 11th, an arrondissement full of comparable openings to pair with a visit

## Finding it in the 11th

The 11th arrondissement is large and split between several distinct pockets, from Oberkampf's bar strip to the quieter streets near Bastille and République. Wherever exactly SaltoBar sits within it, treat the trip as an evening in that neighbourhood rather than a single stop.

Aim to arrive by metro if you can; the arrondissement is well served by several lines, and parking in this part of Paris is genuinely difficult on weekend nights. Give yourself a short walk either side of dinner to see the surrounding streets, since much of the 11th's appeal is the density of small bars and kitchens around any one address.

## Booking a table

A restaurant built around a Top Chef name in a moment when Paris is full of buzzy chef openings will not have easy walk-in availability most nights. Reserve as far ahead as the restaurant allows, and don't assume a same-day table will be there, especially Thursday through Saturday.

Confirm the current opening days and service times directly with the restaurant before you go. New openings frequently adjust their schedule in the first months, closing an extra day or shifting from dinner-only to lunch service, and nothing here should be taken as fixed.

## Ordering like it's your first time there

Fire-cooked Iberian menus tend to reward sharing rather than each diner picking a single main course. If the format allows it, order across a few smaller plates for the table so you get a spread of what the grill is doing that night rather than one dish.

Ask your server what's coming straight off the fire that evening. Live-flame kitchens often flex their offering around what's cooking well that day, and staff at a chef-led spot like this are usually glad to steer you rather than have you guess from a printed list.

## Planning around the visit

Treat SaltoBar as an anchor for an evening out in the 11th rather than a quick bite before moving on. Fire-cooked tasting-style meals with shared plates tend to run longer than a standard sit-down dinner, so build in time rather than booking something else right after.

Because this is a recent and heavily discussed opening, prices, exact hours and the shape of the menu are all things to verify close to your visit rather than rely on anything printed months earlier. A quick check of the restaurant's own channels the same week you go will save you a wasted trip.
