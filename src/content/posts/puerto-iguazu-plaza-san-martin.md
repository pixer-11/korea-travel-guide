## A square on the main avenue

Most visitors walk past this square without planning to. Plaza San Martin fills a block at Av. Victoria Aguirre 201-249, the avenue that runs through Puerto Iguazu and carries most of its traffic. Shops, cafés and travel agencies line the surrounding streets, so the plaza is the open green space in the middle of the commercial grid.

Like hundreds of squares across Argentina, it's named for General José de San Martín, the independence leader. Expect what a town plaza here usually has: paths crossing the grass, benches under the trees and people passing through on their way somewhere else. It's well known in town and well reviewed, but it's not a secret. Its value is that it's useful, central and free to walk into.

## Getting your bearings from Victoria Aguirre

The plaza works as a fixed point in a town most people only pass through on the way to the falls. Victoria Aguirre is the spine of Puerto Iguazu. If you're on it, you're never far from the square.

- **From the bus terminal:** the town's long-distance and local bus station is a few blocks away in the centre, close enough to walk while you wait for a connection.
- **From the falls:** buses back from Parque Nacional Iguazú, about 18 km away, finish at that terminal. The plaza makes an easy first stop on the way to dinner.
- **On foot after dark:** the surrounding blocks are the town's main restaurant and shopping area, so the streets around the square stay lit and in use.

Look at the ground on the paths and verges. Misiones is known for its *tierra colorada*, the iron-rich red earth, and it shows up on pavements, shoes and the hems of trousers all over town.

## The siesta lull and the evening return

Puerto Iguazu runs on a subtropical clock. Summer afternoons are hot and heavy with humidity. Many small shops in Misiones close for a long midday break and reopen in the late afternoon, which changes how the streets around the plaza feel.

In the early afternoon the avenue goes quiet, and the shade is the main reason to sit in the square. Things pick up again once businesses reopen and the heat eases toward evening. That's when the plaza works best: a cooler bench, the centre filling up, and dinner a short walk away. This is general town rhythm, not a measured crowd pattern, so treat it as a guide.

## Sharing a bench, mate and pesos

A few habits help you fit in. Argentine plazas are built for sitting still, and nobody expects you to keep moving.

- **Mate is everywhere.** You'll often see people sharing a gourd of mate from a thermos. If someone offers you one, drink the whole gourd and hand it back to the person who served it. Saying "gracias" means you don't want another round, so only say it when you're done.
- **Pay in pesos.** The kiosks and cafés around the square take Argentine pesos. QR payments through apps such as Mercado Pago are now common. Brazilian reais and dollars sometimes circulate this close to the border, but the rate you get varies a lot, so check before you hand over notes.
- **Greet people when you go in.** Say a short "buen día" or "buenas tardes" when you enter a shop on the square. It's expected, and you'll get better service for it.

## Walking on to the Hito Tres Fronteras

The plaza makes a good starting point for the town's main free viewpoint. Head northwest toward the river and follow Avenida Tres Fronteras to the Hito Tres Fronteras. There, the Iguazú and Paraná rivers meet. From the Argentine bank you can see Brazil and Paraguay, each marked by an obelisk painted in its national colours.

It's a pleasant walk of roughly half an hour, mostly downhill on the way there and uphill coming back. Plenty of people time it for sunset over the water and then walk or take a taxi back to the centre for dinner. Bring water in summer, because the climb back is more tiring than the map suggests.

## Fitting it around the falls

Don't plan a day around the plaza. Plan it into the gaps. Most visitors spend one or two full days at Parque Nacional Iguazú, and the town fills the evenings and the hours before an early bus or flight.

- **How long:** 20–40 minutes is plenty, unless you're waiting on a connection.
- **Best use:** a shaded break after the terminal, or a meeting point before dinner.
- **Pair it with:** the Hito Tres Fronteras at dusk, then a meal in the streets around Victoria Aguirre.

## Before you go

Google currently lists Plaza San Martin as open 24 hours. As a public square it has no gate and no ticket. Nearby shops keep their own hours and some close in the afternoon, so check individual businesses if you're counting on one.

In the hottest months, aim for the morning or the hours after the afternoon break. Carry insect repellent, which you'll want everywhere in this part of Misiones. Late at night, take the usual town-centre precautions: stay on the lit, busy side of the square and keep your phone out of sight on quiet benches.
