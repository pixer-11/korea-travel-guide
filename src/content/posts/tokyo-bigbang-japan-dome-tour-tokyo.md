## Three nights under the Big Egg

The dates are December 13-15, 2026. For three nights, the white air-supported roof of Tokyo Dome hosts BIGBANG, the K-pop group that debuted under YG Entertainment in 2006. Fans fly in from Seoul, Taipei, Hong Kong and further across East Asia, and on these evenings the plaza fills with crown-shaped light sticks and yellow merchandise.

Locals call the venue the Big Egg. It opened in 1988 as the home ground of the Yomiuri Giants baseball team. For concerts it holds tens of thousands of people under a fabric roof kept up by air pressure, which is why the revolving doors at the gates push a gust of air at you as you go through.

BIGBANG are no strangers here. They toured Japan's domes again and again through the 2010s, and that history explains why so many people in the stands are long-time members of VIP, the group's fandom.

## Tickets, lotteries and the name on your seat

Japanese dome tickets work differently from most other markets. Allocation usually happens by lottery, and fan club members typically get the first draw. General lotteries through official ticketing partners come after that.

A few things are standard at Tokyo Dome concerts:

- **Name-linked tickets.** Tickets are usually tied to the buyer, and staff may compare photo ID with the name at entry.
- **Digital tickets.** Many shows send tickets through a smartphone app, and the seat block often appears only shortly before the show.
- **No unofficial resale.** Japan's Ticket Resale Prohibition Act of 2019 bans reselling designated tickets above face value, so transfers outside official channels can be refused at the gate.

The organiser's official channels carry the tour's own ticketing terms.

## Reaching Tokyo Dome by train

The dome sits in Tokyo Dome City, a block of hotels, rides and restaurants in Bunkyo ward. Three stations serve it:

- **Suidobashi Station:** JR Chuo-Sobu Line (local trains) and Toei Mita Line. The JR west exit puts you a few minutes from the gates.
- **Korakuen Station:** Tokyo Metro Marunouchi and Namboku lines, right beside Tokyo Dome City.
- **Kasuga Station:** Toei Oedo and Mita lines, a short walk north.

From Tokyo Station, people usually take the Chuo Line rapid to Ochanomizu and switch to a Chuo-Sobu local for one stop. The rapid trains do not stop at Suidobashi.

## Inside the dome: how a Japanese concert crowd behaves

Etiquette at Tokyo Dome is strict, and staff enforce it. Overseas fans sometimes get caught out.

- **Cameras.** Filming and photography are usually banned during the performance unless the artist says otherwise, and ushers do patrol the aisles.
- **Light sticks.** Fans keep them at chest height so the person behind can still see. Homemade banners are generally limited to a size that stays below shoulder level.
- **Merch lines.** Goods queues on the plaza often start hours before the show. Many fans buy online wherever the tour offers it.
- **Leaving.** After the encore, announcements usually release the crowd one block at a time. Fans wait for their block to be called, and doing the same saves you from a crush on the ramps.

People generally pay by IC card (Suica or Pasmo) or credit card at concessions and the station gates. Coin lockers at Suidobashi and Korakuen are often full on show days, so travelling light makes the evening easier.

## Before and after the show in Bunkyo

Mid-December in Tokyo is cold and usually dry. Evenings on the open plaza are chilly, so fans who queue outside come in coats and layers.

The area has plenty to fill an afternoon:

- **Koishikawa Korakuen**, an Edo-period stroll garden a short walk west, is calm on the morning of a show.
- **Tokyo Dome City Attractions** has a roller coaster that loops through the complex, plus the LaQua shopping and spa building.
- **Kanda Myojin** and the student bookshops of Jimbocho are both one or two stops away.

After the show, the Chuo-Sobu platforms at Suidobashi get very crowded. Plenty of fans walk ten minutes to Iidabashi or Kasuga for a quieter train.

## Where fans base themselves

Tokyo Dome Hotel stands right next to the venue. Its rooms are some of the most sought-after in the city on show nights.

Rooms around Iidabashi, Ochanomizu and Jimbocho give the same short ride or walk at a lower price level. Many visiting K-pop fans also stay near Shin-Okubo, Tokyo's Koreatown on the JR Yamanote Line. Its cafés and fan shops become informal gathering spots on concert weekends, and the trip from there to Suidobashi takes about twenty minutes with one change.
