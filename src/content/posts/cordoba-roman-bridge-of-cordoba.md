## Sixteen arches across the Guadalquivir

Stone arches carry you over the Guadalquivir. The bridge was first built in the early 1st century BCE, when Córdoba was a Roman provincial capital. Almost every century since has patched it, and much of what stands today is medieval Moorish and later rebuilding on the Roman line.

The deck is pedestrian-only and runs about 250 metres. At the north end the Renaissance gate, the Puerta del Puente, frames the Mezquita-Catedral's walls. At the south end the fortified Torre de la Calahorra guards the Campo de la Verdad side. Most people cross it in ten minutes. Allow 30–45 if you stop for the views.

## What you see from the deck

Halfway across, a stone statue of the Archangel Raphael, Córdoba's guardian, stands on the parapet. Candles and flowers usually sit at its base. Look down and the riverbed opens into the Sotos de la Albolafia, a protected stretch of reeds, islets and birdlife.

- **Molino de la Albolafia:** the restored waterwheel by the north bank, a reminder of the river's Moorish mills.
- **Mezquita-Catedral skyline:** the cathedral tower rises above the mosque roof. It looks best from the Calahorra end, looking back north.
- **Triunfo de San Rafael:** the tall baroque monument just beside the Puerta del Puente.
- **Torre de la Calahorra:** houses a museum on life in al-Andalus, with rooftop views back over the bridge.

Television fans may recognise the crossing as the Long Bridge of Volantis from *Game of Thrones*.

## Crossing at the right hour

Come early. The bridge is quietest from 7am to 9am every day of the week. At that hour the low light falls across the arches and you can photograph the parapet without strangers in the frame.

Try not to arrive between 12pm and 10pm on weekends. That is when tour groups, street musicians and evening strollers fill the deck, so getting a clear photo means waiting. In summer, Córdoba's midday heat is a second reason to cross early. The stone deck has almost no shade.

## Walking it the Cordobés way

People in Córdoba walk the bridge as part of the old town, not as a stop to tick off. A good loop links it with the Mezquita-Catedral and the Alcázar de los Reyes Cristianos, both a few minutes' walk from the north end.

- **At the San Rafael statue:** people light candles here as an act of devotion. Keep your voice down and step aside rather than posing against the shrine.
- **For the classic view:** cross to the south bank and walk a little way along the riverside. The full run of arches with the Mezquita behind is clearest from there.
- **Buskers and vendors:** musicians play for tips, so give coins if you stop to listen. Keep your bag zipped in the weekend crowds.
- **After dark:** the Mezquita walls and the arches are floodlit, and the bridge stays open to walkers.

The bridge is at Av. del Alcázar, s/n, in the Sur district. The city's taxis and buses stop along the Ronda de Isasa and the riverside avenue at the north end. On foot, it is about 15 minutes from Plaza de las Tendillas through the Judería.
