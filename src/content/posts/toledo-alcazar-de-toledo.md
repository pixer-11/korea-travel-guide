You see the Alcázar long before you reach it: a square-cut block of pale stone on the city's highest ridge, visible from the train station, from the bridges, from almost anywhere in the Tagus valley below. Toledo was built around this hill by the Romans, and every power that followed, Visigoths, Moors, Castilian kings, put a fortress here. What stands now is largely a 16th-century rebuild ordered by Charles V, though the walls have been patched and rebuilt again since, most extensively after the building was gutted in 1936.

That siege is the reason many Spaniards know the name at all. Nationalist forces held out here for two months during the Civil War while Republican troops besieged and shelled the building to ruins. The museum inside doesn't shy away from this; entire rooms are given over to the siege, with photographs, telegrams and the shattered original masonry left visible in places.

## What's actually inside

The building now houses the Museo del Ejército, Spain's national army museum, and it's a bigger collection than the plain facade suggests.

- Armour and weaponry spanning medieval to 20th-century Spain
- Uniforms, flags and insignia from Spanish military campaigns across five centuries
- A large section on the colonial era and the campaigns in the Americas and North Africa
- The preserved office and command post used during the 1936 siege
- Rotating temporary exhibitions on the ground floor

Give yourself two to three hours if you read labels and watch the short documentary footage on the siege. An hour is enough for a fast pass through the highlights.

## Getting there and orientation

The Alcázar sits at C. de la Union, s/n, at the eastern edge of Toledo's old town, an easy uphill walk from Plaza de Zocodover, the city's central square. Most visitors arrive on foot from there, ten minutes up a gentle slope past souvenir shops and knife stores. If you've come by train, it's a steeper 20-minute climb from Toledo station, or a short taxi ride.

Drivers should aim for one of the paid car parks near Puerta de Bisagra rather than trying to find street parking in the old town, where streets are narrow and mostly pedestrian.

## When to go

The Alcázar is closed on Mondays, open Tuesday through Sunday from 10am to 5pm. Confirm hours before you go, since museums here occasionally shift schedules around holidays.

Weekends between 11am and 5pm are the busiest stretch, with tour groups and day-trippers from Madrid filling the entrance hall and the central courtyard at the same time. If you can, go on a weekday, or aim for the first hour after opening on any day before the coach groups arrive.

## Buying tickets and moving through the museum

Given the volume of visitors this place gets, year-round, buying a timed ticket online in advance is worth the few minutes it takes, especially for weekend visits. At the door, tickets are sold at a counter just inside the main gate, and there's usually a separate queue for those who've already booked.

Once inside, the museum is arranged over several floors around a central Renaissance courtyard, itself worth a slow look for the classical arcades and stone staircase. Free audio guides or included information panels (check current terms at the ticket desk) are in Spanish and English; if you want context on the 1936 siege specifically, look for the dedicated rooms on the upper floors rather than expecting it woven through every gallery.

## Combining it with the rest of Toledo

The Alcázar pairs naturally with a walk through the surrounding streets rather than as a standalone stop. Toledo Cathedral is a ten-minute walk downhill, and the Museo de Santa Cruz, a Renaissance building with its own art collection, sits just below the Alcázar's eastern side. Many visitors do the Alcázar in the morning, break for lunch around Zocodover, then continue to the cathedral and the old Jewish quarter in the afternoon, since the old town is compact enough to cover most of it on foot in a single day.
