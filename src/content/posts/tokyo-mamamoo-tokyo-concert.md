## How Tickets Work for a Tokyo K-pop Show

Japanese concert tickets rarely go on simple first-come sale. Most are sold by lottery (抽選, *chūsen*). You apply during a window, and the winners are drawn afterwards.

The usual order of priority runs like this:

- **Official fan club presale**, typically the best odds for members
- **Promoter and playguide lotteries** through Japanese ticketing platforms
- **General sale** for whatever is left, if anything

Many Japanese platforms ask for a Japanese phone number or address. Some international shows open a separate overseas-applicant lottery. Check the official channel for which route applies to this date.

Tickets are often digital, held in a ticketing app on your phone. Names can be checked against ID at the door. Unofficial resale is illegal in Japan for designated tickets, so buy only through the official route or its authorised resale service.

## Getting to Ariake

Ariake sits on the Tokyo Bay waterfront, south-east of central Tokyo. You have two rail options:

- **Yurikamome line to Ariake-tennis-no-mori Station.** The closest stop, a few minutes on foot.
- **Rinkai line to Kokusai-Tenjijō Station.** A longer walk of roughly ten minutes, with direct trains from Ōsaki and Shinjuku via JR through-services.

The Yurikamome is a driverless elevated line from Shimbashi. It crosses the Rainbow Bridge loop and Odaiba on the way out. It's scenic, but it runs short trains that fill up fast on show nights.

After the show, thousands of people leave at once. Walking to Kokusai-Tenjijō for the Rinkai line usually spreads the load better than queuing for the Yurikamome platform. Load a Suica or PASMO card beforehand so you don't end up at the ticket machines with everyone else.

## Lightsticks, Lotteries and the Quiet Rules

Japanese concert halls run on unwritten rules. MAMAMOO's fandom, Moomoo, follows them closely in Tokyo.

- **Bring the official lightstick.** Fans call it the Moobong, and it often syncs to the stage lighting by Bluetooth. Homemade or oversized light-up signs are commonly banned.
- **Filming and photography are usually prohibited** during the performance. Staff do enforce this. Keep your phone away unless the artist explicitly allows a moment for photos.
- **Stay in your assigned spot.** Standing is normal at K-pop shows, but leaving your seat area or holding banners above head height blocks the row behind you.
- **Learn the fanchants.** Japanese audiences call out on cue and stay quiet otherwise.
- **Merch lines start early.** Official goods booths often open hours before doors. Many tours also sell through an online shop, which is worth checking first.

Seat locations are often revealed only when your digital ticket is issued, so don't plan around a specific block.

Coin lockers near the venue fill early. Bring as little as possible. Japanese venues generally don't allow large bags inside the hall.

## Inside Tokyo Garden Theater

The hall is built for concerts. It isn't a converted sports arena, so the tiers rise steeply around the stage across several levels. Even the upper balconies feel closer to the performers than they would in a dome.

Capacity is around 8,000, which makes it one of Tokyo's mid-sized major venues. That's intimate for a group that also plays larger arenas. The upper tiers are steep, so take the stairs slowly with a drink in hand.

## Before and After in Ariake

Ariake Garden itself is a full complex, so you don't need to leave the block. It has a shopping mall with a food court and restaurants, and the Izumi Tenku no Yu hot-spring facility. There's also a hotel attached, useful if you'd rather not join the post-show rail scrum.

Within walking distance:

- **Tokyo Big Sight**, the convention centre with its inverted-pyramid towers
- **Ariake Arena**, built for the Tokyo 2020 Olympics
- **Odaiba**, one or two Yurikamome stops away, with seaside views of the Rainbow Bridge
