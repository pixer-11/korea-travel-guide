## Why it's worth the walk

You come around the corner from Piazza Venezia, past the roar of scooters circling the Vittoriano, and suddenly the street narrows and quiets. Piazza d'Aracoeli sits in that hinge of the city where the grandeur of the Capitoline Hill gives way to the tangle of the old Jewish Ghetto — travertine steps climbing to Santa Maria in Aracoeli on one side, medieval alleys threading off toward Via del Portico d'Ottavia on the other. It's here, inside a palazzo whose bones go back to the 1500s, that Tribuna Campitelli has opened, and it has become one of the most talked-about arrivals on the Rome dining scene in recent memory. Locals and visiting food writers alike have been passing its name around since it opened, and reservations have reportedly been hard to come by.

## What makes the building itself the draw

Part of the appeal here is architectural rather than culinary. A 16th-century palazzo in this pocket of Rome isn't a novelty — the whole neighborhood is built from layers like this — but having one restored and opened as a dining room, rather than left as offices or apartments, is rarer. Expect the kind of details that make Roman interiors worth lingering over: high coffered or vaulted ceilings, thick stone walls that keep the noise of the piazza outside, maybe a courtyard or loggia glimpsed through a doorway. The name itself nods to the setting — "Tribuna" suggesting an elevated vantage point, "Campitelli" the historic rione (district) this stretch of Rome belongs to, one of the city's original fourteen administrative regions and among its oldest continuously inhabited corners.

## Where it sits in the city

This is about as central as Rome gets. Piazza d'Aracoeli is a two-minute walk from the Capitoline Museums and the Campidoglio, a few minutes more from the Roman Forum and the Colosseum, and it backs directly onto the Ghetto, home to Rome's oldest Jewish community and its own distinct culinary tradition — carciofi alla giudia, fried salt cod, the trattorias along Via del Portico d'Ottavia. Whatever Tribuna Campitelli's own kitchen turns out, it's operating in direct conversation with that history, in a neighborhood where eating well has never been an afterthought.

## Getting there

On foot, this area sits between the Roman Forum and the Tiber, an easy walk from Largo di Torre Argentina, where several tram and bus lines converge. The nearest useful reference point for anyone using transit is the cluster of bus routes running along Via del Teatro di Marcello and Piazza Venezia, just steps from Piazza d'Aracoeli. If you're coming from Termini or staying near the Vatican, a taxi or rideshare into Piazza Venezia will drop you within a very short walk. Rome's metro doesn't reach this deep into the historic center, so plan on buses or your own two feet — which, given the surroundings, is no hardship.

## What to expect (and confirm before you go)

Because this is such a recent opening, treat any specifics you read elsewhere — menu, prices, exact hours — as provisional. Restaurants in a first year often adjust service times, tasting formats, and staffing as they settle in. Call ahead or check the restaurant's own booking channels close to your visit, and reconfirm your reservation the same day if you can; a project generating this much buzz in a city as tourist-heavy as Rome is not one where you want to arrive and hope for a walk-in table.

## How to visit like a local

Romans dining in the centro storico tend to eat later than most visitors expect — a 9pm reservation raises no eyebrows here — and a buzzy new opening like this is exactly the kind of place where booking days or weeks ahead, rather than the same day, is the norm. If you can, call directly rather than relying only on third-party booking sites, since smaller new restaurants often hold back tables for direct reservations. Dress a notch smarter than casual sightseeing gear — this is a palazzo setting, not a pizzeria counter. Card payment is standard in Rome now, but carry some cash for small tips, which are appreciated but not obligatory the way they are in the US. The mistake tourists most often make in this exact pocket of Rome is treating Piazza d'Aracoeli as a pass-through between the Colosseum and Piazza Navona rather than a destination in itself — arrive a little early and climb the Aracoeli steps or wander the Ghetto's side streets before your table's ready, rather than rushing straight in.
