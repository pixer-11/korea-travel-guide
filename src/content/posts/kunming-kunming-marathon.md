## Racing at 1,900 metres

Kunming sits high, and your lungs find out first. The city lies at roughly 1,900 metres on the Yunnan plateau, so the opening kilometres feel harder than the pace on your watch suggests.

That altitude shapes the whole race. Runners coming from sea level usually treat the Kunming Marathon as a time to enjoy the course rather than chase a personal best. Many fly in several days ahead so their body has time to adjust.

December is the heart of Kunming's dry season. Mornings start cold, often only a few degrees above freezing, while the afternoon sun feels strong because of the thin air. Runners typically start in a throwaway layer and still finish with sunburnt shoulders.

## Date and how entries work

The race takes place on Sunday, December 13, 2026. Roughly 10,000 participants line up, which puts it in the mid-sized bracket among Chinese city marathons: big enough for closed roads and crowds, small enough to feel personal.

"Tickets" here means a race entry. Large Chinese marathons commonly allocate places through online registration, sometimes with a ballot when demand outstrips spots. The organiser publishes the categories, entry rules and fees on its official site.

International entrants usually register with their passport details. In China, the name on a race bib is generally tied to the ID used at registration, and race-pack collection normally requires that same document.

## Venue and getting around the city

The course runs through central Kunming. The organiser publishes the exact start, finish and route map on its official site, and roads along the course close to traffic on race morning.

Getting to Kunming is straightforward:

- **By air:** Kunming Changshui International Airport is one of China's main hubs, with Metro Line 6 linking it to the city.
- **By rail:** high-speed trains arrive at Kunming South Railway Station, connected to the centre by Metro Line 1.
- **Around town:** the metro is the usual way to reach the start, since taxis cannot get close to closed roads.

Visitors often stay near Green Lake (Cuihu) or the city centre around Dongfeng Square. Both areas sit on metro lines and have plenty of breakfast spots.

## Running and cheering among the locals

On race day, payment in China is almost entirely mobile. Shops, noodle stalls and taxis expect Alipay or WeChat Pay, and both apps now accept international cards. Foreign runners usually set this up before they land, because a phone is the most useful thing in your kit bag after your shoes.

Along the course you will hear one word over and over: **jiāyóu** (加油), literally "add oil". It is the all-purpose cheer. Shout it back at volunteers and spectators and you will get a grin.

A few habits are typical of Chinese city marathons:

- Bag drop and start corrals are organised by bib colour or letter, and volunteers enforce them.
- Cut-off times are applied strictly, with sweeper buses following the back of the field.
- Finishers collect a medal and a recovery bag; a photo with the medal at the finish backdrop is close to compulsory.

## Noodles before and after

Kunming runs on rice noodles. The classic is **guòqiáo mǐxiàn**, crossing-the-bridge rice noodles: a bowl of scalding broth arrives with raw slices of meat, quail egg, vegetables and noodles on side plates, and you tip them in to cook in the heat.

For a lighter pre-race carb load, many runners pick a simpler bowl of **xiǎoguō mǐxiàn**, small-pot noodles cooked to order in a little copper pot. Afterwards, Yunnan's wild mushroom dishes and **qìguō jī**, steam-pot chicken, are common recovery meals.

## Legs to spare: the day after

December brings Kunming's most famous visitors. Black-headed gulls arrive from Siberia each winter, and crowds gather at Green Lake Park and along the shore of Dianchi Lake to watch them.

If your legs allow stairs, the Western Hills (Xishan) rise above Dianchi, with the cliff-carved Dragon Gate looking down over the water. For a flat stroll instead, the old streets around Yunnan University and Wenlin Street are a gentle half-day on tired legs.

Runners with a few extra days often take the high-speed train to Dali or continue to Lijiang. Both make the trip much more than a race weekend.
