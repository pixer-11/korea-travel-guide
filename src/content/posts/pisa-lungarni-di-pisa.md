## Pisa's other skyline

Stand on Ponte di Mezzo and turn slowly. The Arno swings through the city in a long, shallow curve. Both banks are lined with an unbroken wall of palazzi in ochre, rust, cream and faded pink, their shutters stacked in neat rows above the water.

These embankment streets are the Lungarni, and each stretch has its own name. On the north bank you will find Lungarno Mediceo and Lungarno Pacinotti. On the south bank are Lungarno Galilei and Lungarno Gambacorti. A low stone parapet runs along the river side of all of them, at a height made for leaning or sitting.

## Reaching Ponte di Mezzo

The Lungarni sit about halfway between Pisa Centrale and the Leaning Tower. That makes them easy to walk through on the way from one to the other.

- **From Pisa Centrale:** walk north through Piazza Vittorio Emanuele II and along Corso Italia, the pedestrian shopping street. It ends at the Logge di Banchi and the bridge, roughly 15 minutes on foot.
- **From Piazza dei Miracoli:** head south through Borgo Stretto's arcades to Piazza Garibaldi, at the north end of the bridge. Allow 15–20 minutes.
- **From the airport:** the PisaMover shuttle runs to Pisa Centrale, and from there you walk.

There's no entrance, gate or ticket. These are working streets, and they are open 24 hours.

## What lines the banks

Most visitors walk this stretch looking for the next landmark. The river is what you actually came for.

- **Santa Maria della Spina** is a small Gothic church on Lungarno Gambacorti. Its roof bristles with pinnacles and spires, and it sits almost on the water's edge.
- **Palazzo Blu** is the palazzo painted a strong blue on Lungarno Gambacorti. It's now an exhibition space.
- **Palazzo Lanfranchi**, on the north bank, is another museum building facing the river.
- **The Cittadella and Torre Guelfa** are at the western end, where the old fortifications meet the Arno.
- **Logge di Banchi** is the open, arcaded loggia at the south foot of Ponte di Mezzo.

Walking the full loop takes about an hour. Go west on one bank, cross at Ponte Solferino or a bridge further down, then come back on the other side.

## Evenings on the parapet

The parapet is the seating. Pisa is a university city, and as the light drops students perch along the stone wall with drinks from the bars around Piazza Garibaldi and the lanes just behind the river.

Some habits that help:

- **Coffee and aperitivo:** standing at the bar counter usually costs less than taking a table, so look at the posted list before you sit down. In many bars you pay at the till first and then hand over the receipt.
- **Mind the traffic:** the Lungarni carry cars, scooters and plenty of bikes. The riverside pavement is narrow, so walk single file when it's busy and cross at the marked crossings.
- **Choose your side for the light:** the river runs roughly east to west, so the western stretch towards the Cittadella faces the sunset.

Forecasts put the busiest window at weekends from 5pm to 11pm. If you want the parapet and the photo spots mostly to yourself, come on a weekday evening, or before 5pm on a Saturday or Sunday.

## The nights the river lights up

Every year on the evening of 16 June, Pisa holds the Luminara di San Ranieri. The palazzi along both banks are outlined in tens of thousands of small lights, which are reflected in the Arno.

The next day, 17 June, brings the Regata di San Ranieri, a rowing race on the river. On the last Saturday of June comes the Gioco del Ponte, a costumed contest between the two halves of the city, held on Ponte di Mezzo itself.

Luminara night draws huge crowds. Pick your spot on the parapet early and treat dinner as something you book well ahead or skip. Check the city's official listings for that year's programme.

## Fitting the river into your day

Most people give the Lungarni an hour or two, usually as the link between the station and the Tower. A better plan is to do the Tower in the morning and come down Borgo Stretto in the afternoon. Then walk the river as the sun goes down.

This area is calmer than Piazza dei Miracoli and feels more lived-in, though it's no secret. Corso Italia and Borgo Stretto have shops and cafés for the hours in between. A crossing of Ponte di Mezzo after dark is a good way to end the day.
