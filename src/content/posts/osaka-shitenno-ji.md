## What's actually here

Shitennō-ji claims the title of Japan's oldest state-built Buddhist temple, founded in 593 under Prince Shōtoku. Fire, war and lightning have destroyed the original buildings repeatedly; what stands now is concrete reconstruction from the 1960s, styled after the Asuka-period layout. The five-story pagoda, main hall (Kondō), and lecture hall sit in a straight north-south line inside a walled courtyard, a design called Shitennō-ji-style garan, one of the oldest temple blueprints in the country.

You can climb the pagoda's ground floor for a look at the central pillar and Buddhist relics, though upper floors are usually closed to visitors. The Kondō houses a seated Kannon statue. Outside the main courtyard, Gokuraku-jōdo Garden has ponds thick with turtles and koi, a smaller draw but genuinely restful after the compound's straight lines.

## Getting there and timing it right

The temple sits in Tennoji Ward, a short walk from Shitennoji-mae Yuhigaoka Station on the Tanimachi subway line. It's also walkable from JR Tennoji Station, about 15-20 minutes on foot through quieter residential streets.

- Open daily, 8:30am to 4:00pm; no day-of-week closures
- Quietest window: weekdays, 9am to 10am
- Avoid weekends between 10am and 3pm, when tour groups and school trips cluster around the pagoda
- Allow 45-60 minutes for the main garan plus the garden

Arriving right at 8:30am on a weekday is the single best move here: you get the courtyard largely to yourself before buses of visitors arrive.

## Paying, praying, and the mistake first-timers make

Entry to the outer temple grounds is free; the inner garan (pagoda, Kondō, lecture hall) and the treasure house each charge a separate small admission, paid in cash at the gate. There's no single combined ticket window, so decide in advance whether you want just the grounds or the paid inner areas too.

Visitors can ring the temple bell and pray at the main hall like at any working Japanese temple: a small coin in the offering box, a bow, hands together, no clapping (that's Shinto shrine etiquette, not Buddhist temple etiquette, and mixing the two is the most common mistake here). Photography is generally fine outdoors but restricted inside the Kondō itself.

## Beyond the main compound

Shitennō-ji still functions as a working temple and school complex, not just a museum piece, so you'll see monks and students moving through on ordinary business. A flea market sets up on the temple grounds on the 21st and 22nd of each month, worth timing a visit around if the dates line up. The surrounding Tennoji Ward also holds Tennoji Park and Osaka's zoo within easy walking distance, making this an easy half-day pairing rather than a stand-alone stop.
