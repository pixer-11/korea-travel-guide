Moss covers the trunks right up to the first branches. Ferns grow out of the moss. Water runs off every slope into streams the colour of weak tea, and the cloud often sits low enough to swallow the ridge above you. Mtirala is a fragment of the old Colchic rainforest that once lined the eastern Black Sea, and it still looks the part.

It is consistently one of the best-rated nature stops in western Georgia. Expect company on the main trails in summer, but the forest is big enough to absorb it.

## Where Mtirala actually is

Start with the map. Some listings file Mtirala under Akhaltsikhe, but the park is in Adjara, in the hills behind the coastal resort of Chakvi. The address is Chakvistavi, the last village up the valley, where the main visitor centre and the trailheads are.

That matters for planning. From Batumi it is a short trip inland. From Akhaltsikhe it is a long mountain drive west over the Goderdzi Pass and down through Khulo and Keda to the coast. That road can be hit by snow and closures outside summer, so check conditions before committing.

If you are based in Akhaltsikhe for Rabati Castle or Vardzia, treat Mtirala as part of a separate Black Sea leg, not a day trip.

## Why the mountain weeps

The name says it all. Mtirala comes from the Georgian word for weeping or crying, and the mountain earns it. Moist air comes in off the Black Sea and hits the first high ridge inland. It drops its water here, making this one of the wettest places in Georgia and, by the park's own claim, the former USSR.

That rain is the whole point of the visit. It feeds the dense forest that gives the park its unusual flora and fauna:

- Colchic species such as chestnut, beech and Colchic boxwood
- Evergreen undergrowth of rhododendron and cherry laurel
- Ferns, mosses and lichens on almost every surface
- Wildlife including brown bear and roe deer, which you are far more likely to see signs of than the animals themselves

The park was created in 2006. In 2021 it became part of the UNESCO World Heritage listing for the Colchic Rainforests and Wetlands.

## Getting up to Chakvistavi

The approach runs from the coast. From Batumi, head north to Chakvi and turn inland up the valley road to Chakvistavi. The drive is short but climbs steadily on narrower village roads near the end.

Your options:

- **Taxi or Bolt from Batumi:** the simplest choice. Agree on a pickup time, as phone signal and spare cars are thin up in the valley.
- **Minibus (marshrutka):** local services run between Batumi and Chakvistavi. Check departure times in Batumi, because the last return can leave early.
- **Hire car:** gives you the freedom to stay until late afternoon, and the road is manageable with care.

Many visitors stay a night in one of the guesthouses in Chakvistavi. That way you are on the trail before the day-trippers come up from the coast.

## On the trails

The classic walk follows the river to the Tsivtskaro waterfall and on to a small lake. It is a well-marked out-and-back that suits most fit walkers. Allow roughly half a day with stops. In summer the lake usually has simple boats for hire, which makes a good turnaround point.

Longer routes climb further into the park and need more planning, sometimes an overnight stay at a shelter or campsite. Ask at the visitor centre which ones are open and in what state. Rangers there know how the latest rain has affected the paths.

Underfoot, expect roots, wet stone, mud and stream crossings. Boots with real grip are a better choice than trainers here.

## Signing in at the Chakvistavi visitor centre

Go to the visitor centre first. Before heading up, people register here, check the current trail map and pay any entry or service charges. The current prices are posted at the centre and on the Georgian Agency of Protected Areas website, so check there rather than relying on old reviews.

Google lists the park as open 24 hours, but that refers to the land, not the staffed facilities. The visitor centre, boat hire and guide bookings keep their own daytime schedule. Their hours change with the season, so confirm them before you set off. Arriving early in the day usually puts you ahead of the groups driving up from Batumi.

A few habits make the day go smoothly:

- Carry some Georgian lari in cash. Card readers in the village are not guaranteed.
- If you are staying the night, book your guesthouse ahead in July and August.
- Ask the rangers about hiring a local guide for the longer routes. It is the normal way to do them.
- Stay on the marked paths and carry your rubbish back down. Bins on the trail are scarce.

## Dressing for a rainforest

Plan for rain whatever the forecast says. The coast can be sunny while Mtirala sits in cloud, and showers come in quickly.

Pack:

- A proper waterproof jacket, not just a windbreaker
- Quick-drying layers rather than cotton
- A dry bag or plastic liner for your phone and documents
- Insect repellent for the humid summer months
- Water and food, since there is little to buy past the village

Late spring through early autumn is the usual walking season. Summer is the warmest and busiest. Autumn turns the beech and chestnut gold and tends to be quieter. Winter brings heavier, colder rain and makes the upper paths much harder going.

## Pairing it with the coast

Mtirala fits best into a Batumi stay. Down at sea level, the Batumi Botanical Garden at Green Cape is a few minutes from Chakvi and shows a different, cultivated side of the same humid climate. Closer to the Turkish border, the Gonio fortress makes a good half-day of history.

Chakvi itself was the cradle of Georgian tea growing. The terraced hillsides on the drive up still show traces of old plantations. Look out for them on the way back down.
