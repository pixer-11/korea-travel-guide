Two names on one banner tell you a lot about this event. Ciputra Hanoi is the big gated urban district on the city's northwest edge. Yonex Sunrise is the racket-and-shuttle brand found in nearly every badminton bag in Southeast Asia. Between them sits a six-day BWF tournament in Hanoi, running **March 16 to 21, 2027**.

## Where it sits on the badminton ladder

International Challenge is a mid-level rung on the Badminton World Federation's circuit. It sits below the World Tour events you see on television and above the International Series. The ranking points matter to players trying to break into bigger draws.

That shapes who turns up. You tend to see:

- Young players from Asian badminton powers building their rankings
- Vietnam's own national squad players, competing in front of a home crowd
- Doubles pairs testing new partnerships before the bigger spring events

Results here often hint at who reaches World Tour draws a season or two later. For many fans, that is the appeal: a chance to see players at an early stage of their careers, long before most television coverage reaches them.

## Six days, Tuesday to finals Sunday

The tournament opens on Tuesday, March 16, and closes on Sunday, March 21. Events at this level usually follow the same pattern. Qualifying rounds come first, then the main draw shrinks across the week.

There are five disciplines: men's singles, women's singles, men's doubles, women's doubles and mixed doubles. The early days fill several courts at once. You can drift from court to court and see a dozen matches in an afternoon.

By the weekend, play narrows to fewer courts and the matches stretch longer. Sunday is finals day, with titles in all five events decided one after another. Many spectators come only for Saturday's semi-finals and Sunday's finals. Badminton purists often prefer the crowded early rounds, where the volume of play is the attraction.

## Courtside manners in a Hanoi hall

Badminton etiquette is stricter than it looks from the stands. The shuttle is light and fast, and players read it against the roof lights. That explains most of the rules.

- **No flash photography.** It can blind a player in the middle of a smash.
- **Move between rallies, not during them.** Spectators wait at the aisle end until the point finishes.
- **Applaud after the point.** Noise during a serve is frowned on.
- **Keep your movement low behind the baselines.** That is where players look.

Vietnamese fans cheer loudly for their own players. You will hear "Việt Nam cố lên!" (roughly "Come on, Vietnam!") when a home pair wins a long rally. Badminton in Vietnamese is *cầu lông*, which is useful when you ask a driver or a hotel desk for directions.

Small food and drink stalls around Hanoi sports venues usually take cash or bank QR codes such as VietQR. Card terminals are less certain. Getting there, most locals and visitors use Grab, the app for motorbike and car rides, rather than flagging taxis.

## Hanoi in drizzle season

Mid-March is Hanoi's *mưa phùn* season, the fine spring drizzle that settles over the city for days at a time. Temperatures are mild, but the air stays damp. Pavements and motorbike seats are often wet in the morning.

That has practical consequences for a tournament week. Motorbike rides in the drizzle are cold and damp, so many visitors switch to Grab cars for the trip to the hall. Grey skies also make March a good month for indoor sport, since you lose little by spending the day under a roof.

Noi Bai International Airport lies north of the Red River. The route into the city crosses Nhat Tan Bridge, which lands close to the West Lake district and the Ciputra area. That makes the northwest of town a short ride from your flight.

## The Ciputra and West Lake side of town

Ciputra Hanoi is a large planned urban area in the northwest, near West Lake (Hồ Tây). It has broad roads, villa streets and an international school community. It feels nothing like the tight tube houses and motorbike traffic of the Old Quarter.

The West Lake shore nearby works well between sessions. A loop around the lake covers:

- **Trấn Quốc Pagoda**, the city's oldest Buddhist temple, on a narrow spit of land on the eastern shore
- **Quán Thánh Temple**, a Taoist shrine with a large bronze statue, just across Thanh Niên road
- Lakeside cafés along Tây Hồ's waterfront roads, popular in the late afternoon

Visitors who want the central sights usually stay around Hoàn Kiếm Lake and the Old Quarter. They take Grab out to matches, which adds traffic time but puts the evening street food on the doorstep. Fans who care more about the badminton than the sightseeing tend to stay on the Tây Hồ side.
