Two chefs share one name, and the restaurant carries it. Sombok is one of the newer openings in Phnom Penh's food scene. The kitchen is run by two acclaimed Cambodian chefs who are both called Sombok, and it cooks Khmer food with a modern hand.

Your table there connects you to a wider movement. Cambodian chefs have been reworking their own national cuisine at the top end of the market, cooking it on their terms rather than leaving it to hotel buffets.

## Why the Embassy link matters

Sombok is affiliated with Embassy in Siem Reap. Embassy made its name as one of the places that showed visitors to Angkor that Khmer cooking could be refined, seasonal and ambitious.

That link tells you a lot before you arrive. Expect a kitchen that treats Cambodian ingredients and techniques as the main event, not as a curiosity next to a Western menu. If you have eaten at Embassy, this is the same family in the capital. If you haven't, Sombok lets you try that approach without the drive north.

## What "modern Khmer" means here

We haven't been given a menu, and dishes at a new restaurant change quickly. So read this section as background on the cuisine, not a list of what's on the menu.

Khmer cooking is built on a few pillars that modern chefs tend to rework:

- **Kroeung**, a pounded paste of lemongrass, galangal, turmeric and kaffir lime that underpins many curries and stir-fries
- **Prahok**, fermented fish paste, the deep savoury backbone of the country's cooking
- **Fish amok**, the steamed coconut curry most visitors already know
- **Freshwater fish and river herbs**, drawn from the Mekong and Tonlé Sap

Creative takes on these usually play with texture, presentation and portion size while keeping the sour, salty and herbal balance intact. Ask your server which dishes lean most traditional and which push furthest. Staff at chef-led Khmer restaurants are generally glad to explain.

## Picking your hour

The day here starts early and runs late. Sombok serves from early morning to late night, which is unusual for a chef-driven restaurant and gives you real flexibility.

Phnom Penh's heat shapes how locals eat. Breakfast is a serious meal in Cambodia, and early mornings are the coolest part of the day, so a morning visit makes sense. A late dinner suits travellers who spend the afternoon at the Royal Palace or the National Museum and want to avoid the midday sun.

Exact hours weren't supplied and may shift while the restaurant settles in. Confirm them with the restaurant directly before you go.

## Booking, paying and tipping in the capital

A new, talked-about restaurant tends to fill up. Reserve rather than walking in, especially for dinner or for a group. Booking through the restaurant's own channels or social media pages is the norm for chef-led places in Phnom Penh.

On paying, a few things work differently in Cambodia:

- **Two currencies.** US dollars and Cambodian riel are both in everyday use, and change often comes back as a mix.
- **Cards and QR.** Higher-end restaurants usually take cards, and local QR payments such as ABA Pay are everywhere. Ask before you assume.
- **Tipping.** It isn't obligatory, but leaving something for good service is appreciated, particularly where no service charge appears on the bill.

Ordering a few dishes to share, with rice in the middle of the table, matches how Cambodians eat. It also lets you taste more of what the kitchen is doing.

## Getting there and planning the rest of your day

Most visitors cross the city by ride-hailing app. Grab and PassApp both work well in Phnom Penh, offering cars and the three-wheeled rickshaws locals call remorks or tuk-tuks. Fares show up in the app before you book.

Confirm Sombok's current address with the restaurant and share a map pin with your driver. Street numbering in the city can be confusing, and a pin saves a lot of circling.

Before or after your meal, the riverfront along Sisowath Quay gets busy as the heat fades. The Royal Palace, the National Museum and Wat Phnom all make easy pairings for a day out. If you're eating in the morning, go on afterwards to the Central Market (Phsar Thmei) or the Russian Market (Phsar Tuol Tom Poung) to see the raw ingredients of Khmer cooking piled high on market stalls.
