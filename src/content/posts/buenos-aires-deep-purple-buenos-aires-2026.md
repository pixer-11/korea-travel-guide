## One night, one riff, one arena

Deep Purple play Movistar Arena on December 10, 2026. It's a Thursday night in early summer, and Buenos Aires stays up late anyway.

This is the band that gave the world "Smoke on the Water". They formed in England in 1968, and the 1972 album *Machine Head* is still the record most of the crowd will be waiting to hear. Argentina has a long, loud relationship with British hard rock, and several generations share the floor at these shows.

## Inside Movistar Arena

The arena sits in Villa Crespo, a residential barrio of low apartment blocks and corner cafés between Palermo and Caballito. It's a fully enclosed, purpose-built concert hall, so the show doesn't depend on the December weather.

Two ticket words matter here:

- **Campo**: the standing floor in front of the stage.
- **Platea**: the tiered seating that rings the floor.

People in campo usually get there well before the band, because the closest standing spots go first. People in platea seats tend to turn up nearer showtime. Movistar Arena's website lists its events, seating plans and ticket links.

## Reaching Villa Crespo

Most concertgoers take the Subte. Line B runs under Avenida Corrientes, and Dorrego and Malabia are the stations people usually use for the arena. From either one it's a walk of roughly 10–15 minutes through quiet residential streets.

Coming from the centre, Line B starts at Leandro N. Alem, near the Microcentro, and runs straight out along Corrientes. Many city bus (colectivo) routes also run down Corrientes. You pay for both the Subte and the buses with a SUBE card.

After the show, thousands of people leave at once. Lots of fans walk a few blocks away from the doors before calling a ride or hailing a black-and-yellow taxi. Traffic right outside the arena tends to jam as it empties.

## Doing the night the porteño way

People here eat late, and that suits a concert night. Locals call themselves porteños, and dinner at 9pm counts as early. Plenty of fans eat after the encore.

- **Paying:** cards work at most restaurants and bars. Many places also take QR payments through Mercado Pago. Carry some pesos too, for kiosks and street vendors.
- **Tipping:** at a sit-down meal, around ten percent is usual. People often leave it in cash because the card terminal may not add it.
- **Ordering:** at a parrilla (grill house), *bife de chorizo* is the classic steak and *provoleta* is grilled provolone. A *choripán* is a chorizo sandwich, the standard street snack around Buenos Aires events.
- **Language:** "una entrada" means one ticket and "la salida" means the exit. A simple "gracias" goes a long way.

At big rock shows in Argentina, the crowd chants between songs. Many fans also wear band shirts from earlier tours as a badge of how many times they've been.

## Before and after the show

Villa Crespo is one of the city's most active neighbourhoods for eating and drinking. Its streets fill with bodegones, the old-school dining rooms serving milanesas and huge plates of pasta, alongside newer wine bars and cafés.

Palermo is just across Avenida Córdoba. Its Palermo Soho blocks around Plaza Serrano keep bars open well past midnight, and many visitors carry on there after the encore.

The neighbourhood also has a long history in the city's textile trade. During the day the outlet shops along Avenida Córdoba and nearby streets draw bargain hunters. If you're in town for a few days, that pairs easily with an afternoon in Palermo's parks.

## December in Buenos Aires

December is early summer in the southern hemisphere. Evenings are usually warm and often humid, and summer storms can arrive fast. Light clothing makes the walk from the Subte comfortable, and the arena's roof means no one gets rained on mid-set.

Many visitors stay in Palermo, which is close enough to walk or taxi to the arena. Others base themselves in Recoleta or San Telmo and ride Line B from the centre. December is also when the jacarandas finish flowering and the city's terraces fill up, so the concert sits well inside a longer trip.
