Ten days in October, a handful of cinemas, and a programme made up of the year's festival-circuit films. That's Filmekimi, Istanbul's autumn film showcase. It's one of the main cultural events of the city's season. The 2026 edition was set for **October 9-18**, with screenings at **Atlas 1948** and three other cinemas around Istanbul.

There's no outdoor stage, no wristbands and no festival village. Filmekimi is dark rooms, subtitles and queues on the pavement. Fitting several films into one day is entirely normal.

## What Filmekimi actually is

Filmekimi is a city showcase, not a competition. It's organised by İKSV, the Istanbul Foundation for Culture and Arts, which also runs the Istanbul Film Festival in spring. In autumn the job is curation: films that have played the major international festivals during the year, brought to Istanbul audiences over a short, packed run.

The ten-day format shapes everything. Most titles get only a screening or two. Miss one and you may wait months for a release in Turkey, or it may never arrive.

## The Atlas 1948 screen on İstiklal

Atlas 1948 is the venue most people mean when they talk about Filmekimi. It's on **İstiklal Caddesi**, the long pedestrian street running from Taksim Square down towards Tünel. The entrance is through **Atlas Pasajı**, one of the old covered arcades off the street.

The cinema takes its name from the year it opened. After a long restoration it reopened as part of the Istanbul Cinema Museum, which shares the building. The museum makes a good stop before a film starts.

Inside, it's a single proper auditorium, not a multiplex box. That's a big part of why festival screenings here feel like an event. The most talked-about titles usually fill the room.

## Getting to the cinemas

For Atlas 1948, the M2 metro is the simplest option:

- **Taksim station (M2)**: come up at Taksim Square and walk down İstiklal. It's a short, flat walk.
- **Şişhane station (M2)**: useful from the Galata or Karaköy side. A walk up through Tünel leads onto the lower end of İstiklal.
- **Tünel funicular**: the historic underground funicular climbs from Karaköy to the bottom of İstiklal.
- **Nostalgic tram**: the red heritage tram runs the length of İstiklal. It's slow, but handy on tired legs.

An **Istanbulkart**, the city's tap-on transit card, covers the metro, funicular and tram on one card. The 2026 programme also included three other cinemas, and each ticket carried the address of its screening, so not every film was at Atlas.

## Booking without missing out

Tickets are the whole game at Filmekimi. A short run with limited screenings means the films people are talking about tend to sell out, and the opening weekend goes fastest. For 2026, tickets were sold through the official İKSV Filmekimi site, which organisers presented as the source to trust over any reseller.

Some habits regulars rely on:

- **A shortlist first.** Regulars read the full programme, rank a top five, then book in order rather than waiting for every slot to look perfect.
- **The subtitle line on each listing.** Films are generally shown in their original language. Whether English subtitles are included varies by title, and the listing says which.
- **The venue on every ticket.** With four cinemas, it's easy to end up with two films back to back at venues that are a real journey apart.
- **The confirmation on a phone.** A ticket ready to scan at the door keeps the queue moving.

Tickets for the 2026 edition were sold online and at the box office. Card payment is standard almost everywhere in central Istanbul.

## Doing a festival day like an Istanbul regular

Istanbul audiences take Filmekimi seriously, and a few unwritten rules make the day go smoothly.

Regulars leave time to spare. The crowds on İstiklal can slow a five-minute walk to fifteen, especially in the evening. Festival screenings tend to start on time, and walking in after the lights go down means fumbling for a seat in a full house.

Phones go away completely, and that includes the glow of checking messages. People stay seated through the credits more than you might think, since festival films often end on a final shot people want to see out. When a director or guest introduces a film, the room is usually quiet and ready by the scheduled time.

Between films, the routine is simple: step out into the arcade or onto the street, grab a tea or a quick bite, and argue about what you've just seen. A simit from a street cart is the classic way to keep going between screenings.

## Between screenings on İstiklal

Atlas 1948's location is a big part of Filmekimi's appeal. The gaps between films land you on one of the city's busiest streets. A few things fit into an hour or two:

- **Istanbul Cinema Museum**: in the same building as the cinema, the obvious first stop.
- **Çiçek Pasajı**: the old "Flower Passage" arcade further down İstiklal, lined with meyhane restaurants.
- **Galatasaray High School gates**: a landmark on the street and a common meeting point.
- **Galata Tower**: downhill past Tünel, a good walk for a longer gap.
- **Taksim Square**: at the top of the street, where the metro is.

İstiklal is crowded from late afternoon through the night. In a ninety-minute gap, one stop fits far better than three.

## Planning your ten days

Mid-October suits a festival like this. Evenings cool down, which makes a night of back-to-back films indoors feel natural.

As for how many films: two a day is comfortable for most visitors, while three makes a proper festival marathon. Weekday screenings are usually easier to get into than weekend ones, purely because more people can make weekends.

For visitors in Istanbul for only part of the run, the programme usually sets the shape of the trip. The films are fixed, while the city's other sights are still there the day after.
