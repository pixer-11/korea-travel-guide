## Half a metre of track to the end of the world

The track is barely half a metre wide. It's narrow enough that the carriages look like they belong to a model railway, and the small steam locomotive at the front comes up to roughly shoulder height. The Southern Fuegian Railway is this line, known in Spanish as the Ferrocarril Austral Fueguino and sold to visitors as the Tren del Fin del Mundo.

The ride takes about 1.5 hours in vintage-style carriages. It follows the Pipo River valley west of Ushuaia and finishes inside Tierra del Fuego National Park. It's one of the most-reviewed attractions in Argentine Patagonia, and the reviews are consistently strong. Don't expect a quiet ride. Expect full carriages, a running commentary and a lot of camera windows.

## The prisoners' railway

The line has a harsh history. In the early 1900s, inmates of the Ushuaia prison laid the original railway and rode it into the forest to cut timber. That wood heated the prison and built the town. The convict railway closed in the mid-20th century, and the current narrow-gauge line was revived for tourists in the 1990s along part of the old route.

The prison is still standing in town as the Museo Marítimo y del Presidio. Visit it before the train and the ride makes more sense. You'll have seen the cell blocks that the timber and the prisoners went back to.

## Getting out to Estación Fin del Mundo

The departure station is at RN3 km 3065, on the road west of Ushuaia toward the national park. No city bus is worth relying on for this, so most visitors get there one of these ways:

- **Taxi or remís** from downtown: a short drive of roughly 15 minutes. Agree a pickup time if you're riding the train both ways.
- **Tour agency transfer**: many Ushuaia agencies combine the train with a minibus through the national park. This is the easiest option on a cruise day.
- **Rental car**: the station has its own parking, and RN3 carries straight on into the park afterwards.

The station is a working terminal with a ticket hall, a café and a souvenir shop. Arrive with enough time to collect tickets before your departure is called.

## Fifteen minutes at La Macarena

The one pause on the ride is at Estación Cascada de la Macarena. Passengers get off here for a short stop of around a quarter of an hour. A boardwalk and a short climb lead to a viewpoint over the waterfall and the valley. You'll also find a reconstruction showing how the Yámana people, the region's original canoe-going inhabitants, sheltered here.

The stop is short, and everyone climbs at once. If you want the viewpoint without a crowd, go up first while others are still photographing the locomotive. Leave enough margin to get back, because the train doesn't wait long.

## Through the tree cemetery

Past La Macarena, the valley opens into what guides call the cemetery of trees. It's a wide, boggy stretch dotted with short, cut stumps, and those are the prisoners' work. They felled the trees by hand, and in this cold, slow-growing climate the stumps have lasted more than a century.

Commentary plays through the carriages in Spanish and English. After the stumps the line runs past peat bog and lenga forest with the Andes in the background, then ends at Estación Parque Nacional inside the park itself.

The park charges its own entry, separate from the train ticket. The current rate is posted at the park gate and on Argentina's national parks website.

## Booking ahead and beating the cruise-ship crush

This is a very popular attraction. Most of the planning is about avoiding the crowds, not finding the place.

- **Book ahead.** Departures fill up in the November–March season, especially on days when cruise ships are in port and tour groups take whole blocks of seats. Reserve through the railway's official website or a downtown agency.
- **Watch the weekend.** On Saturdays and Sundays the station is busiest from 11am to 5pm, which covers most of the operating day. If weekends are your only option, book the first departure after the 8:30am opening. If you can, go on a weekday.
- **Go late on a weekday.** Currently the station is quietest between 4pm and 5pm on weekdays. Check the timetable first, because the station closes at 5pm and the ride takes 1.5 hours. A late visit may only work for one direction or the station facilities.
- **Choose your class.** There are several classes of seat. Higher classes get more space and on-board service. The official site lists what each one includes.
- **Sit for the view.** Ask at the ticket desk which side faces the river valley on your direction of travel.

Most people pay by card at the ticket hall and on the website. Tipping isn't expected for the train itself. A small thank-you for a good tour guide on an agency package is common.

## Seasons at the bottom of the map

The train runs year-round, but it changes a lot with the season. In the southern summer you'll get long daylight, green bog and the busiest departures. In winter the valley is under snow, the carriages are heated, and the steam stands out against the white hills.

Departure times change through the year. At the moment the station opens 8:30am–5pm every day of the week. Weather this far south changes fast, so pack a windproof layer even in January. Have something warm for the open-air stop at La Macarena.

## After the last station: Lapataia and the coast

Most riders don't come back by train. They continue into the park by road. A few places are worth the time:

- **Bahía Lapataia**: the end of Ruta Nacional 3, with the well-known sign marking the end of the road and boardwalks over the bay.
- **Laguna Verde**: a short stop with a view of the river and the mountains.
- **Senda Costera**: a coastal trail along the Beagle Channel, for walkers with a few spare hours.

Plan for half a day if you do the train one way and the park by vehicle. If you hike as well, it's a full day. Back in Ushuaia, the prison museum is the right place to finish.
