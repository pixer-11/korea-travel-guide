## Why go

Banyan Garden is a pocket of shade in the middle of a working university campus. You come for one tree, really — a banyan so old and so wide that its aerial roots have dropped down and thickened into secondary trunks, turning a single organism into something that reads like a small grove.

Around it spreads a lawn dotted with smaller banyans, their canopies knitting together overhead into a green ceiling. Sunlight comes through in coins, not sheets.

## Getting there

The garden sits on NCKU's Guang-Fu Campus, at No. 1 Dasyue Road in Tainan's East District. It's an easy walk or bike ride from central Tainan — many visitors combine it with a stop at nearby Cheng Kung Lake, also on campus, or with the shops and eateries around Cheng Kung University's main gate.

There's no dedicated parking lot for visitors, so most people arrive on foot, by bicycle (Tainan's YouBike stations are common nearby), or by scooter. Because it's inside a real, active campus, expect to share the paths with students on bikes and between classes.

## What to see

The star is the old banyan itself — its trunk has spread wide enough that walking its full circumference takes a minute or two. Look up and you'll see the aerial roots still descending, some thin as string, others already fused into wood.

Beyond the tree, the garden functions as an open lawn:

- Grass wide enough for a full picnic spread
- Benches and low walls for sitting in shade
- Paths connecting to other parts of the historic Guang-Fu campus, including older Japanese-era buildings

It's unfussy. There's no ticket booth, no signage overload — just a big tree and a lawn built for lingering.

## When to go

Because it's part of a university, the rhythm of the garden follows the academic calendar. Term-time weekday afternoons bring students out between classes; weekends bring more families with kids and picnic blankets.

The garden is open 24 hours, every day, so there's no risk of arriving to locked gates. That said, this is Tainan — midday sun is intense for much of the year, so late afternoon or early evening, when the heat breaks and the light turns gold under the canopy, is the most comfortable window.

Early morning is your best bet for having the lawn mostly to yourself, before students arrive for the day.

## How to visit like a local

Nobody here is chasing a single "best" hour to beat crowds — this is a well-known spot, not a hidden one, so plan around the campus's own schedule instead.

- Avoid early afternoon on weekdays during term if you want quiet — that's when classes let out and foot traffic peaks near the garden.
- Bring your own mat or blanket; there are no rentals, and grass sitting is the norm rather than benches alone.
- Treat it as a campus, not a park attraction — keep noise down near classroom buildings, and don't block paths students are cycling through.
- No entry fee and no ticket gate, so there's nothing to pre-book; just walk in.
- If you're cycling in on a shared bike, look for a proper rack rather than leaving it on the lawn.
- Locals and students often call it 榕園 (Róngyuán) — useful if you're asking for directions on campus.

## Nearby

Pair your visit with a slow loop of the rest of Guang-Fu Campus. The nearby lake and older brick buildings from the university's Japanese colonial-era origins make for an easy 30–45 minute add-on walk after you've had your fill of shade under the banyan.
