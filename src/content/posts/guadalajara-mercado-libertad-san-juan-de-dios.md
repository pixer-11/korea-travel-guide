## Three Floors Under a Concrete Roof

It's one of the biggest indoor markets in Latin America. Architect Alejandro Zohn designed Mercado Libertad, and it opened in 1958. Tapatíos still call it San Juan de Dios, after the barrio and the old church nearby. It's a modernist block of bare concrete, and daylight comes in through raised sections of the roof onto open galleries.

Inside you get narrow aisles, stall after stall packed to the ceiling, and staircases that all seem to lead somewhere else. You won't find this one in a quiet corner. It ranks among the most-reviewed places in Guadalajara, and it's busy all day, every day. So treat it like a destination in its own right, not something to squeeze in.

## The Fonda Level: Birria, Tortas Ahogadas and Jericalla

The best reason to come is on the middle floor. That's where the fondas are: dozens of small kitchens, each with a counter and a row of stools. Cooks and waiters call out from every stall as you walk by. Most kitchens have their pots and comals in full view, and laminated menus are taped up or handed to you.

This is the place to eat the Jalisco classics:

- **Birria**: goat or beef stewed in chile, served with its consomé, chopped onion, cilantro and lime.
- **Torta ahogada**: a crusty birote roll filled with pork and soaked in tomato and chile sauce. Ask for it *media ahogada* if you want it less fiery.
- **Pozole**: a hominy stew, usually rojo here, with radish and oregano on the side.
- **Jericalla**: Guadalajara's custard with a burnt top, the usual dessert.
- **Tejuino and fresh juices**: tejuino is a cold fermented corn drink with lime and salt, and the juice stands blend fruit to order.

Before you sit, take a full lap of the floor. Plenty of fondas serve the same dishes. The difference is in who has a free stool and whose pots look busiest.

## Ground Floor: Produce, Sweets and Crafts

Downstairs is the everyday market. You'll find stacks of fruit and chiles, butchers, and stalls of dried goods. The Mexican sweet stalls are worth slowing down for. Look for cajeta, cocadas, tamarind candies and sugared fruit, piled up and sold by the piece or by weight.

The crafts are mixed in here too. Look for leather goods, huaraches, hats, woven bags and pottery, much of it from around Jalisco. Quality varies a lot from stall to stall, so check the stitching and the soles before you buy.

## Upstairs: Electronics, Clothes and Everything Else

The top level is a different market altogether. It's mostly phone accessories, electronics, clothing, footwear and stalls that are hard to label. The aisles here feel tighter, and the selling is more relentless.

Come up to see the scale of the place, not because you need anything. If you do buy electronics, test the item at the stall before you pay. Assume there's no warranty or return.

## Paying, Bargaining and Getting Waved Over

Bring cash in pesos, and plenty of small bills and coins. Some larger stalls take cards, but most of the market runs on cash. The restrooms usually have an attendant who charges a small fee in coins, so keep some loose change handy.

How you bargain depends on what you're buying:

- **Crafts, leather and clothes**: polite haggling is normal. Ask "¿Cuánto cuesta?", then make a lower offer and stay friendly.
- **Food, produce and sweets**: don't haggle. Prices are fixed, or set by weight.
- **At the fondas**: sit down, order, and pay at the end. Waving at the person who served you is enough. Leaving a tip in cash is customary.

You'll hear "¡Pásele!" ("come in!") at every food stall. A smile and a "gracias" lets you keep walking. In the crowded aisles, keep your phone and wallet in a front pocket, and wear your backpack on your front.

The doors are open 8am to 8pm every day. It's very much a working market, though, and some stalls start packing up before closing. The food level is at its best from late morning through the afternoon. Weekend afternoons draw the most local families, so a weekday morning usually means calmer aisles.

## Getting There Along Plaza Tapatía

The market is at Calle Dionisio Rodríguez 52, next to Calzada Independencia in the San Juan de Dios district. The closest metro stop is **San Juan de Dios station on Line 2**, a short walk away.

From the historic centre, walk instead. Start at the Cathedral, near Guadalajara Centro station on Line 3, and head east along the pedestrian Plaza Tapatía. You'll pass the Teatro Degollado and the long fountains. At the far end of the plaza, the market sits just to your right.

## Making a Morning of It: Cabañas and the Mariachis

You can pair the market with Hospicio Cabañas, a short walk away at the eastern end of Plaza Tapatía. It's a UNESCO World Heritage site, and its chapel is covered in José Clemente Orozco murals, including *The Man of Fire* in the dome. Many visitors see Cabañas first and come over to the fondas for lunch.

Just south of the market, across the road, is the Plaza de los Mariachis. Musicians wait there to play for a fee per song, and the plaza fills up later in the day. Take care around the busy road, and enjoy it as people-watching rather than a set show.
