## What's actually inside

Musée d'Aquitaine covers the Bordeaux region from prehistory to the present, in one continuous chronological walk. Ground floor: Paleolithic carvings, Gallo-Roman mosaics, medieval Bordeaux under English rule. Upstairs: the port's 18th-century boom, built on sugar, indigo and the slave trade, told through ship models, plantation ledgers and portraits of the merchant families who profited.

The colonial and slavery galleries are the museum's most discussed rooms. Bordeaux was France's second-largest slave-trading port, and the museum doesn't soften that history. Labels are in French and English throughout.

A separate ethnography wing holds artifacts from Africa, Oceania and the Americas, collected during the same colonial period the upstairs galleries examine.

## Getting there and getting in

The museum sits at 20 Cours Pasteur, a five-minute walk from Place de la Victoire and the tram stops there (lines B and C). It's inside a former university building, so the entrance is grander than a typical museum front door: expect a stone courtyard before you reach the ticket desk.

- Closed Monday
- Open Tuesday to Sunday, 11am to 6pm
- Busiest on weekends between 1pm and 6pm
- Permanent collection has historically been free in French municipal museums of this type; confirm current pricing for temporary exhibitions before you go

## Timing your visit

Weekday mornings right after the 11am opening are the calmest window here; school groups and weekend crowds haven't arrived yet. If you can only come on a Saturday or Sunday, get there at opening rather than after lunch. The 1pm–6pm weekend stretch is when the galleries fill up, especially the ground-floor prehistory rooms near the entrance.

Plan for two hours minimum if you want to read through the slavery and colonial exhibits properly rather than skim them. Add thirty minutes for the ethnography wing if world artifacts interest you.

## Reading the museum the way it's meant to be read

Start at the prehistoric galleries on the ground floor and move forward in time; the layout is built for a linear walk, not random wandering. Skipping straight to the 18th-century wine-money rooms means missing the medieval English-rule context that explains why Bordeaux's port grew the way it did.

Bring earbuds if you want the audio guide content on your own phone; check at the desk whether one is offered in your language. Photography is generally allowed without flash in the permanent galleries, but temporary exhibitions sometimes restrict it, so ask at the desk first.

The museum shop near the exit sells regional history books in French, useful if you want to go deeper after the visit but don't read French fluently: skip it unless that's a genuine interest.
