## Why go

You come here for one man's story, told through his own walls. Tjong A Fie was a Chinese-Indonesian tycoon who helped build modern Medan, and this mansion was his home from 1895 until his death in 1921.

Step through the heavy front doors and the street noise of Jalan Ahmad Yani drops away. The layout blends Chinese carved timber screens, Malay verandas and Dutch colonial windows and tiles in a way that still feels coherent, not cluttered.

## What you'll see inside

The house unfolds room by room, led by a guide rather than left to wander alone. You'll pass through an open central courtyard where light pours down onto worn marble floors.

Highlights to look for:

- The ballroom, once used for lavish parties and still strung with old chandeliers
- The family altar room, thick with incense residue and ancestral portraits
- Upstairs bedrooms with original furniture and black-and-white family photographs
- A small gallery of documents tracing Tjong A Fie's rise from penniless immigrant to plantation and banking magnate

Notice the mix of dragon motifs and European stained glass in the same doorway — a quiet clue to how cosmopolitan this corner of Sumatra once was.

## Getting there

The mansion sits in Kesawan, Medan's old colonial trading quarter, on Jalan Jend. Ahmad Yani No.105. It's an easy walk from Merdeka Walk and the old Medan train station, both just a few minutes away on foot.

Most visitors arrive by ride-hailing app (Grab or Gojek), taxi, or on foot if staying downtown. Parking is limited right on the street, so factor in a short walk if you drive.

## When to go

The mansion is open daily from 9am to 5pm. Foot-traffic data shows weekday mornings between 9am and 10am are the quietest window all week.

On weekends, expect a steadier flow of visitors, with the busiest stretch between 11am and 4pm. If you want the courtyards to yourself for photos, an early weekday visit is your best bet.

Plan for 45 minutes to an hour, a little longer if you linger over the document gallery or ask the guide questions.

## How to visit like a local

This is a well-known stop on Medan's heritage trail, not a hidden find, so plan around the crowds rather than expecting solitude.

- **Timing:** Arrive right at 9am on a weekday for the emptiest rooms and best light in the courtyard.
- **Guides:** Entry typically includes a short guided walkthrough — listen closely, since much of the family history isn't posted on signage.
- **Payment:** Bring small cash for the entrance donation/ticket and any souvenirs; card acceptance is inconsistent at small heritage sites like this.
- **Dress:** Wear something you can slip shoes off in if asked near the altar room, and keep voices low there — it's still an active family shrine.
- **Etiquette:** Photography is generally fine in public rooms, but ask before photographing the altar or any staff/guides directly.
- **Common mistake:** Rushing through in 15 minutes. The value here is in the stories your guide tells, not just the architecture, so slow down and ask questions.
- **Pair it with:** A walk down Jalan Ahmad Yani to see Kesawan's other colonial-era shophouses, or a stop at nearby Merdeka Walk for food afterward.

Since hours and ticket details can shift, it's worth a quick check before you go, especially around local holidays when many Medan attractions adjust their schedules.

## Nearby and after

Kesawan rewards a slow stroll once you're done inside. The old Medan train station and London Sumatra building are both within easy walking distance, giving you a fuller picture of the city's colonial-era commercial district.

Merdeka Walk, a few minutes away, is a reliable spot for a coffee or meal to unwind after the tour, especially in the early afternoon once you've beaten the mansion's busier hours.
