The Central Harbourfront is a strip of reclaimed land facing Victoria Harbour, normally used for exhibitions, concerts and the odd temporary carnival. During the festival it typically fills with rows of white marquee tents, folding tables and standing bars, each one rented out to a wine producer, a distillery, or a restaurant group pouring and plating for visitors. That is the Hong Kong Wine & Dine Festival, and it is the largest event of its kind in the city.

## What the festival actually is

Over 300 booths were announced for October 29 to November 1, 2026. The festival typically puts wine importers from Bordeaux, Australia and Chile next to Hong Kong restaurant groups doing char siu or dim sum by the glass. Whisky and craft beer get their own corners too, so it is not exclusively a wine event despite the name.

Visitors usually buy coupons or a festival wristband at entry, then use them to sample pours and small plates booth by booth. It works like a food market crossed with a wine fair: people graze, compare, and carry a plastic cup around all afternoon.

## Getting to the Harbourfront

The venue sits on the Wan Chai side of the harbour, an easy walk from Wan Chai MTR station or the Hong Kong Convention and Exhibition Centre. Star Ferry piers on both the Wan Chai and Central sides are within a 10 to 15 minute walk of the site.

- MTR: Wan Chai station (Island Line), Exit A1, then signs toward the waterfront
- Star Ferry: Wan Chai Pier, short walk east along the promenade
- Taxi drop-off is possible but traffic backs up on peak evenings, so the MTR is more reliable

## Tickets and what they cost

Entry is typically ticketed with tiered options: a basic entry pass, and packages bundled with wine coupons or tasting glasses. Prices and ticket tiers have varied year to year.

Weekend afternoons and evenings usually draw the heaviest crowds at the festival, and on-site ticket queues can eat into tasting time before the first glass is poured.

## Wine & Dine Month: the festival's long tail

The harbourfront programme was set to end on November 1. Wine & Dine Month extended through November, with participating restaurants across Hong Kong offering set menus, wine pairings and promotional deals tied to the festival branding.

The month was meant to give visitors arriving later in November a way into the festival after the harbourfront weekend, through restaurants tagged as part of Wine & Dine Month by the Hong Kong Tourism Board.

## The walk-the-loop rhythm

Regulars treat the festival less like a sit-down meal and more like a loop. Booths line both sides of a long harbourfront path, so the usual approach is one slow lap first, just to see what is on offer, before doubling back to spend coupons.

A typical first loop looks like this:

- Scouting both wine and food rows on the first pass without buying
- Noting which booths have the longest lines and whether they are worth the wait
- Circling back for top picks once the full spread has been seen
- Pacing drinks against the food stalls rather than sampling reds on an empty stomach

Because the venue sits directly on the water, the last hour before sunset is when the harbourfront view competes hardest with the tents for attention. Skyline views across to Central and Kowloon are part of the draw for a lot of visitors, not just the food.

## Eating and drinking around the tasting

Portions at individual booths are small by design, since the point is sampling widely rather than filling up at one stand. Most visitors spread their tasting across a few hours rather than treating any single booth as a full meal.

- Cash or a mobile payment method is useful for coupon top-ups, since not every booth handles cards individually
- The ground is pavement, not grass, so comfortable shoes matter over several hours of standing
- Companions often split larger tasting packages for variety without overbuying pours

## Practical notes

Hong Kong in late October and early November is warm and generally dry, sitting in the tail end of the city's more comfortable season after summer humidity breaks. Evenings by the harbour catch a breeze, which makes standing around tents for hours more bearable than it would be in July.

Dates, booth lineups, ticket tiers and exact venue boundaries within the Harbourfront have shifted between editions of the festival.
