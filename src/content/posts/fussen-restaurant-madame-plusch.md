## A table that eats from the middle

Order spätzle for yourself and you get a side dish. At Madame Plüsch, the dishes are built to be shared. The house's reputation rests on platters that go in the middle of the table: spätzle noodles, smoked sausages and sauerkraut, with everyone reaching in.

That changes how a meal here works. Rather than ordering one main each, a group decides together, passes plates and lingers. The dining rooms are traditional in style. That suits the cooking, which belongs to the hearty end of the Bavarian and Swabian kitchen rather than anything light.

## What arrives on the platter

The three anchors are the ones the restaurant is known for. If you have not eaten in this corner of Germany before, here is what you are getting:

- **Spätzle:** soft, irregular egg noodles, scraped or pressed into boiling water. This corner of Bavaria borders the Allgäu, where the classic version is Kässpatzen, layered with cheese and topped with fried onions.
- **Smoked sausages:** dense and well seasoned, the kind that wants mustard beside it.
- **Sauerkraut:** fermented cabbage, sharp enough to cut through the richness of the other two.

The menu is posted at the restaurant and changes, so check it on the day rather than arriving set on one dish. Prices sit on the pricier side for Füssen. This is a proper sit-down meal, not a quick bite between sights.

## The four o'clock gap

The schedule is the thing most likely to catch you out. Madame Plüsch runs two separate services: lunch from 12 to 4pm, then dinner from 5 to 11pm. In that hour between them, the kitchen is not serving.

The weekly pattern matters just as much. The restaurant is **closed on Tuesdays and Wednesdays**. It opens Monday and Thursday through Sunday, with the same hours on each of those five days.

For a short stay, check those two dark days first. If you arrive on a Monday and leave on a Wednesday, Monday is your only chance. A late lunch also needs care: arrive at 3:30pm and you are eating against the clock before the break.

## Finding Drehergasse

Drehergasse is one of the lanes in Füssen's compact old town, and the restaurant sits at number 48. The whole historic centre can be walked end to end in a few minutes. Wherever you are staying in town, you will almost certainly reach it on foot.

From **Füssen station**, the end of the regional line from Munich, the old town is a short walk. The pedestrian shopping street Reichenstraße is the main route through the centre. Driving in is more trouble than it is worth, because the centre is pedestrian-heavy. Leave the car in one of the town car parks and walk in.

## Booking, paying and ordering

The sharing format and the pricier menu both suggest planning ahead, especially for dinner on Friday or Saturday. A few habits will smooth the evening:

- **Reserve, especially in summer.** Füssen fills with castle visitors from late spring to autumn, and town restaurants feel it at dinner. Booking a table is normal in Germany and nobody will think it fussy.
- **Order as a table.** Settle on the platters together, then add individual dishes if someone wants their own.
- **Ask about card payment.** Plenty of Bavarian restaurants still prefer cash or take only some cards. Ask when you book, or carry cash as a backup.
- **Tip the German way.** Service is included, but rounding up is customary. Rather than leaving coins on the table, tell the server the total you want to pay as they take your money. "Stimmt so" means "keep the change".
- **Wait for the bill.** Servers will not usually bring it unprompted. Catch their eye and say "Zahlen, bitte" when you are ready.

Plan on about one and a half to two hours. Sharing platters are slow food by design, and rushing them defeats the purpose.

## Fitting it around the castles

Most visitors are in Füssen for **Neuschwanstein** and **Hohenschwangau**, about four kilometres away. Buses run there from Füssen station. A castle day usually takes up the late morning and early afternoon, and that makes lunch here awkward. You get back into town just as the four o'clock gap begins.

Dinner is the more natural fit. Do the castles, rest at your hotel, then walk over for the 5pm opening or later. If you want lunch instead, keep it to a non-castle day. Take a slow morning in the old town, eat at noon and spend the afternoon by the river.

## Around the restaurant

The old town gives you plenty to fill the time before or after a meal. All of these are within easy walking distance:

- **Hohes Schloss:** the former summer residence of the prince-bishops of Augsburg. Its painted courtyard facades are a trompe-l'oeil, and it now houses a state art gallery.
- **St. Mang Abbey (Kloster St. Mang):** the former Benedictine monastery that is home to the Museum der Stadt Füssen. Its collection covers the town's history as a centre of lute and violin making.
- **The Lech:** the river runs along the edge of the old town. A short walk south brings you to the **Lechfall**, a stepped weir where the water drops through a narrow gorge. It makes a good walk after a heavy meal.

If you plan to see everything, the museums close earlier than the restaurant. Do the sights first and leave the platters for last.
