A squat, three-sided tower of grey coral stone sits on a low point of land where Bohol's west coast bends. On one side are rooftops and palms. On the other, open water runs out toward Cebu. Punta Cruz has stood watching that water since 1796, and it is still the kind of place you can often have nearly to yourself.

## A Lookout Built for Raiders

This was a working watchtower, not an ornament. Spanish-era Maribojoc built it in 1796 under the Augustinian friars. Lookouts posted here watched the strait for raiding boats from the south, and a warning from the tower gave the town time to react.

Its shape is what sets it apart. Most Philippine coastal watchtowers are round or square. Punta Cruz is triangular, so each face looks out over a different stretch of sea. The walls are cut coral stone, the same material as the colonial churches along this coast. Up close you can see the pitted, porous blocks that give that building style its texture.

The name comes from the cross that the townspeople, or *Punta Cruz*, raised on the point. Local tradition credits it with protecting the town from harm coming in off the sea.

## Climbing the Triangle on the Point

The tower is small, and a visit is easy to take in. You walk across the grounds to a compact stone structure, step inside and climb up to the viewing level. The space at the top is tight. If a few people are already up there, wait your turn at the bottom instead of squeezing in.

What you get from the top:

- **West and north:** the Cebu Strait, with Cebu's coastline visible on clear days
- **Inland:** Maribojoc's low roofs and coconut palms running back from the shore
- **Below:** the rocky shoreline and the point the tower was built to guard

Thirty to forty-five minutes covers the tower and the grounds. Stay longer only if you're waiting for the light to change.

## The Weekday-Only Timing Quirk

This is where visitors most often get caught out. Google's current listing shows the tower open **Monday to Friday, 9am–8pm**, and **closed on Saturday and Sunday**. A lot of Bohol day trips happen at weekends, so it's easy to arrive at a locked gate.

Heritage sites in the Philippines can change their hours around holidays and fiestas. Check the listing or ask your driver before you set off. On a normal weekday, the long evening hours mean the late-afternoon light falls well inside the schedule.

## Light Over the Cebu Strait

The tower faces west across open water, so the late afternoon is its best hour. Low sun hits the coral stone from the sea side and turns the grey walls warm. Boats crossing the strait show up as silhouettes.

Plan to arrive an hour or so before sunset. That gives you time to climb and walk the shoreline, then find a place on the grounds to watch the light go. At midday the stone and the open grounds get little shade, so bring water and a hat if you come then.

## Getting There From Tagbilaran

Maribojoc is on the coastal road north of Tagbilaran City. The usual route goes through Cortes, and the drive takes roughly 30–40 minutes depending on traffic. Most visitors come one of these ways:

- **Hired van or car with driver:** the easiest option, and simple to add to a west-coast loop
- **Tricycle:** fine from Maribojoc town itself; agree on the fare before you get in
- **Jeepney or bus** toward Loon: ask to be dropped at Maribojoc, then take a tricycle to the point
- **Scooter:** the coastal road is straightforward, but watch for tricycles pulling out

If you come by public transport, tell the driver "Punta Cruz" rather than "the watchtower". The barangay name is what people here know it by.

## Visiting a Quiet Heritage Site

The tower doesn't get much tour-bus traffic. That makes it a calmer stop than the Chocolate Hills circuit, and it also means services are basic. Some habits make the visit go smoothly:

- **Carry small peso notes.** Rural Bohol runs on cash, and nobody wants to break a large bill for a small purchase or tricycle fare.
- **Ask about any fee at the entrance.** Arrangements at small municipal sites can change, so check what's posted when you arrive.
- **Treat the cross and tower respectfully.** Many in Maribojoc see the point as having religious meaning. Don't climb on the walls or sit on the cross base for photos.
- **Greet people on the grounds.** A simple "Maayong hapon" (good afternoon, in Cebuano/Boholano) goes a long way.
- **Take your rubbish with you.** Bins can be scarce at small coastal sites like this one.

Your driver will wait while you look around. A tip at the end of the day is appreciated but never required.

## Pairing It With Maribojoc and the West Coast

The tower fits neatly into a slow west-coast day. Maribojoc's Holy Cross Parish Church in the town centre was badly damaged in the 2013 Bohol earthquake. The church is still a reminder of how hard that quake hit this coast.

Further north, Loon has its own Our Lady of Light church, which was also struck in 2013, plus a coastline of mangroves and fishing villages. Heading back south, the Abatan River near Cortes is known for evening firefly boat trips. That makes a good finish after a late-afternoon stop at the tower.

A sensible weekday order: Loon in the morning, Maribojoc's church at midday, Punta Cruz for the late light, then the river after dark.
