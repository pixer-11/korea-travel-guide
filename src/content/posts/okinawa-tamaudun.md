## A palace built for the dead

You enter through a low stone gate into a courtyard paved with coral gravel. Ahead stands a long wall of grey Ryukyu limestone. Behind it sit three tomb chambers carved into the rock, with roofs shaped to copy the wooden palace buildings of the Ryukyu court. Carved stone lions keep watch from the parapets.

King Shō Shin built Tamaudun in 1501 to hold the bones of his father, Shō En. It remained the royal tomb of the Second Shō Dynasty until the kingdom ended in the 19th century. Since 2000 it has been a UNESCO World Heritage Site, one of the Gusuku Sites of the Kingdom of Ryukyu. It was named a National Treasure in 2018.

## Reading the three chambers

Each chamber had its own job, and the layout makes more sense once you know them:

- **Central chamber:** bodies were laid here first. Once only bones remained, they were washed in a ritual known as senkotsu.
- **East chamber:** the washed bones of kings and queens.
- **West chamber:** other members of the royal family allowed burial here.

To the left of the courtyard stands a stone stele carved in 1501. It names who could be interred in the tomb and curses anyone who breaks the rule. Before you reach the courtyard you pass the reception building, which has a small underground exhibit on Ryukyu burial customs. Most people spend 30 to 45 minutes here.

## Getting there and timing it

Take the Yui Rail to Shuri Station. From there it's roughly a 15-minute walk through quiet residential streets towards Shuri Castle Park. Tamaudun sits just off the approach to Shureimon Gate, so it fits easily into a castle visit. The full address is 1-3 Shuri Kinjo-cho, Naha, which is useful for a taxi.

It opens every day of the week from 9am to 6pm, including Mondays. The calmest window is 9am to 10am on weekdays, when you may have the courtyard nearly to yourself. Try not to come between 10am and 4pm on a weekend. That's when crowds peak and your photos of the facade will be full of other people.

Nearby stops pair well with the tomb:

- **Shuri Castle**, still being rebuilt after the 2019 fire. The rebuilding work is itself on view.
- **Shuri Kinjo-cho Stone-Paved Road**, a steep lane of old cobbles running downhill south of the castle.
- **Shureimon Gate**, the painted gate that appears on Japan's 2,000-yen note.

## Tomb manners in Shuri

This is still a grave, and Okinawans treat ancestral tombs with real care. Keep your voice down in the courtyard, and don't climb the walls, sit on the lion statues or step past any barrier.

Tickets are sold at the reception building before the gate. The current admission is posted there, so check it on the day. Come in the first hour and do the castle afterwards. You'll beat the rush and follow the old route downhill from the royal dead to the royal court.
