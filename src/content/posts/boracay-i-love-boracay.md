## Why go

Boracay's fame rests on four kilometers of White Beach, the powder sand and the sunset crowds at Station 2. "I Love Boracay" is a different kind of stop — a set of oversized wooden letters and a modest lookout point that hasn't been swallowed by the tourist machine the way the main beachfront has. You won't find tour buses idling outside or a queue snaking around the block. What you get instead is a slower few minutes: island light bouncing off corrugated rooftops, the smell of salt and frangipani drifting up from the shoreline below, a breeze that actually moves through the trees instead of the recycled hum of a beach bar's aircon.

## Getting there

The address places it in Malay, Aklan — the municipality on Panay Island that administers Boracay, reachable via Caticlan (Godofredo P. Ramos Airport, code MPH) or the slightly farther Kalibo International Airport. From either airport, it's a short tricycle or van ride to the Caticlan jetty port, then a five-to-ten-minute boat crossing to Boracay's Cagban or Tabon jetty. From the jetty, tricycles are the standard way to get around the island — flag one down or ask your hotel to arrange a ride, since there's no fixed public bus route. Most riders quote a fare on the spot; agree on it before you climb in. The spot sits inland from the main beach strip, so budget a short uphill walk or a tricycle drop-off rather than expecting beachfront access.

## What to see and do

The core draw is straightforward: the giant "I Love Boracay" lettering makes for a clean, uncluttered photo, and the vantage point behind it opens up over rooftops toward the sea — a good spot to actually see the island's shape rather than just its sand. Bring a wide-angle lens or just your phone's panorama mode; the letters are large enough that getting the whole word in frame from up close takes some backing up. Because this isn't a ticketed attraction with a gift shop and a queue system, there's no formal "tour" to take — you look, you photograph, you move on, and the whole stop rarely needs more than 20 to 30 minutes.

Afterward, most visitors continue on to the more famous fixtures nearby: White Beach for swimming and sunset drinks, D'Mall for food stalls and souvenir shopping, or Bulabog Beach on the other coast if you're there for kiteboarding season (roughly November to April, when the amihan wind blows steady).

## When to go

Boracay's dry season, November through May, is the safest bet for clear skies and calm seas — December through February brings the coolest, most comfortable air. Because this viewpoint has no shade structure and no indoor waiting area, aim for early morning or late afternoon rather than midday, both for the light (softer, more flattering in photos) and for comfort (the tropical sun directly overhead by noon is intense). Given its under-the-radar status, you're unlikely to fight for space at any hour, but late afternoon light gives the best photos and the mildest heat.

## How to visit like a local

Skip the late-morning rush that builds around the island's more famous landmarks and come here instead when you want a five-minute break from the beach crowd — locals and residents treat it as a passing curiosity rather than a destination, so there's no real "right time" beyond avoiding the harshest midday sun. Cash still rules for tricycle fares and small roadside purchases across Boracay, so keep small bills on hand; card and mobile payment (GCash is widely used locally) work at bigger restaurants and hotels but not for casual transport. There's no entrance fee or booking system here, so the only real etiquette is the usual courtesy of waiting your turn for a photo if anyone else is around, and not blocking the view for others while you set up a shot. The mistake most visitors make on Boracay generally is spending their entire trip on the main White Beach strip — a quick detour to a quieter spot like this one is a reminder that the island has corners beyond the sunset crowd.
