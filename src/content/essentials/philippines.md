**Quick answer:** Most nationalities, including the US, UK, EU, Canada, Australia, and New Zealand, can enter the Philippines visa-free for up to 30 days provided they have a passport valid six months beyond arrival, an onward ticket, and complete the mandatory eTravel registration; dial 911 anywhere in the country for police, fire, or medical emergencies.

## Visa & entry

Citizens of more than 150 countries, including the US, UK, EU nations, Australia, Canada, New Zealand, and many Southeast Asian countries, are granted visa-free entry on arrival, and this allows an initial stay of up to 30 days. This visa-free entry comes with conditions: you must hold a valid passport with at least six months remaining and show proof of onward travel within 30 days, and airlines often enforce this rule strictly, with passengers regularly denied boarding if they cannot present an onward ticket, even if otherwise eligible to enter.

There are important nationality-specific exceptions. Effective January 16, 2026, Chinese nationals are granted a non-extendable 14-day visa-free stay for tourism and business, provided they enter through designated ports like NAIA (Manila) or Mactan-Cebu. Indian nationals also have a distinct arrangement: since May 2025 the Department of Foreign Affairs grants 14 days visa-free entry for all Indian tourists, and an enhanced 30 days conditional visa-free entry for Indian tourists with valid visas or permanent residencies from select countries. Some nationalities, mainly across Africa, the Middle East, and parts of South Asia, still need a visa before departure, applied for at a Philippine embassy or consulate.

Every arriving foreign passenger, regardless of visa status, must also complete an online travel declaration: foreign travelers entering the Philippines must complete the Philippine government's eTravel registration before arrival, an online travel declaration that is mandatory for arriving foreign passengers, easy to complete, and completely free when submitted through the official website. If you want to stay longer, extensions are handled locally: visa extensions can be applied for at the Bureau of Immigration, and the initial 30 days can be extended up to an additional 29 days for a total of 59 days, with further monthly extensions possible up to a maximum cumulative stay of 36 months.

**Confirm before you fly:** entry rules, visa-free lists, and eTravel requirements can change with little notice, so always check the official Bureau of Immigration and eTravel sites listed below rather than relying on any guidebook, including this one.

## Getting around

Metro Manila's rail network is the backbone of city travel. It is made up of LRT-1, LRT-2, and MRT-3, which run along key corridors including a stretch of EDSA, and outside rush hour the trains are an efficient way to cover long distances that would crawl by car. The universal fare card is the beep card: it aims to reduce time spent queueing for tickets and streamline transfers from the three elevated railways, LRT1, LRT2, and MRT3, as well as P2P and provincial buses, ferries, and modern jeepneys. A card typically costs around ₱30 to ₱100 depending on where you buy it, and can be topped up at station booths, convenience stores, or via GCash and Maya apps.

Jeepneys remain the classic way to get around short in-city routes: fares run 13 to 20 pesos for any in-city hop, paid in cash directly to the conductor, who sits near the back and shouts destinations as you pass. Tricycles and habal-habal motorbikes fill in for shorter local hops, and all are cash-only, so keep small bills and coins handy. For point-to-point travel, ride-hailing is dominant: Grab lets you pay by cash or a linked card, and the country's popular e-wallets, chiefly GCash and Maya, are used widely for topping up transit cards and paying for rides. Between islands, domestic flights and ferries connect major destinations; book flights early in peak season and always check weather advisories before any inter-island ferry crossing, as services are frequently suspended when storm signals are raised.

## Money & costs

The Philippine Peso (₱/PHP) is the currency, and it is the only one officially accepted for everyday transactions. Besides the peso, no other currency is officially accepted in the Philippines, so plan to convert cash or withdraw pesos locally rather than paying in dollars or euros.

Cash still rules outside the big cities: credit cards are often accepted at major establishments in popular tourist areas, such as large grocery stores, upscale restaurants, and malls, but outside major cities and tourist hubs, cash remains the primary mode of payment. On smaller islands the gap widens further: most businesses such as tour operators, tricycles, markets, and budget guesthouses are cash-only, so you should never rely on being able to pay by card and should always have sufficient pesos on hand.

ATMs are widespread in cities but thin out fast in remote areas: on islands like Batanes, Camiguin, Siquijor, and some Palawan destinations, there may be one ATM in the entire municipality, and it is not always stocked or functioning. Withdrawal caps vary by bank, typically a maximum of PHP 10,000 to 20,000 per transaction, with Metrobank and BDO allowing up to PHP 20,000, so withdraw what you need in a major hub before heading to smaller islands. A travel-friendly multi-currency card such as Wise or Revolut, combined with a small cash buffer, is the most cost-effective approach; e-wallets like GCash and Maya are also increasingly used for local payments, transit top-ups, and Grab fares.

## Best time to visit

The Philippines has a clear seasonal split. Dry season, from November to May, is safer for travel, while typhoon season runs from June to November, with August to October being the peak. The Philippines gets about 20 typhoons per year, but most don't affect all tourist areas at once. Within the dry season, December to February is the peak, drawing the biggest crowds and highest prices, while September and early October offer good weather, fewer tourists, and cheaper rooms for travelers willing to gamble on the tail end of wet season. Whenever you travel in the June to November window, check forecasts, build flexibility into inter-island plans, and get travel insurance that covers trip interruption.

## Emergencies & safety

The national emergency number is simple to remember: 911 is the national emergency telephone number of the Philippines, managed by the Emergency 911 National Office, covering police, fire, and medical response nationwide. Calls are free even without phone credit, and English is an official language, so emergency operators communicate in both Filipino and English. The system has recently been consolidated: beginning September 11, 2026, anybody facing emergencies needs only dial 911, replacing more than 30 local emergency numbers, with the service free, available 24/7, and language-sensitive so calls in Tagalog, Cebuano, Ilocano, Waray, Tausug, and other dialects can be understood. For maritime incidents, emergencies are dispatched through 911, with the Philippine Coast Guard handling search and rescue at sea and also contactable via VHF Channel 16.

On overall safety: the Philippines is a safe and friendly destination for the vast majority of travellers, and headline tourist regions such as Palawan, Cebu, Bohol, Boracay, and Siargao call for nothing more than the everyday common sense you'd use anywhere. The main genuine exception is parts of western Mindanao around the Sulu archipelago, where many governments maintain travel advisories, well away from the standard tourist trail. Health-wise, plan ahead financially: the Philippines does not participate in broad reciprocal healthcare arrangements comparable to the EU's EHIC or Australasia's reciprocal health agreements, and foreign nationals should assume they are responsible for the full cost of medical treatment, making travel insurance strongly advisable.
