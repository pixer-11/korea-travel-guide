**Quick answer:** Most nationalities get 30 days visa-free on arrival in the Philippines (14 days for China and for some other categories), everyone must complete the free eTravel registration, and the peso is the currency, with 911 as the single nationwide emergency number since 2025; the dry season from roughly December to April is the most reliable time to visit, since June to November brings monsoon rains and typhoons.

## Visa & entry

The Philippines offers visa-free entry for 30 days to citizens of 157 countries, including the US, UK, EU, Canada, and Australia, and for most eligible nationalities these stays can be extended for up to 36 months via the Bureau of Immigration. Under Executive Order 408, nationals from qualified countries can enter the Philippines for tourism or business purposes without a visa for an initial stay of up to 30 days.

A significant change in 2026: effective 16 January 2026, Chinese nationals may enter the Philippines without the requirement of obtaining a visa for a stay of up to fourteen (14) days, and this privilege allows for a non-extendable 14-day stay, which is non-convertible to any other type of Philippine visa category. Indian nationals also have a special path: they get 14 days visa-free, and those holding a valid American, Japanese, Australian, Canadian, Schengen, Singapore or UK visa or residence permit may instead stay visa-free for 30 days.

Whatever your nationality, your passport must be valid for 6 months beyond your intended departure date from the Philippines, and airlines and immigration strictly enforce this requirement. Many visitors also get asked for proof of onward travel; you must show proof of onward travel within 30 days, and airlines often enforce this rule strictly, with passengers regularly denied boarding if they cannot present an onward ticket.

Every traveler, regardless of visa status, now needs a separate pre-arrival step: the eTravel Pass is the Philippine government's unified electronic travel declaration platform, administered by the Bureau of Immigration and the Bureau of Quarantine at all international ports of entry and departure. You may register online within 72 hours prior to your arrival into or departure from the Philippines, and you'll receive a QR code by email and on-screen, which you show at immigration or quarantine counters; a green QR code means you are cleared for entry, while a red one may require additional checks. Note that eTravel is not a visa, so if your nationality requires a visa, you must still apply for one separately. Beware of scams: the eTravel registration process is absolutely free of charge, and travelers are advised to register only at the official government website, etravel.gov.ph.

Because rules and eligible nationalities shift with little warning, **always confirm your specific requirements on the official Bureau of Immigration, DFA, and eTravel/e-Visa sites before you fly**, especially if you are not from one of the long-standing visa-free countries.

## Getting around

Metro Manila's main rail lines run on a shared contactless card: the beep card is the Philippines' unified contactless stored-value card for public transit, working across LRT-1, LRT-2, MRT-3, the EDSA Carousel, and most P2P bus routes. A new card costs PHP 100, which includes a non-refundable PHP 20 card fee and PHP 80 initial stored value, and it can be topped up at Bayad Centers, SM Bills Payment Centers, and via mobile apps for NFC-enabled devices.

Keep in mind the card's limits: beep does not pay for Grab, taxis, tricycles or traditional jeepneys, so those still need cash or an e-wallet. For everyday city trips, ride-hailing is popular and straightforward, while jeepneys and tricycles remain the cheapest options for short local hops, though for jeepneys you need to know the routes, fares are adjusted from time to time so you should check the posted fare matrix, and unlike a taxi there is no negotiation and no tipping, you simply tap and go or hand over the fare.

If you're flying between islands, note that no NAIA terminal has a direct rail connection; the LRT-1 extension brings you into the airport's general area for an onward connection, but for a flight it's better to take a car and budget generously for traffic. Given the country's archipelago geography, inter-island travel is typically by domestic flight or ferry, so build in buffer time, especially in stormy months.

## Money & costs

The currency is the Philippine peso (PHP), and cash still rules outside big cities: the Philippines runs on a mix of cash, cards, and crossed fingers; you can pay by card in cities and malls, but step outside the urban grid, or even into a beach shack, and you'll need pesos in your pocket. For ATMs, machines are everywhere in cities but can be flaky or run dry in smaller towns and islands, so bring a backup card and always get cash before heading to a remote area.

Budget travelers can eat and move cheaply: street food and simple meals can run well under a few hundred pesos, and train fares in Metro Manila are modest, with fares ranging from roughly ₱13 to ₱35 depending on distance on the beep-enabled rail network. Accommodation costs swing widely by season and island; expect resort towns like Boracay or El Nido to command a premium over inland or off-peak destinations.

## Best time to visit

The Philippines has a clear seasonal rhythm. The Philippines has two major seasons: the dry season from December to May, and the rainy season from June to November. Much of the country has a hot and dry climate from March to May, the southwest monsoon and the typhoons it brings predominate from June to October, and the dry, cooler season during the northeast monsoon lasts from November to February.

If you want the calmest seas and clearest skies for island-hopping, aim for the heart of the dry season: dry season, from December to May, is generally the safest bet, with clear skies, calm seas, and island-hopping in full swing, and January and February are cooler, good for hiking in the north or exploring volcanoes like Mayon. Typhoon risk is concentrated later in the wet season: the country enters typhoon season from June until September, experiencing about 10 to 15 typhoons a year, primarily affecting the eastern Pacific coastline, including Luzon, Samar, Leyte, and parts of Mindanao.

Budget-conscious travelers can look at shoulder months: the shoulder months are where savvy travelers live; late May and June sit just before the rains settle in, giving tail-end dry weather at near-low-season prices, while September through November is the back shoulder, with prices low, crowds thin, and weather steadily improving. Whatever month you choose, monitor forecasts once you land, since you can't out-schedule a typhoon, so it's worth accepting occasional delays.

## Emergencies & safety

The country now has a single, unified emergency number. Beginning September 11, 2025, anybody facing emergencies in the Philippines only needs to dial one hotline, 911, which replaced more than 30 local emergency numbers. The system is designed for accessibility: the service is free, available 24/7, and designed to be language-sensitive so calls in Tagalog, Cebuano, Ilocano, Waray, Tausug, and other Philippine dialects can be understood and acted upon. 911 calls are free and can be made without airtime credit, and English is an official language, so emergency operators communicate in both Filipino and English.

For maritime emergencies specifically, these are dispatched through 911, with the Philippine Coast Guard handling search and rescue at sea, also contactable via VHF Channel 16. Other still-useful direct numbers include the Philippine Red Cross hotline at 143, which provides blood services, disaster response, water rescue, and humanitarian assistance, available 24/7, and for weather, you can monitor PAGASA advisories by calling (02) 8284-0800 or checking pagasa.dost.gov.ph for storm signal levels and rainfall warnings.

Overall safety for tourists is reassuring, though services vary by region: visitors from countries with well-funded emergency services may notice differences in the Philippines, particularly in regions beyond the major cities, where police and fire services can be less resourced and response times less predictable. It's wise to identify the location of the nearest barangay hall and local police station when moving to a new area, as these local contacts can be just as valuable as national hotlines when an emergency develops quickly. During storm season, follow official guidance closely, including PAGASA storm signals for ferry and boat travel, and never assume that a numbered hotline reaches the same department nationwide without checking, since local variations still exist for non-911 lines.
