**Quick answer:** Most Western, ASEAN, and 150+ other nationalities can enter the Philippines visa-free for up to 30 days with a valid return ticket and 6-month passport validity, but rules vary by nationality and change often—always verify with the Bureau of Immigration before you fly.

## Visa & entry

Under Executive Order 408, citizens of more than 150 countries — including the US, UK, Canada, most of the EU, Australia, and Japan — can enter the Philippines without a visa for tourism or business purposes, typically for up to 30 days. The Philippines maintains a welcoming policy for over 150 countries, and under EO 408, nationals from qualified countries can enter the Philippines for tourism or business purposes without a visa for an initial stay of up to 30 days. Two notable exceptions were added recently: China (PRC) now has 14-day unconditional visa-free entry via NAIA and Mactan-Cebu airports (effective 16 January 2026), and India now has 14-day visa-free entry (effective 8 June 2025). Indian and Chinese nationals currently receive 14 days under a non-extendible arrangement and should check current rules before travelling.

If your country isn't on the visa-exempt list — for example Afghanistan, Algeria, Bangladesh, Iran, Iraq, Lebanon, Libya, North Korea, Pakistan, Palestine, Somalia, Syria, and Yemen — you'll need a visa in advance. Helpfully, the Philippines e-Visa (evisa.gov.ph) is now fully operational for key markets, allowing travelers to apply for their 9A visa remotely, available for citizens of India, Japan, South Korea, UAE, and several European nations.

Regardless of nationality, expect these standard checks at the border: your passport must be valid for 6 months beyond your intended departure date, and airlines and immigration strictly enforce this requirement. You'll also need proof of an onward/return ticket. Nearly all travelers must also complete eTravel registration within 72 hours before arrival (and also for departure) via the official eTravel portal.

Want to stay longer? A non-visa-required tourist wishing to remain beyond the original 30-day admission may request an extension, and according to the Bureau of Immigration, an initial extension of 29 days may be applied for through its authorised offices. Note that once your stay exceeds 59 days, the Bureau of Immigration requires you to obtain an Alien Certificate of Registration Identity Card (ACR I-Card), typically processed alongside your first extension beyond the 59-day mark.

**Bottom line:** rules genuinely shift year to year (e-visa rollouts, new bilateral arrangements, eTravel tweaks), so confirm your specific nationality's requirements on the official Bureau of Immigration and eTravel sites shortly before departure — don't rely solely on this guide or older blog posts.

## Getting around

**Metro Manila rail & buses:** The city's three train lines — LRT-1, LRT-2, and MRT-3 — use a common contactless card. The Beep card is the stored-value card used on LRT-1, LRT-2, MRT-3, and some bus lines; buy one at any rail station for around PHP 100 (including a card fee), then load additional credit at station kiosks or convenience stores. Fares are distance-based: roughly ₱15 (1–5 stations), ₱20 (6–10 stations), or ₱30 (11–20 stations) on LRT-1, with similar tiers on the other lines. For more comfortable cross-city trips, UV Express vans run fixed express routes and are typically PHP 35–70 per trip, while air-conditioned Point-to-point (P2P) buses connect suburban areas with major business districts for fixed fares of PHP 60–100 with reserved seating.

**Jeepneys:** The classic jeepney remains the backbone of local transport nationwide. Jeepneys are the most widely used form of public transport in most Philippine cities; routes are fixed, and you board from the roadside and pay the driver or a conductor, typically PHP 13–15 minimum in Metro Manila. Keep small bills handy, as change is made by hand. Note that modern articulated minibuses are replacing old-style jeepneys under the government's PUV Modernisation Programme, and the transition is ongoing with coverage varying by route.

**Ride-hailing:** Grab is the dominant app-based option in cities, useful when you don't want to navigate jeepney routes. Credit cards can be linked directly in the app, or you can pay cash to the driver.

**Getting between islands:** Since the Philippines is an archipelago, inter-island travel almost always means flying or taking a ferry. Domestic journey times should not be underestimated — flights or ferries are often required between destinations, and weather disruptions may also affect transport, especially during periods of heavy rain or tropical storms. Book domestic flights and ferries with some flexibility built in, especially June–November.

## Money & costs

The currency is the Philippine Peso (₱/PHP). Bills come in denominations of 20, 50, 100, 200, 500, and 1,000, and coins are 1, 5, 10, and 20 pesos. Note that the Philippines introduced new polymer banknotes starting with the ₱1,000 note in 2022 (followed by ₱500, ₱100, and ₱50 versions in 2025), and the ₱20 banknote is being gradually phased out and replaced with a ₱20 coin — both remain valid during the transition.

As of late July 2026, roughly the exchange rate fluctuated between a high of about 61.8 and a low of about 61.2 pesos per US dollar — check a live converter before you travel since rates move daily.

**Cash vs. cards:** The Philippines runs on a mix of cash, cards, and crossed fingers — you can pay by card in cities and malls, but step outside the urban grid, or even into a beach shack, and you'll need pesos in your pocket. More specifically, cards are accepted reliably at major hotels, Grab rides, SM/Ayala/Robinsons malls, and tourist-oriented restaurants in BGC, Makati, and Cebu IT Park, but smaller vendors, tricycles, and remote resorts are cash-only.

**ATMs:** ATMs are everywhere in cities but can be flaky or run dry in smaller towns and islands — stick to ATMs from big banks like BDO, BPI, or Metrobank, as they're more reliable and have better security. Withdrawal limits and fees vary: some machines limit withdrawals to ₱10,000–₱20,000 per transaction (about $175–350), and fees vary by bank. Withdraw cash in cities before heading to remote islands, since ATMs are widely available in populated areas but scarce on remote islands, so it's wise to withdraw cash before island-hopping.

**Digital wallets:** GCash is the Philippines' dominant digital wallet — used for everything from Grab payments to sari-sari store purchases to utility bills. Full verification generally requires a local SIM and ID/bank account, so for most tourists on a standard trip, GCash is not necessary — Grab, major restaurants, and malls all accept cards or cash.

**Tipping:** Tipping is not mandatory but highly appreciated; a 10% service charge is often included in restaurant bills, and if not, a 10% tip is a good guide — it's polite to tip hotel staff, guides, and drivers.

## Best time to visit

The Philippines has two broad seasons. The dry season (November–May) is generally safer for travel. Within that window, peak season runs from December to February when the weather is cool and dry (around 26–30°C/79–86°F), followed by a hot dry season from March to May that's perfect for beach trips.

Avoid — or at least plan flexibly around — the wetter half of the year. Typhoon season runs from June to November, with August to October being the peak; the Philippines gets about 20 typhoons per year, but most don't affect tourist areas, so check weather forecasts, plan flexibly, and get travel insurance. During this period, the rainy season brings frequent afternoon showers and a higher risk of typhoons, particularly from August to October, and travel can be disrupted by ferry cancellations, though hotel rates are significantly lower.

**Practical tip:** Given you're reading this in August 2026, you're in the heart of typhoon season — build buffer days into inter-island itineraries and monitor PAGASA weather advisories.

## Emergencies & safety

The Philippines uses 911 as its single national emergency number, covering police, fire, and medical assistance. This system was administered by the Emergency 911 National Office and launched in 2016 to replace the previous 117 emergency hotline, connecting callers to police, fire, and medical emergency services. It has since been consolidated further: in 2025 the government rolled out a "Unified 911," a centralised emergency hotline that brings together local emergency contact numbers from across the country and integrates police, fire, and hospital emergency services into a single platform. The service is free, available 24/7, and designed to be language-sensitive so calls in Tagalog, Cebuano, Ilocano, Waray, Tausug, and other Philippine languages can be understood and acted upon.

Useful supplementary numbers include the **Philippine Red Cross at 143** (ambulance, blood, disaster rescue) and, in Metro Manila, the **MMDA hotline at 136** for traffic and flood-related roadside issues.

**Health note:** Emergency treatment is available at public hospitals but is not universally free — foreign nationals and uninsured visitors should generally expect to cover costs themselves, and private hospitals typically require a deposit or confirmation of insurance coverage before admitting patients, so robust travel/health insurance is strongly advised.

**General safety tips:** Don't drink tap water — it is not recommended to drink tap water in the Philippines — stick to bottled or filtered water. Keep photocopies (digital and paper) of your passport and visa stamp, avoid displaying valuables in crowded transport hubs, and register your trip with your embassy if staying long-term. Given typhoon exposure, download a weather-alert app and keep a charged power bank during rainy season.
