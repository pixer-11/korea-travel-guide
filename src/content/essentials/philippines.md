**Quick answer:** Citizens of 157 countries, including the US, UK, EU, Canada and Australia, get 30 days visa-free entry to the Philippines, China and India are on a separate 14-day track, everyone must complete the free eTravel registration, and 911 is now the single nationwide emergency number; the dry season from December to May remains the best time to visit, while June to November brings monsoon rains and typhoons.

## Visa & entry

The Philippines keeps its long-standing open-door policy: the Department of Foreign Affairs has published a list of 157 countries whose nationals can enter without a visa for 30 days, a group that still covers the US, UK, EU, Canada, Australia and most of ASEAN. These visa-free stays can be extended at the Bureau of Immigration, with the initial 30-day visa-free entry extendable repeatedly, all the way up to a cumulative 36 months for most non-visa-required nationals.

China is the big 2026 change. Effective 16 January 2026, Chinese nationals may enter the Philippines without the requirement of obtaining a visa for a stay of up to fourteen (14) days, and the privilege allows for a non-extendable 14-day stay, which is non-convertible to any other type of Philippine visa category. Unlike the general visa-free list, this one is restricted: the visa-free privilege is valid for entry only through Ninoy Aquino International Airport (NAIA) in Metro Manila and Mactan-Cebu International Airport (MCIA) in Cebu, and travelers must show a passport valid for at least six months beyond the contemplated stay, confirmed hotel accommodation/booking, and a return or onward ticket. The arrangement is temporary: it will run for about a year before being reviewed, so check it is still active before you fly.

India also has its own two-tier rule: the Philippines introduced a two-tier visa-free scheme for Indians from 8 June 2025, offering a non-extendable 14-day visa-free stay to all Indians, and a 30-day visa-free stay to Indians holding valid visas or permanent residence from Australia, Canada, Japan, Schengen countries, Singapore, the US or UK.

Whatever your passport, bring a passport valid well beyond your trip and expect to be asked for an onward ticket at check-in. Every traveler, regardless of nationality, must also complete the free eTravel declaration. You may only register within 72 hours prior to your arrival or departure in the Philippines, and you must then present your eTravel QR code before flight boarding. Registration is entirely free; only use etravel.gov.ph, since only websites ending in .gov.ph should be trusted, as look-alike sites have charged travelers PHP 1,000 to PHP 5,000 for the same free form.

Because eligibility shifts without much warning, **always verify your specific requirements on the official Bureau of Immigration, DFA, and eTravel/e-Visa sites shortly before you fly.**

## Getting around

Metro Manila's rail network, LRT-1, LRT-2 and MRT-3, runs on the shared beep contactless card. A new card currently costs around ₱30, and you'll need a minimum top-up on top of that; it costs ₱30 plus a ₱14 minimum load and works on LRT-1, LRT-2 and MRT-3. Beep fares vary by distance and line: LRT-1 beep fares run ₱16 to ₱52, with single journey tickets ₱20 to ₱55, while MRT-3 and LRT-2 trips are generally cheaper over shorter hops. Students, seniors and PWDs now travel at a discount: students get 50% off from June 20, 2025, and senior citizens and PWDs from July 16, 2025, on all rail lines.

Beep does not cover Grab, taxis, tricycles or traditional jeepneys, so carry cash or a mobile wallet for those. Ride-hailing apps are the easiest way to get around cities, while jeepneys and tricycles remain the cheapest options for short hops, provided you know the route and have small change ready. No NAIA terminal has a direct rail connection, so for flights it's safest to book a car and allow generous time for Manila traffic. Given the archipelago geography, inter-island travel is typically by domestic flight or ferry, so build in buffer days, especially during the rainy season when sailings and flights can be delayed or cancelled.

## Money & costs

The currency is the Philippine peso (PHP). Cards and e-wallets work well in malls, hotels and city restaurants, but cash is still essential once you leave the urban core, including at beach towns, markets, tricycles and small eateries. ATMs are plentiful in cities but can run dry or go offline in smaller towns and outer islands, so carry a backup card and withdraw cash before heading somewhere remote.

Everyday costs remain budget-friendly: street food and simple meals can run well under a few hundred pesos, and city train fares are modest, generally in the ₱13 to ₱35 range per trip depending on distance and line. Accommodation swings widely with season and location, with resort hubs such as Boracay or El Nido commanding a premium over inland or off-peak destinations.

## Best time to visit

The Philippines has two broad seasons: a dry season running roughly December to May, and a wet season from June to November. Conditions shift within that pattern too, with a hot, dry stretch from March to May, a southwest monsoon and typhoon-heavy period from June through October, and a cooler, drier northeast monsoon from November into February.

For the calmest seas and best island-hopping conditions, aim for December through May; January and February bring cooler temperatures that suit hiking or volcano visits in the north. Typhoon risk peaks later in the wet season, broadly June through September, mainly affecting the eastern seaboard, Luzon, Samar, Leyte and parts of Mindanao. Shoulder months reward flexible travelers: late May into June offers tail-end dry weather before the rains settle in, while the back half of the wet season, September through November, tends to bring thinner crowds and lower prices as conditions gradually improve. Whichever month you pick, monitor forecasts once you land, since typhoons can disrupt even a well-timed trip.

## Emergencies & safety

The Philippines now runs on one emergency number. The unified 911 emergency hotline replaces over 30 local emergency numbers nationwide, launched on Thursday, September 11, 2025, available in all regions across the country. The service is built for accessibility: it is free of charge, available nationwide 24/7, and capable of handling calls in major Philippine languages, including Tagalog, Cebuano, Ilocano, Waray and Tausug, and English is widely understood by operators as well.

For maritime emergencies, calls route through 911, with the Philippine Coast Guard handling search and rescue at sea, also reachable on VHF Channel 16. Other still-useful direct contacts include the Philippine Red Cross hotline at 143 for blood services, disaster response and water rescue, and PAGASA's website for storm signal levels and rainfall advisories.

Overall, the Philippines is a reassuring destination for tourists, though resourcing and response times vary once you're outside major cities. It's worth noting the location of the nearest barangay hall and local police post when you arrive somewhere new, since these can respond faster than a national line in a fast-moving situation. During storm season, follow PAGASA's official storm signal guidance closely, especially before any ferry or small-boat trip, and don't assume every three-digit hotline works the same way nationwide, since some local variations persist outside 911 itself.
