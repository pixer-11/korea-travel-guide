**Quick answer:** Most Western passport holders can enter Taiwan visa-free for up to 90 days by completing the mandatory online Taiwan Arrival Card (TWAC) before landing; get around cheaply with an EasyCard, pay mostly in New Taiwan Dollars (cash still matters), and aim for October–April to dodge the worst of typhoon season — but always double-check your specific nationality's rules on the official BOCA/immigration sites before you fly.

## Visa & entry

Taiwan runs one of Asia's more generous visa-free schemes. Taiwan offers visa exemption for short-term stays to citizens of 170 countries and regions, one of the most generous visa policies in Asia. Most Western passport holders get 90 days visa-free entry to Taiwan, including EU/EEA citizens, UK, US, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, Japan, South Korea, and many others. Some nationalities get shorter windows — for example, Taiwan's visa-exempt entry rules give ordinary passport holders from certain countries up to 14 days, a hard limit that cannot be extended or converted to a visa-based stay. Citizens of countries without visa-free access, or those wanting a longer stay, need to apply for a visa or e-Visa in advance via a Taiwan overseas mission; for nationalities requiring a visa, Taiwan offers an eVisa for many countries, applied online and approved within 3–7 business days, with no physical sticker required.

Regardless of visa category, paperwork has moved online. As of October 1, 2025, paper arrival cards have been officially decommissioned across all international airports and ports in Taiwan, and every foreign national — including those entering under the 90-day, 30-day, or 14-day visa-exemption programs — must complete the digital Taiwan Arrival Card (TWAC) prior to border control. You can submit it shortly before you fly: travelers can complete their submission online within 7 days (including the arrival day) prior to landing in Taiwan, and it's wise to download or screenshot the TWAC completion confirmation page before boarding, so it can be shown offline without waiting for mobile data reconnection. Passports generally need at least six months' validity remaining.

**Bottom line: rules change frequently by nationality and political circumstance** — visa-free windows have recently been extended for some countries and revoked for others (e.g., a facilitation program for Cambodian nationals was removed in mid-2026). Always verify your specific requirements on Taiwan's Bureau of Consular Affairs (BOCA) website and the National Immigration Agency's TWAC portal before booking.

## Getting around

Taiwan's public transport is efficient, and a stored-value smartcard is the single most useful tool for visitors. EasyCard and iPass are the two most common transportation smartcards in Taiwan; functionally, they're nearly the same, though EasyCard is more common in northern cities while iPASS is prevalent in the south. MRTs, city buses, TRA local trains, and YouBikes can all be tapped with one card — the EasyCard handles it all. Cards typically cost around NT$100 and are sold at MRT stations and convenience stores, generally for NT$100, with the exception of limited issue cards with special designs which will be more expensive. Taipei MRT users also get a monthly rebate when they use an EasyCard.

For longer intercity trips, note the limits: EasyCard is not accepted on the High-Speed Rail (HSR) or most long-distance buses — for HSR, you must purchase separate tickets in advance or at the station. Local, non-reserved TRA trains do accept EasyCard tap-in-tap-out just like the MRT. On buses, pay attention to signage: on most Taiwan buses you tap the card both when you board and when you get off, and a helpful mnemonic is that "上車刷卡" means tap when boarding and "下車刷卡" means tap when alighting.

If you're a heavy transit user staying a month, Taiwan also offers TPASS: launched in 2023, TPASS is a monthly unlimited-rides pass for transit, with the Northern TPASS covering Taipei, New Taipei, Keelung, and Taoyuan (including the Airport MRT) for NT$1,200/month. For most short-term tourists, though, TPASS usually isn't worth it, since a NT$1,200 monthly pass requires roughly 60 NT$20 MRT rides in 30 days to break even versus an EasyCard. Short-stay travelers may prefer a dedicated tourist bundle: the Taiwan Pass – Railways Edition includes unlimited train rides for 3 consecutive days, one MRT pass, and a choice of a shuttle bus, forest railway ticket, Uber voucher, or scooter voucher. Taoyuan International Airport connects to Taipei via the Airport MRT, and Taiwan's High Speed Rail links major west-coast cities from Taipei to Kaohsiung in a few hours.

## Money & costs

The local currency is the New Taiwan Dollar (TWD), and while cards are accepted in hotels, department stores, and chain restaurants, many small eateries, street vendors, and transport options are still cash-only. The only accepted currency in Taiwan is the New Taiwan Dollar, with notes in denominations of NT$100, NT$200, NT$500, NT$1000, and NT$2000. Foreign cash, including US dollars, is rarely usable directly: US dollars are not normally accepted by shops, restaurants, taxis or attraction counters — exchange them for New Taiwan Dollars or use an accepted credit card.

ATMs are your easiest source of local cash. ATMs are abundant in Taiwan, especially in 7-Eleven and FamilyMart convenience stores, which are open 24/7, and most machines allow international withdrawals, though foreign card fees vary. Look specifically for machines that accept international networks — ATMs marked "International" or with the Visa Plus/Mastercard Cirrus logos, with most 7-Eleven and post office ATMs qualifying, plus FamilyMart, Hi-Life, and OK convenience store ATMs. Carrying some daily cash is sensible: approximately NT$1,000 to NT$3,000 per person per day is a practical starting amount for small purchases and cash-only expenses. For exchanging bills, note that Chunghwa Post, the national postal service, operates currency exchange at major branches with competitive rates, and Taiwanese banks are notoriously picky about damaged or torn foreign banknotes. Mobile payments (Line Pay, Apple Pay, Google Pay) are growing but less essential for short-term visitors than an EasyCard plus cash.

## Best time to visit

Taiwan's climate is subtropical in the north and tropical in the south, with a pronounced typhoon season to plan around. Winter is generally high season in Taiwan due to holiday events and winter attractions, summer is low season due to typhoons and extreme heat, and spring/autumn are shoulder seasons. Most guides converge on the same recommendation: the autumn months (October to November) and early spring (March to April) are ideal for visiting, with mild temperatures and clear skies.

Typhoon timing is the key planning variable. Typhoon season officially runs from May 1 to November 30, but storm activity ramps up in July, peaks in August and September, tapers through October, and is essentially over by mid-November. December through April is reliably typhoon-free. If you do travel in peak season, don't panic: travel is possible with reasonable precautions, since Taipei is well-prepared for storms, the MRT and most infrastructure stay operational, and modern forecasting gives 3–7 days of warning, with most visitors experiencing no major disruption. When a serious storm does hit, expect a full pause: if the threat is significant, local governments declare a "typhoon day," which functions as an official holiday — schools, offices, and most businesses close, public transport shuts down, and flights are cancelled. Fortunately these are short-lived: most typhoons pass through within 12 to 36 hours, and the city typically returns to normal operations within a day of the storm's departure. Build in a buffer if traveling July–September, and monitor Taiwan's Central Weather Administration for real-time warnings.

## Emergencies & safety

Taiwan uses a split emergency-number system rather than one universal hotline. Taiwan uses two separate emergency numbers — 110 for police and 119 for fire and ambulance — rather than a single catchall number like 112 in the EU or 999 in the UK. Both are staffed with English-capable dispatch in major areas, and most public payphones allow free calls to both 110 and 119. If you lack a working SIM, dialling 112 and pressing 0 will connect you to police, though this route involves a transfer delay compared to dialling 110 directly.

Other useful numbers include the Anti-Fraud hotline (165), the Foreign Affairs Service Station in Taipei at (02) 2556-6007, and the domestic violence and sexual assault helpline at 113. Note that, unlike locals covered by National Health Insurance, travellers and short-stay visitors without NHI coverage are responsible for the full cost of medical care, so anyone not covered is strongly advised to hold a valid travel insurance policy. As always, confirm these numbers against official sources, as they are subject to revision.

Overall, Taiwan is considered a very safe destination for tourists, with low violent crime rates; standard precautions (watch belongings in crowded night markets, follow mountain/hiking safety advisories, and heed typhoon warnings) cover most risks.
