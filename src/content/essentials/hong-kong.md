**Quick answer:** Most visitors get visa-free entry to Hong Kong for 7 to 180 days depending on nationality, moving around is easy and cheap with an Octopus card on the MTR, and the best weather is from late October to December, well outside typhoon season; always double-check the rules on the official Immigration Department site before you fly.

## Visa & entry

Hong Kong offers visa-free entry to citizens of around 170 countries, with stays permitted ranging from 7 to 180 days depending on nationality. For example, citizens of the United States, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, and Singapore can stay for up to 90 days without a visa, while UK citizens can stay for up to 180 days.

A few other essentials apply to everyone: all visitors must hold a passport valid for at least 1 month. You're also required to have adequate funds to cover your stay without working and, unless in transit to Chinese Mainland or Macao, to hold onward or return tickets. Importantly, visitors are not allowed by law to take up employment, establish or join a business, or enter school as a student, and cannot change immigration status after arrival except in unusual circumstances, so if you're coming for work or study you need the correct visa before you travel.

Some nationalities, including Indian citizens, must complete a Pre-Arrival Registration (PAR) even though they can still enter visa-free: this applies to specific nationalities like Indian citizens for visa-free stays of up to 14 days, while Singapore, US, UK, and Australian passport holders do not need pre-arrival registration and can stay visa-free for 90 days. PAR is valid for 6 months from approval, so you can apply up to 6 months in advance.

If you do need a full visa, Hong Kong's Immigration Department now runs everything digitally: electronic services for visa applications let applicants complete the entire process online through the GovHK website, the ImmD's website, or the ImmD Mobile Application, for all visa types including visit, employment, and study.

**Confirm before you travel:** visa rules, exempt-country lists, and PAR requirements change periodically, so verify your specific nationality's requirements on the Immigration Department's official website shortly before booking and again before departure.

## Getting around

Hong Kong's public transport is fast, clean, and easy to navigate, and the Octopus card is the backbone of it. With over 180,000 acceptance points, it lets you tap and go on the MTR, buses, trams, ferries, and at places like 7-Eleven, supermarkets, and cafés. You can buy one on arrival: at Hong Kong International Airport, use the MTR Customer Service Centres in the arrival hall or the platform vending machines, and 7-Eleven stores across the city also sell the tourist version and handle top-ups. For most visitors, the standard On-Loan physical card is best since the deposit is refundable and it works even if your phone dies, though iPhone users who'd rather go card-free can use the digital Octopus through the Octopus App for Tourists. There's no fixed expiry date, so you can keep it between trips and reuse it years later, though it goes dormant after long non-use.

From the airport, the Airport Express train is the quickest link into town, reaching Hong Kong Station in roughly 24 minutes. Once in the city, the MTR subway network, buses, trams (including the iconic double-decker "Ding Ding" trams on Hong Kong Island), and the Star Ferry across Victoria Harbour cover almost everywhere tourists want to go. Taxis are another option: all taxis are metered, you'll pay extra for luggage in the boot and for tunnel tolls, and following recent 2026 mandates all taxis must accept electronic payments. Ride-hailing works too, since Uber is available in Hong Kong and works just as it does elsewhere.

## Money & costs

The local currency is the Hong Kong dollar (HKD). It's pegged to the US dollar at a rate of about 7.80 HKD to 1 USD, which makes it unusually stable and predictable for budgeting.

Card payments are the norm in most places: credit cards such as American Express, Visa, and Mastercard are widely used across hotels, shops, and restaurants, and mobile wallets like Apple Pay and Google Pay are increasingly accepted. Still, for smaller or more traditional establishments, carrying cash is recommended, as they may not accept cards or mobile payments. A useful middle ground is the Octopus card itself, which doubles as an everyday payment tool beyond transport.

If you're bringing cash, note the reporting threshold: travellers can bring up to HK$120,000 (or the equivalent in other currencies) into Hong Kong without any declaration. It's also wise to tell your bank you're travelling, since unusual overseas card activity can otherwise trigger a block. Typical daily costs vary widely, from cheap noodle shops and dim sum to expensive hotels and fine dining, so budget according to your travel style.

## Best time to visit

Hong Kong is a year-round destination, but the seasons differ sharply. The best time to visit is usually from October to December, when the weather is warm but more comfortable, humidity is lower than in summer, and skies are often clearer. This makes it the prime window for harbour views, Victoria Peak, and hiking trails.

Summer is a different story: summer is the hottest and wettest season, with average daily maximum temperatures around 30 to 32°C from June to August and high rainfall. It also coincides with typhoon risk: the official typhoon season runs May to November, with the peak risk window from June through September, and in an average year 3 to 5 typhoons pass close enough to affect Hong Kong. A strong signal can disrupt your plans, since a T8 signal (sustained winds 63–117 km/h) shuts down public transport, ferries, outdoor attractions, and many shops.

Winter is a solid alternative for sightseeing: winter, from late November to January, is considered a great time to visit since there are fewer weather surprises. Whenever you go, keep an eye on the Hong Kong Observatory's official typhoon and rainstorm signals rather than relying on secondhand reports, and be aware that Chinese New Year and mainland "Golden Week" holidays bring crowds and higher hotel prices.

## Emergencies & safety

Hong Kong's emergency system is simple to remember: call 999 for police, fire, or ambulance, or, for those with speech or hearing impairments, send an emergency SMS to 992 (992 requires prior registration). The number 112 also works on mobile phones and will connect you to emergency services, with English-speaking operators typically available.

For non-emergencies, keep the police hotline handy: if you lose your passport, make a "lost report" at the nearest police station by calling the Police Hotline on +852 2527 7177, or check their website for a Report Room. Afterward, contact your consulate to arrange a replacement passport, and call your airline to reschedule your flight if needed, since a new passport can take time to process. If your valuables are lost or stolen, notify your hotel immediately, report the theft to police, and separately notify your card issuers as quickly as possible.

Overall, Hong Kong is considered a very safe city for travellers, with efficient policing and low violent crime rates. The main safety consideration for visitors is weather-related: during typhoon season, follow the Observatory's official signal numbers and avoid outdoor activity, hiking, or swimming when a high signal is hoisted.
