**Quick answer:** Most Western passport holders enter Mexico visa-free for tourism and receive an FMM tourist permit on arrival, the peso (MXN) is the only currency that matters day to day, and the national emergency number is 911; always verify current details on the official INM and SRE sites before flying, since policy and fees change.

## Visa & entry
Mexico's tourist entry document is the Forma Migratoria Múltiple (FMM). The FMM is an immigration document issued by Mexico's Instituto Nacional de Migracion (INM). Citizens of approximately 65 countries can enter Mexico with just the FMM and a valid passport, including nationals of the US, Canada, UK, all EU and EFTA countries, Japan, South Korea, Australia, New Zealand, Singapore, Malaysia, and the UAE. Travellers from India, China, Pakistan, Bangladesh, and roughly 130 other countries need a Mexican visa obtained from a consulate before travel, in addition to the FMM issued at the border.

Upon arrival, visitors are authorized to stay in Mexico for tourist, business or transit purposes for up to 180 days, though the immigration officer decides the exact number of days, so check your stamp or digital record before leaving the counter. Holders of a valid US, Canada, UK, Japan, or Schengen visa can enter Mexico visa-free for up to 180 days, regardless of nationality, and will still receive an FMM.

If you arrive by air, the cost is normally built into the ticket. By land, longer stays trigger a fee; all visitors entering Mexico must obtain a Multiple Immigration Form (FMM) to present at checkpoints within the country, and as of 2026 the FMM has a fee of 983 pesos (roughly 52 USD at late-2026 rates) for stays over seven days; shorter land visits remain fee-exempt.

A few extra wrinkles worth knowing: cruise passengers who only visit Mexican ports during a cruise generally do not need a Mexican visa, and visitors to Quintana Roo (Cancún, Tulum, Playa del Carmen, Cozumel) also have to pay the state tourist tax Visitax before leaving the state, separate from the FMM. Nationals of Russia, Turkey and Ukraine, and as of 5 February 2026, Brazilian nationals travelling by air for tourism, business, or transit, can use the Electronic Authorization System (SAE) instead of a consular visa.

Rules on nationalities, fees and permitted stays shift periodically, so confirm your specific situation directly with INM, the SRE visa portal, or your nearest Mexican consulate rather than relying solely on third-party blogs. A passport valid at least six months beyond your arrival date is standard practice.

## Getting around
Mexico City's public transport remains cheap and extensive. The legendary magnetic paper ticket was bid farewell on March 1, 2024; today, access is fully digitalized through the Integrated Mobility Card (MI), which you can buy for $15.00 MXN at the ticket booths of any Metro station or at automatic vending machines located in Metrobús, Cablebús, and Light Rail stations. A useful 2026 upgrade: it is now possible to pay for access directly at the turnstiles using contactless credit or debit bank cards, as well as digital wallets from your smartphone or smartwatch (Apple Pay, Google Wallet, etc.), so a foreign contactless card can substitute for the MI card on the Metro.

Fares are flat and low: the universal cost per Metro trip is $5.00 MXN, regardless of distance or internal transfers, while senior citizens, people with disabilities and children under 5 travel free. Metrobús costs 6 pesos on every line except Line 4 to the airport, which has a flat fare of 30 pesos, and the Tren Ligero to Xochimilco costs 3 pesos. Metro and Metrobús use the same MI card, but it is not a shared fare: tapping one system's balance does not give a free transfer into the other.

Outside the capital, intercity travel relies heavily on comfortable long-distance buses, domestic flights between major cities, and ride-hailing apps such as Uber and Cabify, which operate widely in cities and resort zones. On toll highways, the free roadside-assistance patrol known as the Green Angels can help if you break down; see Emergencies below.

## Money & costs
The Mexican peso (MXN) is the national currency, shown with the $ symbol. Card acceptance keeps growing in cities and resort areas, with Visa and Mastercard widely taken by hotels, restaurants and retailers; American Express is accepted less consistently. Cash still rules in markets, small shops, taxis and rural areas.

For withdrawals, ATM fees vary enormously by bank. From one operator to the next, for the exact same action, the spread runs from 22 to 150 pesos. Inbursa charges roughly 22 pesos per withdrawal, with HSBC near 80 pesos and BBVA around 150 pesos for the same transaction. Airport kiosks can charge far more, so use a branch-attached bank ATM when possible. Always choose to pay in MXN rather than accepting the on-screen currency conversion offer, since your card will then convert at the better interbank rate.

Exchange rates have moved noticeably through 2026. The rate was $16.86 as of September 4, 2026, but it weakened afterward; the USD/MXN exchange rate fell to 18.7867 on October 30, 2026, with the peso weakening 2.64% over the preceding month. Check a live source like Banxico or XE before budgeting, since the rate has swung between roughly 17 and 18.9 over the year. US dollars are not a reliable substitute for pesos outside heavily touristed strips.

## Best time to visit
The best time to travel to Mexico is during the dry season between November and April, when sunny, dry conditions suit beaches, colonial towns and archaeological sites. This is also peak season, so expect higher prices and bigger crowds, especially around the December and February holidays. September is typically the wettest month and falls in peak hurricane season, with frequent rain on the Gulf, Caribbean and Pacific coasts. For a reasonable compromise of lower prices, thinner crowds, and tolerable afternoon showers, consider May or late October into early November, which also brings Día de los Muertos celebrations around the country.

## Emergencies & safety
911 remains Mexico's national emergency number for police, fire, or medical situations anywhere in the country. For roadside breakdowns or tourist help on federal highways and toll roads, you can reach the Green Angels simply by calling "078"; this connects you to a 24-hour toll-free dispatch and radio service to your nearest patrol, and the Ángeles Verdes are bilingual. If 078 doesn't connect well from your cell phone, dialing 911 can also route you to the same assistance network.

For non-emergency help in the capital, LOCATEL remains Mexico City's information and help line for non-life-threatening situations. Emergency care is not automatically free; uninsured travellers are typically billed, so travel medical insurance is strongly recommended. Keep your embassy's contact details and your hotel address written in Spanish in case you need to describe your location by phone.
