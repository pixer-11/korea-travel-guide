**Quick answer:** Citizens of the US, Canada, the UK, most of the EU, Australia and New Zealand can currently enter South Korea visa-free for 30 to 90 days and, among 67 countries in all, are temporarily exempt from K-ETA through December 31, 2026, but everyone without a K-ETA must still file the free e-Arrival Card online within three days of arrival; bring a T-money card for transport, some won alongside your cards, and aim for spring or autumn for the best weather.

## Visa & entry

South Korea's entry system for visa-waiver travelers now has two separate layers, and mixing them up is the most common mistake. The first is K-ETA (Korea Electronic Travel Authorization), normally a mandatory pre-travel screening. South Korea's K-ETA portal confirmed a one-year extension of the temporary K-ETA exemption, with the Ministry of Justice stating the exemption would be effective until December 31, 2026 (KST). The exemption covers nationals from 67 countries and territories, including the United States, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, the United Kingdom, Singapore, Japan, and most EU member states. The exemption is not permanent: from January 1, 2027, nationals from most of the currently exempt countries will need to obtain a K-ETA before travelling. You may see blogs claiming only 22 countries are exempt; this appears to be a misreading, so treat the official portal, not secondhand lists, as the final word for your passport.

The second, newer layer is the e-Arrival Card. A separate e-Arrival Card is now mandatory for everyone entering Korea from 1 January 2026: every foreign traveller entering Korea, including K-ETA-exempt nationalities, must submit an e-Arrival Card within three days (72 hours) before arrival. It is free, submitted only at e-arrivalcard.go.kr, and replaces the old paper arrival card. The one exception: if you hold a valid K-ETA, you don't need to fill in an arrival card on each trip. So exempt travelers face a choice: skip K-ETA and file a free e-Arrival Card each trip, or apply for K-ETA anyway (there is a non-refundable fee, roughly ₩10,000) to skip the arrival-card step entirely.

Typical stay lengths run 30 to 90 days depending on nationality and reciprocal agreements, with 90 days standard for the US, Canada, the EU, the UK, and Australia. Because exemption lists, fees, and the e-Arrival system can change, **confirm your specific country's rules on the official K-ETA portal (k-eta.go.kr) and the e-Arrival Card portal (e-arrivalcard.go.kr) shortly before you fly.**

## Getting around

Get a T-money card on day one; it works on subways, buses, most taxis, convenience stores, and across other major cities including Busan, Daegu, Incheon, Gwangju, and Jeju. The base fare is about ₩1,550 with a transit card and ₩1,650 for a single-journey ticket, plus a refundable ₩500 deposit on single-journey tickets. The base fare rose to ₩1,550 on 28 June 2025.

If you're sightseeing heavily inside Seoul, consider the Climate Card tourist pass. Short-term passes are available for tourists: 1-day (₩5,000), 3-day (₩10,000), or 5-day (₩15,000), offering unlimited rides on Seoul subways and buses. Note that the 30-day Climate Card has ended; Seoul discontinued the monthly card in September 2026, so only the short tourist passes remain for visitors. The Discover Seoul Pass and WOWPASS (which combines currency exchange with a T-money chip) are useful alternatives.

Card payments are improving but have limits. Foreign cards now work at Seoul subway kiosks at 273 stations and 440 machines, a system live since March 17, 2026. However, you cannot yet tap your home credit card directly at the gate; despite headlines about open-loop payments, Seoul turnstiles in 2026 still accept only transit cards and single tickets, with direct foreign-card tap-to-ride due on city buses first in 2027 and the subway later. So use the foreign-card kiosks to buy a ticket or top up a transit card, but don't expect to tap a bank card straight through the gate. A physical T-money card remains the simplest fallback everywhere, especially in smaller cities where reload machines are often cash-only.

For longer distances, high-speed rail connects Seoul to Busan, Gyeongju, and other major cities in a few hours; the Seoul-Busan KTX fare is now around ₩54,400 following the KTX/SRT merger in September 2026. Taxis are metered and plentiful, and Kakao T works well for English-speaking visitors.

## Money & costs

The currency is the Korean won (KRW), with coins (10, 50, 100, 500 won) and banknotes (1,000, 5,000, 10,000, 50,000 won). Foreign currency is not accepted in most everyday shops, so plan to use won or cards.

South Korea is highly card-friendly: hotels, malls, convenience stores, cafés, airports, and train stations usually take cards without trouble. Cash still matters at traditional markets, street food stalls, small local restaurants, and for transit card top-ups. For ATMs, look for machines marked Global ATM, International ATM, or showing Visa, Mastercard, Plus, Cirrus, JCB, or UnionPay logos, and expect an English-language option. A reasonable plan is to carry roughly ₩100,000-₩200,000 in cash as backup, keep at least two cards, and have a working transit payment method in case one option fails. Mobile wallets like Apple Pay still have patchy acceptance for visiting tourists, so don't rely on them exclusively.

## Best time to visit

South Korea has four distinct seasons, and the shoulder seasons are consistently the most comfortable for sightseeing. The best windows are spring, roughly mid-April to May, and autumn, late September to November; both are mild, dry, and sunny, with cherry blossoms in spring and fiery foliage in autumn. Cherry blossom season typically unfolds from late March to mid-April, peaking in early April, while fall colour generally runs mid-to-late October through early-to-mid November, starting in the northern mountains around Seoraksan in late October and finishing in Busan and Jeju by mid-November. Winter (December-February) suits winter sports; summer (June-August) brings a muggy monsoon and is generally the least recommended season. If you want fewer crowds with still-decent weather, autumn is often a better bet than spring's peak-bloom crowds.

## Emergencies & safety

South Korea splits emergency numbers by service rather than using one universal number:

- **112** – Police, for immediate response; say "경찰이 필요해요" if you need to ask for police help, and English-speaking support is available.
- **119** – Fire and ambulance. Emergency public ambulance transport dispatched via 119 is 100% free of charge nationwide in South Korea.
- **1330** – Korea Travel Hotline, run by the Korea Tourism Organization. It is a free, 24-hour line offering help in Korean, English, Japanese, and Chinese, with some other languages during daytime hours. Both 119 and 112 can connect foreign callers to an interpreter, often through a three-way call with the 1330 tourism interpreters.
- **1339** – KDCA medical information and consultation line; it provides information on hospitals, clinics, medical services, and health advice, but is not a dispatch line, so call 119 for an actual emergency.
- **1345** – Immigration contact centre for visa and residency questions, operating Monday to Friday, 09:00 to 22:00.

All of these numbers are free to call. South Korea remains a very safe destination with low violent crime and excellent medical infrastructure; the main practical risk for visitors is the language barrier, which is exactly what 1330's multilingual interpreters exist to bridge. Save 112, 119, and 1330 in your phone before you land, and keep your embassy's contact details handy for lost passports or legal issues.
