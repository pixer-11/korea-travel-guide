## Visa & entry

Citizens of 50 countries holding ordinary passports can enter mainland China and stay up to 30 days for business, tourism, visits to family and friends, exchanges, or transit, with no visa and no advance application. As of February 17, 2026, ordinary passport holders from the United Kingdom and Canada can enter mainland China and stay up to 30 days with no visa at all, bringing the list to 50 countries, with the whole scheme running through December 31, 2026. The list also includes Australia, New Zealand, Japan, South Korea, and most of the EU.

Important exception: the United States is not on the 30-day visa-free list, so US passport holders still need a visa to fly in for a normal trip. What Americans gained instead is a far more generous transit option — the 240-hour visa-free transit policy, which lets US travelers stay up to 10 days at dozens of ports as long as they are passing through to a third country.

A few practical details:
- The 30-day count starts from 00:00 the day after entry, not from the moment you clear immigration.
- Your passport must be valid for at least six months and have two blank pages, and you must register with the local Public Security Bureau within 24 hours of arrival (hotels usually handle this automatically at check-in).
- Emergency/temporary passports are typically excluded from the visa-free scheme even if your nationality otherwise qualifies — a full-validity ordinary passport is required.
- Since November 20, 2025, foreigners can fill in a digital arrival card in advance via the National Immigration Administration's website, app, or WeChat/Alipay mini-program before traveling, or on arrival by QR code or paper form.

**Because visa-free lists, transit rules, and durations change frequently, always confirm your specific nationality's status on the official National Immigration Administration site or your nearest Chinese embassy/consulate before booking flights.**

## Getting around

China's public transport is extensive, fast, and overwhelmingly cashless. China's high-speed rail network is enormous — over 45,000km of track connecting nearly every major city, with Beijing to Shanghai in about 4.5 hours; for anything under 5–6 hours the train almost always beats flying once airport time is factored in. Note that the official railway booking app, 12306, works fine for Chinese citizens but can be a headache for foreigners, with SMS codes that never arrive, slow identity verification, and a Mandarin-only interface — third-party booking platforms are often easier for visitors.

For city travel, public transportation is fast, clean, affordable, and almost entirely cashless; set up Alipay and WeChat before you arrive and you can tap through metro turnstiles, board buses, and hail rides without a Chinese bank account or phone number. Subway single trips typically range from 2–10 RMB and buses 1–3 RMB, making it the top choice for foreign travelers. In major hubs like Beijing and Shanghai, you can also tap in with a foreign contactless credit card on the metro, though it usually costs slightly more than paying via Alipay.

For taxis and ride-hailing, Didi works directly inside WeChat, so there's no need for a separate download, though Didi rides are typically paid in-app (cashless); if you need a cash option you may need a regular taxi instead. Be cautious of unlicensed operators — watch out for unlicensed "black taxi" drivers approaching at tourist spots and train stations, offering no meter, no insurance, and made-up prices.

## Money & costs

China's currency is the Renminbi (RMB/yuan, ¥). The country runs on mobile payments: the practical answer for the best payment method in 2026 is Alipay or WeChat Pay, backed up by a little cash and a physical card for hotels. As of 2026, foreigners can bind international cards (Visa, Mastercard, JCB, Discover) directly to Alipay and WeChat Pay without needing a Chinese bank account — set this up before you arrive if possible.

Foreign plastic alone won't get you far outside hotels: foreign cards aren't accepted everywhere — Visa/Mastercard are usually fine in high-end hotels and tourist restaurants but far less accepted in small local places, and many small vendors expect mobile payment only, so relying on just a foreign card can be limiting. Keep some cash as backup: carrying around ¥500–1,500 per person is a common cushion for snacks, small souvenirs, tips, and backup when apps fail. Some travelers use a UnionPay "Tour Card," though Tour Card application services are being gradually discontinued and the card carries a 5% top-up service fee, so direct card-binding to Alipay/WeChat is generally the better option in 2026.

## Best time to visit

Spring (March–May) and autumn (September–November) are the best times, with pleasant weather, beautiful natural scenery, and fewer crowds. For most travelers, April, May, September, and October are the best months to visit China, offering mild temperatures, more comfortable sightseeing conditions, and attractive seasonal scenery.

Since you're planning for August specifically: August is a popular time for families to visit China, but expect hot, sticky weather and some crowded viewpoints. If you have flexibility, November to March is China's low season, a great option for saving on your travel budget, with fewer crowds and shorter queues at top attractions. Whenever you go, try to avoid China's National Day holiday (October 1–7), a peak travel period with large crowds and higher prices.

Given China's size, timing also depends on region — the country has five climate zones from north to south, so travelers visiting in winter may want to avoid the far north, where temperatures can drop well below freezing, while southern cities stay mild year-round.

## Emergencies & safety

China does not have a single all-in-one emergency number like 911, 999, or 112 — you call the specific service directly. Key numbers:

- **Police: 110** — for crime, theft, disputes, or anything requiring law enforcement.
- **Ambulance: 120** — the number to call if someone needs urgent medical help.
- **Fire: 119** — for fires, gas explosions, or when people are trapped due to fire or heavy smoke.
- **Traffic accidents: 122** — for road accidents involving vehicles, bicycles, or pedestrians, especially when injuries occur or a police report is needed for insurance.

Be prepared for language barriers: for the main emergency numbers, English support is inconsistent, so practical solutions include having your hotel call on your behalf, using Alipay's SOS function, or having your location and situation written in Chinese ready to read out. If you lose something or are a victim of crime, report to local police (110) first to get a loss report, then contact your embassy.

For healthcare: public hospitals treat foreigners — bring your passport, register at the front window first, and pay as you go at each step; big cities also have international clinics with English-speaking doctors. Public hospitals generally expect payment upfront, so keep every receipt to claim from your insurer afterward. Overall, China is a very safe destination for tourists, but as always, keep copies of your passport and travel insurance details, and register your trip with your embassy if it offers that service.
