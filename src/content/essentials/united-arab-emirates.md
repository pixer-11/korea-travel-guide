**Quick answer:** Most visitors from Western countries, the GCC, and dozens of other nations can enter the UAE visa-free or with a visa on arrival for 30 to 90 days, pay easily by card everywhere (dirham, AED, pegged to the US dollar), and get around via a modern metro and taxi network; the most comfortable time to visit is November to March, and the emergency numbers to save are 999 (police), 998 (ambulance), and 997 (fire).

## Visa & entry

The UAE has one of the most open entry regimes in the Gulf region. As of 2026, citizens of over 70 countries can enter the UAE without arranging a visa in advance. The terms vary by nationality, with some countries enjoying 90-day visa-free stays while others receive 30-day visa-on-arrival entry. Citizens of all EU member states receive 90-day visa-free entry to the UAE, allowing a stay of up to 90 days within any 180-day period. No visa fee is charged, and no advance application is needed; EU citizens simply present their passport at the border.

GCC nationals can enter the country without a visa and can enter with either their passport or national ID, and travel is essentially unrestricted between member states.

Some other nationalities, including several in Asia and Africa, qualify for a conditional visa on arrival rather than full visa-free entry. This route typically depends on the traveller already holding a valid visa or residence permit from an approved country. Visa on arrival for 14 or 60 days, with a qualifying residence permit added in June 2026, has been extended to nationals including Indonesia, Vietnam, Thailand, the Philippines, Kenya and South Africa, while India qualifies for 14 days with a valid qualifying visa. To qualify for these on-arrival categories, travellers must hold a valid residency permit from an approved country, such as the US, UK, EU, Canada, or Japan.

For anyone who doesn't fall into a visa-free or visa-on-arrival category, a standard e-visa (14, 30, or 60 days, single or multiple entry) can be applied for online in advance through an approved agent or airline, or directly via the immigration authorities. A valid passport is required, and it is standard practice to make sure it is valid for at least 6 months from your travel date.

Because rules on nationality lists, stay lengths and fees are updated frequently, always confirm your specific category before booking. Verify your eligibility with the UAE Federal Authority for Identity, Citizenship, Customs and Ports Security (ICP) before travelling, or with Dubai's General Directorate of Residency and Foreigners Affairs (GDRFA) if you're flying into Dubai specifically, since GDRFA is the Dubai government authority responsible for managing residency, entry permits, visas and related immigration services in the emirate, while ICP covers the rest of the country.

## Getting around

Dubai and Abu Dhabi both have efficient, affordable public transport, though Dubai's network is the most extensive for visitors. The centrepiece is the Dubai Metro, the world's longest fully automated, driverless metro network, serving 53 stations across the Red and Green lines, connecting the emirate's key districts, airports, and major attractions with modern, air-conditioned trains.

To ride it, you need a Nol card, the RTA's rechargeable smart card. You must purchase a Nol Card from the ticket offices or vending machines available at all stations, since cash is not accepted at the gates. There are different types of Nol cards: the Red Ticket (paper, best for single or few trips), the Silver Card (ideal for residents and regular tourists), and the Gold Card (which gives access to the premium Gold Class cabin). A Silver or Gold card costs around AED 25 including starting credit. Fares are zone-based: fares start at 3 AED within a single zone, 5 AED for two zones, and 7.5 AED for crossing three or more zones. The same card also works on the tram, buses and water buses, and for paid parking provided by the Roads and Transport Authority (RTA).

For trip planning in real time, download the RTA "S'hail" app for live route planning, balance checking, and metro timings. Taxis are plentiful, metered, and generally accept cards as well as cash; ride-hailing apps also operate widely. Abu Dhabi relies more on buses and taxis, with its own metro bus network expanding. Renting a car is straightforward with an international licence if you want to explore beyond the cities, such as the desert or other emirates, though driving standards and speeds can be aggressive, so exercise caution.

## Money & costs

The official currency is the Emirati dirham (AED), pegged to the US dollar at US$1 = Dhs 3.6725, a rate that has held since 1997. The most important thing to know about the UAE dirham is its stability: since November 1997, the Central Bank of the UAE has held the dirham at a fixed exchange rate of 3.6725 to the US dollar. This means US dollar travellers can budget with unusual certainty, while other currencies will move slightly day to day because they float against the dollar, not the dirham directly.

Card payments dominate daily spending. The UAE is highly cashless compared with many international destinations; credit and debit cards are widely accepted, contactless payments are common, and mobile wallets are increasingly convenient. Even so, it's wise to carry some cash: traditional souks, some independent taxis, smaller neighbourhood shops and street-food vendors may prefer or only accept cash. A useful rule when paying by card or withdrawing from an ATM is to always choose to be charged in dirhams rather than your home currency, since dynamic currency conversion offered at the terminal is usually a worse rate.

Costs vary sharply by season. Dubai's pricing is seasonal, with costs rising sharply during the winter peak months of November to March, when the weather is ideal and major events draw large crowds. Conversely, summer offers better value: hotels can be 40 to 50 percent cheaper between June and September compared to peak season rates. If bringing cash into the country, be aware of declaration thresholds; travellers should always confirm current customs limits with UAE customs before arrival, as these can be revised.

## Best time to visit

The best time to visit Dubai and the wider UAE is during the winter months, specifically November through March, when daytime temperatures range from roughly 20 to 25°C (68 to 77°F) with low humidity and clear skies. This is peak season for outdoor activities. It's the peak season for shopping festivals, art fairs and outdoor concerts, and the desert is cool enough for comfortable evening and morning safaris. Expect higher hotel prices and bigger crowds during this window, especially around winter holidays.

May, September and October form a shoulder season: the days are hot and the nights are balmy, but with winter crowds gone these months are good for quieter beach holidays, with sea temperatures averaging around 28°C, and it's still possible to camp in the desert.

Summer (June to August) is the low season, and genuinely hot. June to August heat can exceed 45°C, and humidity makes it feel more intense outdoors. This period suits budget travellers who don't mind spending most of the day indoors, since travellers on a budget can more easily book into Dubai's five-star accommodations thanks to steep discounts, with life moving indoors and summer sales luring shoppers into the air-conditioned malls.

One more seasonal note for planning: Ramadan is a lunar-calendar observance that shifts earlier each year, and during it, restaurant hours, alcohol service and public behaviour norms change somewhat during daylight hours, though the country remains fully open to tourists. Confirm the current Ramadan dates before you travel, since they move by about eleven days annually.

## Emergencies & safety

The UAE is considered very safe for travellers, with a low violent-crime rate and efficient emergency services. Save these numbers before you arrive: in case of an emergency, you can call the following numbers while anywhere in the UAE: 999 for Police, 998 for Ambulance, 997 for Civil Defence (fire). These lines are toll-free and countrywide. These numbers are toll-free and can be dialled from any mobile or landline.

If you're involved in a traffic incident, do not attempt to resolve it privately. Call the police on 999 from anywhere in the UAE, inform them about the accident, mention your location and wait for their instructions, since no traffic incident in the UAE can go unreported. For a fire or building evacuation, call civil defence on 997, evacuate the premises immediately; all public buildings are marked with fire exits, and you should walk towards the exit calmly without rushing, and not stay behind to collect belongings.

A general international backup also works locally: in the UAE, 112 serves as an alternate emergency number to 999, making it a crucial backup in any situation. Additional services exist for non-emergencies and specific concerns, including coast guard, search and rescue, utility faults, and helplines for women, children and mental health support, several of which are listed on the Ministry of Interior's own site.

Standard travel-safety precautions apply: keep copies of your passport and visa, respect local laws around alcohol (generally confined to licensed venues), dress, and public behaviour, and note that penalties for drug possession, including trace amounts, are strict. Tourist police units in Dubai and Abu Dhabi can also assist visitors with reporting theft, scams or losses.
