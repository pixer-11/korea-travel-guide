**Quick answer:** Most Western travelers can enter Vietnam visa-free for up to 45 days or apply for a 90-day e-visa online in advance, pay in Vietnamese Dong (cash still rules outside big cities), and enjoy relatively low costs, easy Grab-based transport, and low overall crime risk — but always double-check current entry rules on the official portals before you fly, since Vietnam's visa policy changes frequently.

## Visa & entry

Vietnam's entry system splits into three tracks: unilateral visa-free access for specific nationalities, the government e-visa, and traditional consulate visas. Citizens of Germany, France, Italy, Spain, the United Kingdom, Russia, Japan, South Korea, Denmark, Sweden, Norway, Finland, Belarus, and roughly 12 additional European countries enjoy 45-day visa-free entry under Resolution 44/NQ-CP, effective through March 2028. For everyone else — including the US, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, China, and India — the standard route is the e-visa. Citizens of 38 countries travel to Vietnam without a visa for either 30 or 45 days, while for all other nationalities, a Vietnam e-visa covers up to 90 days and is available through the official online portal. The e-visa can be applied fully online for US$25 with a stay of up to 90 days, issued as single-entry (US$25) or multiple-entry (US$50).

A few practical points worth noting: Vietnam does not allow e-visa extensions or renewals from within the country — if you need to stay longer, you must exit Vietnam and apply for a new e-visa from outside, though re-entry is permitted immediately. Almost every guide agrees on one baseline rule: you must always have at least six months of passport validity remaining from your arrival date, strictly enforced regardless of visa status. There's also a special case for the island of Phu Quoc — Phu Quoc has its own 30-day visa exemption for all nationalities.

New for 2026: some sources report that starting mid-2026, arrivals into Ho Chi Minh City's Tan Son Nhat Airport must complete a **digital arrival card** before landing, and a separate **online health declaration** requirement was also flagged for mid-2026. These kinds of procedural add-ons change quickly and vary by source reliability — **always verify current requirements directly on the official Vietnam e-visa portal and immigration website before booking flights**, not on third-party blogs.

## Getting around

Vietnam's cities are increasingly easy to navigate without a car. Vietnam offers five primary public transport categories: metro systems, city buses, ride-hailing apps (Grab, Be, Xanh SM), intercity trains, and metered taxis, with metro systems now operating in Hanoi (Line 2A) and Ho Chi Minh City (Line 1, 14 stations). Ho Chi Minh City's metro Line 1 (Ben Thanh–Suoi Tien) began service in December 2024, and it is a 19.7-kilometer rapid transit line with 14 stations, operated with Hitachi trains.

For most tourists, ride-hailing apps are the default choice. Most travelers find Grab so affordable and convenient that city buses aren't worth the hassle unless staying long-term. Practically, Grab requires a local SIM or a foreign number, so it's worth registering before you arrive if possible, and SIM cards cost USD 3–8 at airports and convenience stores. A useful free tool for bus navigation is BusMap: a free GPS-enabled navigation app providing live bus locations, route maps, and arrival times in English for Hanoi, Ho Chi Minh City, Da Nang, Binh Duong, and Can Tho, which has exceeded 44 million downloads.

For long-distance travel, the 1,726-kilometer Reunification Express train connects Hanoi to Ho Chi Minh City, and domestic flights remain the fastest option between major hubs, taking roughly 2 hours versus 30+ hours by sleeper train or bus. If you rent a motorbike, note that legally you need an International Driving Permit (IDP) with motorcycle endorsement plus a valid home-country motorcycle license, and most guides recommend sticking with Grab unless you're an experienced rider comfortable with local traffic.

## Money & costs

The official currency is the Vietnamese Dong (VND), and while cards are increasingly accepted, cash remains important. Cash is still widely used for everyday spending, especially in smaller restaurants and local transport, while cards are accepted in hotels, malls, and larger restaurants but may not work in smaller shops. Cash is king — small cafés, taxis, and markets often don't take cards.

ATMs are widespread and reliable. ATMs are everywhere in Vietnam's cities and work reliably with international debit cards on Plus, Cirrus, and Visa networks; most local machines cap withdrawals between 2,000,000 and 5,000,000 VND per transaction (roughly $80–$200), while HSBC and Citibank machines allow up to 10,000,000 VND with lower fees, and Vietcombank, BIDV, and Techcombank offer the widest coverage in smaller towns. Expect a small fee each time: Vietnamese ATMs typically charge a withdrawal fee of 22,000 to 55,000 VND per transaction (roughly $0.85–$2.15), on top of any fee your home bank charges. Always decline "conversion to home currency" at the machine — when an ATM offers Dynamic Currency Conversion, always decline and choose VND instead, since the ATM's conversion rate is almost always worse than your own bank's.

Daily budgets vary widely by travel style. A realistic daily budget for backpacking is 800,000–1,500,000 VND ($30–$57) covering hostels, street food, and local transport; mid-range travel runs 2,000,000–4,000,000 VND ($76–$152); and comfort/luxury travel is 5,000,000+ VND ($190+). Note also that Vietnamese customs regulations require you to declare amounts exceeding USD 5,000 (or equivalent) and VND 15,000,000 upon arrival. Since Dong is a closed currency, VND is nearly impossible to exchange outside Vietnam, so spend or reconvert leftover cash before departure.

## Best time to visit

Vietnam's elongated shape means "best time" really depends on which regions you're visiting. In the North (Hanoi, Sapa), the ideal window is October to April when weather is cooler and less humid; Central Vietnam (Hue, Da Nang, Hoi An) is best from February to May with moderate rainfall and warm temperatures; and the South (Ho Chi Minh City, Mekong Delta) is favorable from December to April, avoiding the monsoon.

If you're doing a single multi-region trip, aim for the shoulder seasons. March and April offer the best balance across all three regions — the north is warm and dry, the center is sunny, and the south is hot but not yet in peak rainy season — with October and November as the second-best option. Be cautious about typhoon season on the central coast: September and October are the worst months for central Vietnam, as typhoon season peaks then, Hoi An and Hue frequently flood, and Da Nang airport can close during severe storms.

For winter travel, remember the North gets genuinely cold: the north of Vietnam experiences cold dry winters from December to February, where overnight temperatures can dip to 4°C. Meanwhile, southern Vietnam's climate is simpler: Southern Vietnam has only two seasons, dry and wet, with the dry season running from December to April.

## Emergencies & safety

Vietnam doesn't have a single unified emergency number like 911 — instead, memorize three separate lines. Vietnam operates three official emergency hotlines accessible 24/7 nationwide: 113 for police, 114 for fire emergencies, and 115 for ambulance services. There is also a newer nationwide backup line: hotline 112 connects with existing emergency services (113, 114, 115) as well as the Vietnam Maritime Communication Station System and civil defense networks, based at Military Commands of provinces and major cities.

Language is the biggest limitation. While Vietnam's official services 113, 114, and 115 operate 24/7 nationwide, they function exclusively in Vietnamese language, according to UK Foreign Office travel advice. In Ho Chi Minh City there is a bilingual private alternative: the *9999 service provides bilingual English-Vietnamese emergency response in Ho Chi Minh City with significantly faster response times than traditional services. In any emergency, it's often fastest and easiest to save your hotel's reception number, since hotel staff can offer quick help, communicate with local authorities on your behalf, or arrange transport to the nearest hospital.

Overall, Vietnam is considered a safe destination for tourists, with petty theft (bag-snatching, pickpocketing in crowded markets) being the main risk rather than violent crime — carry only small cash amounts day-to-day, use hotel safes, and keep photocopies of your passport. Comprehensive travel insurance covering medical evacuation is strongly recommended given variable rural healthcare access, and English-speaking staff are more reliably found in major-city hospitals than in remote areas.
