**Quick answer:** Most nationalities can enter Vietnam either visa-free for up to 45 days (a shortlist of countries) or via the official e-visa (US$25, up to 90 days, multiple entry) for everyone else; getting around mixes low-cost Grab rides, expanding city metros, and domestic flights, while cash (Vietnamese dong) still rules alongside widely accepted Visa/Mastercard.

## Visa & entry

Vietnam grants e-visas for citizens of all countries and territories with 90-day stay duration and valid for multiple entry. The e-visa is the standard route for nationalities such as the United States, Canada, Australia, New Zealand and Ireland, which are not on Vietnam's visa exemption list, so citizens of these countries need a visa for any tourist visit. The application is straightforward: it's fully online, costs US$25, and allows a stay of up to 90 days.

Separately, a shortlist of countries enjoys unilateral visa exemption. Citizens of 13 countries enjoying unilateral visa exemption can stay in Vietnam up to 45 days, a policy that took effect from 15 August 2023 and extended stay duration from 15 days to 45 days, regardless of passport type and entry purpose. Those original 13 include Germany, France, Italy, Spain, the UK, Russia, Japan, Republic of Korea, Denmark, Sweden, Norway, Finland and Belarus. In August 2025 the government added another batch: Resolution 229 granted visa exemption for citizens of 12 more countries, including Belgium, Bulgaria, Croatia, the Czech Republic, Switzerland, Luxembourg, Hungary, the Netherlands, Poland, Romania, Slovakia and Slovenia, entering for tourism purposes and granted a temporary stay of 45 days, effective from 15 August 2025 until 14 August 2028.

A few important caveats: British National Overseas (BNO) passport holders are not eligible for the 15-day free visa exemption and must obtain a valid visa to visit Vietnam. Neighbouring nationalities get different terms too, for example Mongolian citizens may enter Vietnam without a visa and stay for up to 30 days, effective from March 7, 2024. Whichever route you use, your passport must be valid for at least 6 months from your date of entry into Vietnam, and you need at least two blank pages, free of stamps or visas from other countries. Because exemptions are for "temporary" stays, immigration officers and airlines need proof that you intend to leave, so you may be asked to show a confirmed return ticket or a flight to a third country departing within your exemption period.

Vietnam has also begun digitising arrivals: travellers arriving by air should be aware of a new Pre-Arrival Information Declaration, introduced during 2026, which can be completed within three days before arrival or upon arrival, and completing it before your flight may help reduce waiting time at immigration.

**Rules change with little notice**, and unofficial sites often overcharge: dozens of unofficial websites copy the government portal's design and charge $50–$100 for e-visas that cost only $25 on the official site, so always look for the .gov.vn domain. Always confirm your nationality's current status, exact permitted stay, and any conditions directly on the official immigration portal before booking flights.

## Getting around

Vietnam's cities are dense and traffic-heavy, so app-based ride-hailing is the default for most visitors. Grab has operated in Vietnam since 2014 and remains the most straightforward option for international visitors, accepting foreign Visa and Mastercard credit cards with upfront fares displayed before confirmation. In Hanoi, a GrabCar from Noi Bai International Airport to Hoan Kiem district, roughly 25 km, runs approximately 300,000–375,000 VND ($12–$15 USD), with a small airport surcharge shown clearly in the app. A cheaper alternative is the public bus 86, at USD 0.50, about 1 hour to the city, though it is excellent value but infrequent.

Urban rail is expanding but still limited. Ho Chi Minh City's first metro line opened on 22 December 2024, running from Ben Thanh market to Suoi Tien park across 14 stations over 19.7 km, with a ticket costing 7,000–20,000 VND (about $0.30–0.80) and trains running 5:00–22:00 every 8–12 minutes. Hanoi's network is smaller but growing; overall, Hanoi has two lines, while Ho Chi Minh City's Line 1 opened in December 2024, covering a 19.7 km route from Ben Thanh market to the suburb of Suoi Tien, serving 14 stations. Big expansion plans are underway: authorities in Vietnam's two principal cities have promised to add 600 km of metro in Hanoi by 2035 and 187 km in Ho Chi Minh City by 2030, though timelines have historically slipped, so treat "opening soon" claims cautiously.

For longer distances, Vietnam offers flights, trains and buses. A domestic flight is available from about 500,000 VND (roughly $20) for a 50-minute hop over distances such as the 430 km between major hubs, and if your time is worth more than the money, flying makes sense. The railway is scenic and practical: the railway connects major cities like Hanoi and Ho Chi Minh City, with stops in Hue, Da Nang, and Nha Trang, offering beautiful coastal views and countryside landscapes. The full-length journey is an experience in itself: the Reunification Express from Ho Chi Minh City to Hanoi is a cult ride along the entire coast, 33 hours over 1,726 km, with an air-conditioned sleeper berth from about 1,200,000 VND (roughly $48). Overnight sleeper buses are the budget option between many cities.

Within towns, walking, cyclos and motorbike taxis (xe ôm) fill the gaps: motorbike taxis are one of the fastest ways of getting around Vietnam, perfect for short trips in busy cities, with drivers skilled at navigating tight streets. Note that Grab rides during morning and evening rush hours cost 30–50% more due to surge pricing, so shifting departure times can save money.

## Money & costs

The official currency is the Vietnamese Dong, abbreviated as VND and symbolized by the ₫ sign, the sole official currency of Vietnam. Exchange rates have hovered around 26,300 VND to 1 USD in 2026, with denominations reaching 500,000. Be careful with notes: the largest note is ₫500,000 (about USD 20), and travelers should be careful not to confuse it with the ₫20,000 note, which is a similar color.

Cash is still essential for everyday spending. Pho, banh mi, local coffee, and small restaurants often only accept cash, and markets such as Ben Thanh Market and Han Market usually operate on a cash basis. That said, plastic works well in tourist-facing venues: Visa and Mastercard are accepted at hotels, upscale restaurants, shopping malls, and most travel agencies in major cities like Hanoi, Ho Chi Minh City, Da Nang, and Nha Trang, and contactless tap-to-pay is increasingly supported, with mobile wallets like Apple Pay and Google Pay working at many modern establishments.** Expect some surcharges though, since <cite index="24-4,24-5">debit and credit cards are widely accepted in hotels or shopping malls but probably not suitable for roadside stalls, and you may be charged a 3%-4% commission if paying with plastic.**

ATMs are plentiful but capped and fee-laden. <cite index="25-2,25-3">ATMs generally limit withdrawals to ₫2–5 million (about USD 80–200) per transaction, so plan for fees. A useful tip when using them: when an ATM asks "Would you like to be charged in your home currency?", always decline, since this is Dynamic Currency Conversion and accepting it typically costs 3-5% in hidden markup. Some banks are friendlier to foreign cards than others; TPBank or VPBank are often cited as best for international cards, frequently fee-free with higher withdrawal limits.

For budgeting, realistic 2026 daily figures look like this: backpacking Vietnam runs about 800,000–1,500,000 ₫/day ($30–$57) covering hostels, street food and local transport; mid-range travel runs 2,000,000–4,000,000 ₫/day ($76–$152) for 3-star hotels and a mix of dining and guided tours; and comfort/luxury travel runs 5,000,000+ ₫/day ($190+) for 4-5 star hotels, private tours and fine dining. Tipping is optional but appreciated: tipping is not required, but it is appreciated in spas, hotels, private tours, and transportation services.

## Best time to visit

Vietnam's long, narrow shape means weather varies dramatically by region, so "best time" depends on your route. The north (Hanoi, Ha Long Bay, Sapa) has a distinct cool, dry season roughly October to April and a hot, humid, rainier summer; the central coast (Hue, Da Nang, Hoi An) is prone to typhoons and heavy rain from around September to December, with the driest, sunniest window generally February to May; the south (Ho Chi Minh City, Mekong Delta) runs on a simpler wet season (May–October) and dry season (November–April) pattern. Many travellers aim for **December to April**, when the north is cool and dry, the centre has largely cleared its typhoon season, and the south is in its dry stretch, though this is also peak tourist season with higher prices around Tet (Lunar New Year), so book accommodation and domestic flights early if travelling then.

## Emergencies & safety

Vietnam does not use a single number like 911. Instead: Vietnam does not have a single universal number like 911; instead, use 113 (police), 114 (fire), or 115 (ambulance) depending on the situation. These lines are free and nationwide: emergency contacts in Vietnam are 113 for police, 114 for fire, 115 for medical emergencies, and 112 for search and rescue, and these hotlines are available 24/7 nationwide.

Language is the main obstacle for foreign visitors. Vietnam's official services 113, 114, and 115 operate 24/7 nationwide, but they function exclusively in Vietnamese language, according to UK Foreign Office travel advice. In Ho Chi Minh City there is a bilingual alternative: the *9999 service provides bilingual English-Vietnamese emergency response in Ho Chi Minh City with significantly faster response times than traditional services. Given the barrier, it helps to have backup: carrying a note with essential details or using translation apps can be beneficial, and police officers are generally responsive and trained to assist foreigners, though patience and calmness are key.

For serious injuries or illness, calling an ambulance is best, but for minor issues speed can matter more than protocol: for serious emergencies call 115; for minor issues, taking a taxi or ride-hailing car to a reputable hospital may be quicker. Save your embassy's contact details too, since consular staff can assist with serious incidents, lost passports or legal trouble, and note that response quality varies by area: in rural or remote areas response times might be longer due to logistical challenges, while in urban centers such as Hanoi, Ho Chi Minh City, or Da Nang, the police response is typically quicker.

One practical technical point: to make an emergency call in Vietnam, travelers need to use a SIM card from a Vietnamese carrier or an international SIM card with roaming capability; if your SIM does not support roaming, purchase a Vietnam SIM card for tourists to ensure you can make emergency calls when needed. Overall, Vietnam is considered a safe destination for tourists, but standard precautions (watching belongings in crowds, using licensed taxis or Grab, and avoiding unlicensed money changers) are sensible everywhere.
