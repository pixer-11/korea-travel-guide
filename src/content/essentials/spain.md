**Quick answer:** Most visitors from the US, UK, Canada, Australia and dozens of other countries can still enter Spain visa-free for up to 90 days in any 180-day period, border checks now use biometric scans instead of passport stamps, and the EU's ETIAS pre-travel authorisation has not launched and has no confirmed date, so always double-check your nationality's rules before flying. Spain uses the euro, has excellent trains and metros, and its "112" number reaches police, fire and ambulance anywhere in the country.

## Visa & entry

Passport holders from the United States, Canada, Australia, the UK, Japan and most Latin American countries can still enter Spain without a visa for stays of up to 90 days within any 180-day period across the entire Schengen Area. Citizens of countries including India, China, South Africa, Nigeria, Pakistan and most African and Asian states must apply for a Schengen visa before travelling.

Two system changes matter in late 2026. First, the EU's Entry/Exit System (EES) is now fully in force: as of 10 April 2026 the European Entry-Exit System is fully operational at all external border crossing points of the countries using EES, so instead of a passport stamp you should expect a biometric check (fingerprints and a facial photo) on your first crossing, with occasional queues reported at busy airports as the system beds in. Second, ETIAS, the pre-travel screening for currently visa-exempt nationals, still has **no confirmed launch date**. The EU spent most of 2025 and 2026 promising a "last quarter of 2026" start, but the Commission's ETIAS page now says only that the system is not in operation, that no applications are being collected, and that the EU will announce the start date several months before launch, and as of 19 October 2026, no date has been announced. Independent trackers as of late October confirm the same picture: as of 26 October 2026, no ETIAS start date has been announced, with some reporting pointing to a 2027 launch as more realistic. When it does eventually launch, officials have indicated it will carry a small fee, with the European Commission setting the ETIAS fee at EUR 20 through the official ETIAS website, and a transitional grace period is expected once it starts, though the exact length will only be confirmed with the launch itself.

Because dates keep slipping, **confirm your own requirement on the official Spanish Ministry of Foreign Affairs entry-conditions page and the official EU ETIAS/EES portals before you travel**, not on a blog or third-party "visa" site. Regardless of visa status, carry a passport valid for at least three months beyond your planned departure, plus proof of funds, accommodation, and a return or onward ticket.

## Getting around

Spain's rail network remains a major asset. AVE high-speed trains connect Madrid and Barcelona via Guadalajara, Calatayud, Zaragoza, Lleida and Tarragona, with other AVE lines linking Madrid to Valencia, Seville, Alicante and beyond, run by state operator Renfe alongside private competitors. Within cities, metro networks serve Madrid, Barcelona, Valencia and Bilbao, with modern tram systems in Barcelona, Valencia and Bilbao, plus expansion underway in Málaga and Zaragoza.

For short stays, city passes are usually the best value. In Madrid, Tourist Travel Passes are loaded onto a rechargeable Tarjeta Multi smartcard, sold as 1, 2, 3, 5 or 7-day unlimited passes; these do not cover AVE or Renfe regional trains. In Barcelona, single tickets and the T-casual multi-ride card do not include the L9 airport metro line; a dedicated Airport ticket is sold exclusively for the Barcelona Metro, and its price for 2026 is €5.90, while the Hola Barcelona card, priced from about €18.70 for 48 hours, gives unlimited travel for 48 to 120 hours with the airport metro included.

If you're travelling longer or around the country by regional rail, Spain's national Abono Único pass (launched 19 January 2026) gives unlimited travel during a 30-day validity period for €60, or €30 for a youth/children rate, covering interregional state buses and Renfe Cercanías/Rodalies and Media Distancia trains. It is aimed at residents in Spain, not short-term tourists, though a visitor can use the standard pass where sales conditions are met, since Renfe lists a passport or equivalent identity document for the normal pass. Short-term visitors doing a lot of long-distance rail travel should instead look at Renfe's dedicated tourist rail pass. Google Maps and Citymapper work well for real-time routes, and licensed taxis and major ride-hailing apps are widely available.

## Money & costs

Spain's currency is the euro. Card payments, including contactless, are accepted almost everywhere in cities, from major stores to small cafés, though it's wise to carry some cash for small rural vendors, markets, or public toilets. When paying by card, choose to be charged in euros rather than your home currency if given the option, since dynamic currency conversion at the terminal is usually a worse rate than your own bank's conversion. ATMs are widely available; using your bank card or a fee-free travel card typically beats currency exchange bureaus. Budget travellers will find lower costs outside Madrid and Barcelona and in the interior versus the coast, while peak summer and major festivals push up accommodation prices everywhere.

## Best time to visit

The shoulder months of April, May, September and October remain the best overall balance of pleasant weather, fewer crowds and good hiking conditions. Weather countrywide is mild with little rain except along the Atlantic coast, and you'll avoid the peak crowds that July and August bring to the Balearic and Canary islands and the coast generally. High summer delivers classic beach weather but also the heaviest crowds and highest prices, with intense heat inland; the edges of the shoulder seasons, late April to early May or late September to early October, offer warm weather with thinner crowds. Winter is mild in the south and along the coasts but colder inland and in the north, and it remains good for museums, cities and lower prices away from the coast.

## Emergencies & safety

Spain's single emergency number, 112, covers police, fire and medical emergencies, works everywhere in the country, is free from landlines and mobiles, and can be reached even with a blocked phone, an inactive SIM, or no coverage from your own provider. Specific services also have direct lines: 091 for Policía Nacional, 062 for Guardia Civil (rural areas and roads), 092 for Policía Local, and 061 in many regions for medical emergencies (elsewhere 112 covers this directly). The fire brigade number is 080 or 085 depending on the province, so 112 is the safest default if you're unsure. For roadside breakdowns, RACE roadside assistance answers 24 hours a day on 900 112 222 (+34 91 594 89 43 from a foreign phone). Non-EU visitors should carry private travel insurance, since you may otherwise be billed directly for treatment, while EU/EEA travellers can use a valid EHIC. Spain is considered a safe destination overall; the main practical risk for tourists is pickpocketing in crowded areas, and if something is stolen, request a denuncia (police report) promptly for insurance claims, embassy support, or replacing documents.
