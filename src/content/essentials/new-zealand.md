**Quick answer:** Most visitors need a pre-approved NZeTA (plus a mandatory visitor levy) before flying to New Zealand, public transport runs on city-specific smartcards or contactless bank cards, the Kiwi dollar (NZD) and tap-to-pay cards are used almost everywhere, and the single number for any emergency is 111.

## Visa & entry

Citizens of roughly 60 visa-waiver countries, including the UK, EU nations, the US, Canada, Japan and Australia, do not need a full visa but must still apply online for a New Zealand Electronic Travel Authority (NZeTA) before travelling. Most visitors from around 60 visa-waiver countries don't need a formal visa, but almost all of them still need an NZeTA (NZD 17-23) plus the NZD 100 International Visitor Levy before travel, roughly NZD 120 total, valid for 2 years. The NZeTA costs NZ$17 through the official mobile app, or NZ$23 through the Immigration New Zealand website, and on top of that the International Visitor Conservation and Tourism Levy (IVL) of NZ$100 is charged at the same time. This combined fee setup has applied since 1 October 2024 (checked October 2026).

The NZeTA itself is valid for 2 years and is required for visa waiver travellers and Australian permanent residents, and it should be requested well ahead of departure, since processing can take up to a few days. Note that any visa-waiver visitor arriving by air or cruise ship needs an NZeTA before travel, including transit passengers who will pass through New Zealand border control. Typical visa-waiver stays allow up to 90 days per visit, though this varies by nationality and can change, so **always confirm your exact entry requirements, fees and permitted stay length on the official Immigration New Zealand NZeTA portal before you book flights**, as rules and fees are periodically reviewed.

## Getting around

New Zealand has no single national transit card; each city runs its own system. In Auckland, get an AT HOP card: NZD$5 for the card at the airport or any dairy, top up NZD$20 or so, and it works on buses, trains, and ferries across the whole region. Paying with the card rather than cash matters financially, because without HOP, every journey costs around 20% more in cash-equivalent fares and many newer routes don't accept cash at all. There's also a cap worth knowing: a $50 seven-day fare cap applies to AT HOP cards, while contactless card and mobile wallet users get a separate $20 daily cap instead.

In Wellington, the system runs on Snapper instead. Get a Snapper card: a NZD$10 starter sold at supermarkets and the airport, working on buses and the Cable Car, with trains around Wellington now using Snapper too on most lines. Christchurch uses its own Metrocard, and most centres also now accept tapping a contactless bank card directly.

For intercity travel, you don't need any card at all: for long-distance coach (InterCity) and scenic trains (Northern Explorer, Coastal Pacific, TranzAlpine), buy tickets online; a contactless bank card works on board. Self-driving remains the most popular way to see the country between cities and national parks. New Zealand drives on the left, and most visitors can use a foreign licence for a limited period, for example a valid U.S. driver's licence for up to 18 months, though requirements (an International Driving Permit, translation, etc.) vary by nationality, so check before you rent a car. Winter alpine driving needs extra caution, since shaded areas of the South Island sometimes develop black ice, and before crossing alpine passes between June and August you should check conditions and carry chains if required.

## Money & costs

The currency is the New Zealand dollar (NZD). Card payments dominate day-to-day spending: contactless "tap" payments using Paywave are now the default for most transactions under $200 and the most common payment method in New Zealand by volume, with most modern terminals supporting it. One quirk to know is that the domestic EFTPOS network is separate from Visa/Mastercard rails; in practice this rarely matters to travellers because EFTPOS is a domestic NZ system foreign cards can't tap into directly, but you'll always see a Visa or Mastercard option on the same terminal.

It's still sensible to carry some cash for small-town stalls, tips-optional cafés or rural spots; a reasonable guide is to budget 100–200 NZD for a typical trip and adjust based on how far off the beaten track you're going. Watch for foreign-exchange traps at ATMs and terminals, where you should always choose to be charged in NZD rather than your home currency to avoid poor dynamic-currency-conversion rates. Bank-branded ATMs typically charge local cardholders little or nothing, but your own bank may apply a foreign transaction fee, so a low-fee travel card or multi-currency card can save money over a longer trip. Tipping is not customary in New Zealand and is never expected, though rounding up or a small tip for exceptional service is always appreciated.

## Best time to visit

New Zealand has four clear seasons, reversed from the Northern Hemisphere: summer runs Dec–Feb; autumn Mar–May; winter June–Aug; and spring Sept–Nov. December to February is the busiest and most expensive stretch, when summer is New Zealand's high season, with long days and warm temperatures making it ideal for a beach holiday or to celebrate New Year's Eve.

For many travellers the shoulder seasons offer the best balance: autumn (March to May) is the sweet spot for many seasoned travellers, as summer crowds thin, prices ease, and the weather often stays settled and pleasant well into April. Spring (September–November) similarly brings fresher landscapes with lighter crowds than summer, though conditions can be more changeable. If you want to combine a road trip with pleasant conditions and thinner crowds, March, April, September, October and November are best months to visit to experience fewer crowds and pleasant weather conditions.

Winter (June–August) is the time for skiing and alpine scenery in the Southern Alps, and also tends to be the cheapest period to fly and find accommodation, though days are shorter and some activities close for the season. Choose your season based on priorities: beaches and festivals favour summer, powder and hot pools favour winter, and hiking, wine and wildlife-watching suit the shoulder months.

## Emergencies & safety

For any genuine emergency, police, fire or ambulance, the single number to dial anywhere in New Zealand is 111. The New Zealand emergency number for Police, Fire or Ambulance services is 111, and calling emergency services from your mobile is free of charge, provided the call is of a genuine nature. You don't need credit or a SIM plan to make the call, since you can call 111 even if you have no credit or minutes on your mobile. When you get through, the operator will ask you which service you need and connect you to the right service.

For situations that need police attention but are not urgent, such as a minor car accident or a burglary report after the fact, use the non-emergency line instead: to report Police non-emergencies that don't require urgent Police assistance, dial 105 from your mobile phone. If you're ever unsure whether something counts as an emergency, the advice is simple: if you are in any doubt as to if it's an emergency, always call 111. Travellers who are Deaf, hard of hearing or have speech difficulties can pre-register for a text-based emergency service, since the 111 TXT service enables people who are Deaf, hard of hearing, or have speech difficulties to contact Police, Fire, or Ambulance services via text in an emergency, and pre-registration is required for the service to work.

For non-emergency health worries, New Zealand also runs a free 24-hour phone health line, Healthline, useful for travellers unsure whether a symptom needs urgent care. General safety in New Zealand is high, but take weather and terrain seriously: alpine areas, beaches with rips, and rural roads with unfamiliar driving conditions (narrow bridges, gravel sections, wandering livestock) are the most common sources of visitor mishaps, so check local conditions before heading into remote or mountainous areas.
