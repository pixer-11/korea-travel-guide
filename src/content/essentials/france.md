**Quick answer:** Most visitors from the US, UK, Canada, Australia and similar countries can still enter France visa-free for up to 90 days in any 180-day period, and should carry a valid passport, proof of onward travel and travel insurance; the EU's ETIAS travel authorisation has not launched and there is still no confirmed start date, so always re-check the official France-Visas and ETIAS sites before you fly.

## Visa & entry

For most short-term visitors from the USA, Canada, Australia, UK, Japan and many other countries, no visa is needed for stays of up to 90 days within any rolling 180-day period, counted across the whole Schengen Area rather than just France. The Entry/Exit System (EES), which digitally logs entries and exits at the external border, became fully operational in 2025 and continues to run alongside this rule.

If your nationality is not on the visa-waiver list, you will need a Schengen short-stay Type C visa, which carries an official government fee of 90 EUR for an adult and 45 EUR for a child aged 6 to 11, with children under 6 free; processing usually takes about 15 calendar days but can extend to 45 days, so apply well ahead of travel.

The big change that has not happened yet is ETIAS, the EU's new travel authorisation for currently visa-exempt travellers. Despite earlier plans for a "last quarter of 2026" launch, the European Commission quietly dropped that target, and as of late October 2026 no ETIAS start date has been announced. Industry trackers and reporting now widely expect the year 2027 remains the most likely possibility, though this is not guaranteed. When ETIAS does go live, expect a modest fee, multi-year validity and a transition period before enforcement becomes strict, but treat any specific launch date you see online with scepticism until the European Commission confirms it, since it has said only that it will announce the date several months in advance.

**Because these rules can shift with little notice, confirm your personal entry requirements on the official France-Visas and EU Travel-Europe/ETIAS websites shortly before you book and again before you fly.** Bring a passport valid well beyond your stay, proof of onward or return travel, evidence of sufficient funds and valid travel insurance, as these can be checked at the border.

## Getting around

Paris and the Île-de-France region now run on a simplified digital ticketing system with no more tariff zones for visitors. As of 2026, the standard Metro-Train-RER ticket costs €2.55, and a separate Bus-Tram ticket costs €2.05, while a flat €14 fare covers Paris Region to airport journeys, and a day pass costs €12.30. For longer stays, the Forfait Navigo Semaine (weekly pass) costs €32.40, and the monthly pass costs €90.80, covering unlimited Métro, RER, bus, tram and suburban trains.

Paper tickets are genuinely finished: magnetic paper tickets stop working on buses in May 2026 and across the entire rail network, including metro, RER and trains, by June 2026. In their place, visitors use either a reloadable Navigo Easy card or a smartphone. Navigo Easy is a prepaid Paris public transport travel card that costs €2 and can be topped up with individual tickets or passes; alternatively, the Île-de-France Mobilités app, Apple Wallet or (from late 2026) Google Wallet can hold the same tickets digitally.

A major practical change for airport transfers: the Roissybus was permanently discontinued on 1 March 2026, and no direct replacement exists. To reach Charles de Gaulle today, use the new Express 9517 service departing from the Saint-Denis Pleyel hub, accessible in 10 minutes from the city centre via line 14, or take the RER B from Parisian stations such as Gare du Nord, Châtelet or Denfert-Rochereau.

Outside Paris, France's high-speed TGV network and regional TER trains connect major cities efficiently; book via SNCF Connect. Renting a car is worthwhile for rural regions like the Dordogne, Provence or the Loire Valley, but city driving and parking can be difficult, so most travellers combine trains for intercity travel with local transit or walking in town centres.

## Money & costs

France uses the euro (EUR). Most places accept cards, with Visa and Mastercard your best bet; smaller shops, street markets or rural bakeries may still be cash-only, so carry some notes and coins, and do not count on Amex or Diners outside big hotels or luxury shops. Contactless payment is common and reliable: you can tap to pay for metro rides, coffee or even market stalls in cities, and Apple Pay and Google Pay work widely, especially in urban areas.

For cash, ATMs are everywhere, but it is safer to choose ones attached to a major French bank such as BNP Paribas, Crédit Agricole or Société Générale, and to avoid standalone machines in tourist zones, which often carry high fees and poor exchange rates.

Tipping is modest and not mandatory, since service is included in the bill by law; rounding up at cafés or leaving 5 to 10 percent for excellent restaurant service is appreciated but not expected, and a euro or two is plenty for taxis. Expect Paris and major tourist hubs such as Nice, Lyon and the Riviera in summer to be noticeably pricier for hotels and dining than rural regions; booking trains and popular museum tickets in advance can also save money and queueing time.

## Best time to visit

There is no single "best" season, but shoulder seasons generally offer the best balance for most travellers. Spring and autumn, roughly April to June and September to October, bring fewer tourists, milder weather and more breathing room, making them ideal for exploring châteaux in the Loire, hiking in the Alps or touring wine regions.

Summer, June to August, is the busiest and priciest period, with full trains, packed beaches, and cities like Paris and Lyon running hot and crowded, while the south buzzes with festivals and seaside life. September is a particular favourite for travellers who want summer-like conditions without peak crowds, since temperatures ease into comfortable territory while days still feel warm enough for swimming, hiking and lingering at sidewalk cafés. For winter travel, the French Alps offer skiing, while Provence, the Riviera and Paris are much quieter and cheaper outside summer, aside from the Christmas and New Year period.

## Emergencies & safety

France's emergency numbers are straightforward: 15 reaches SAMU for medical emergencies and ambulances, 17 reaches the police or gendarmerie for crimes and assaults, and 18 reaches the pompiers, the fire brigade, for fires, gas leaks, road accidents and trapped persons. The pan-European number 112 also works throughout France and will route you to the right service. 114 is reserved for deaf and hard-of-hearing people, a toll-free number available 24/7 for contacting emergency services by fax or SMS. If you are unsure which service you need, dial 112 and the dispatcher will route the call; all of these numbers are free to call from any phone, including locked handsets or those without credit.

General safety in France is good for most tourists, though petty theft such as pickpocketing and phone snatching is a real risk in crowded tourist areas, on public transit, and around major attractions in Paris, Nice and other big cities. Stay alert with bags and phones, use hotel safes, and keep copies of your passport and travel documents separate from the originals. If you lose your passport, contact your embassy or consulate immediately and report lost items to the local police station.
