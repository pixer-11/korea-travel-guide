**Quick answer:** Most Western travelers (US, UK, EU, and dozens more) can enter Turkey visa-free for up to 90 days per 180-day period, while several other nationalities (including the US and Canada, oddly enough for some passport types, and others like Australia) need an e-Visa from the official evisa.gov.tr portal before flying; always double-check your specific nationality before you travel.

## Visa & entry

Turkey sorts visiting nationalities into three groups: visa-free, e-Visa required, and traditional consulate-visa required. Citizens of these countries enter with just a passport, no visa, no fee, no pre-registration, for 90 days visa-free, including all EU countries, the UK, Switzerland, Norway, Iceland, Japan, South Korea, Malaysia, Singapore, Brazil, Argentina, Chile, Uruguay, Hong Kong, New Zealand, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Serbia, Montenegro, Georgia, Ukraine, and Moldova. Some nationalities, including US, Canadian, and Australian citizens, fall into the e-Visa category and must apply online before departure.

Turkey's 90/180 rule means you can stay for no more than 90 days in any rolling 180-day period, and immigration officers look back 180 days from your entry date and count all days already spent in Turkey within that window, a rule that is strictly enforced. If you already need a visa, note that walk-up visa windows at airports and border crossings have been permanently closed, so if you need an e-visa you must apply at evisa.gov.tr before departure. Processing is normally fast: apply at least 48 hours before departure to allow processing time, though the form itself takes about 3 minutes.

Travelers from India and Pakistan face an extra condition: citizens of India and Pakistan can apply for a Turkey e-visa only if they hold a valid visa or residence permit issued by the United States, United Kingdom, or a Schengen area country; without one of those documents, a traditional consulate visa is required. Also check your passport's expiry date, since your passport must be valid for at least six months from your date of entry into Turkey.

**Because these rules change frequently and vary by nationality, always confirm your exact requirements directly on the official evisa.gov.tr portal (or your nearest Turkish consulate) shortly before you travel; do not rely solely on third-party blogs.**

## Getting around

Istanbul's transit network is huge and, for a first-time visitor, the single most useful tool is the Istanbulkart, a rechargeable smart card. It is the essential, multi-purpose travel card for virtually all forms of Istanbul public transport; without it, cash payments are rarely accepted on buses, trams, or metros. This rechargeable, contactless smart card covers metro, tram, bus, ferry, funicular, and even some airport services. You can buy one at any metro station for around ₺150, and top it up at yellow/blue vending machines (Biletmatik) found at stations. One card can be tapped for multiple travelers in your group.

Fares are cheap by international standards. Depending on the source and route, a single ride runs roughly 15-20 TRY on average, making it significantly cheaper than taxis, though newer 2026 pricing puts a standard ride closer to the low-40s TRY with transfers within 90 minutes discounted. Ferries are one of the best ways to see the Bosphorus while commuting: the most important routes for visitors run from Karaköy, Eminönü, or Beşiktaş on the European side to Kadıköy or Üsküdar on the Asian side, with additional ferries from Kadıköy, Eminönü, and Beşiktaş to the Princes' Islands. The historic T1 tram is especially handy for sightseeing: it runs from Kabataş to Bağcılar through the heart of the old city, stopping at Karaköy, Eminönü, Sirkeci, Gülhane, Sultanahmet, Çemberlitaş, Beyazıt Square (for the Grand Bazaar), Laleli, and Aksaray.

Beyond Istanbul, Turkey has an extensive intercity bus network, domestic flights connecting major cities, and high-speed rail linking Ankara, Istanbul, and Konya. Ride-hailing apps and metered taxis are common in big cities, and many taxis now also accept card payment.

## Money & costs

The official currency is the Turkish lira (TRY, symbol ₺). The lira is a floating currency, so its value against the euro, US dollar, and British pound changes daily; because rates move, it is worth checking the current exchange rate shortly before you travel and again once you arrive.

Card acceptance has grown substantially in recent years: in Istanbul and other tourist areas, credit and debit cards are accepted almost everywhere, including hotels, restaurants, larger shops, museums, and many attractions. Still, cash has its place: small family-run cafes, neighborhood markets, street food vendors, some taxis, and bazaar stalls may prefer or require it, so a sensible approach is to pay by card where you can while keeping a modest amount of lira on hand. Visa and Mastercard dominate; American Express is generally limited to higher-end hotels, restaurants, and selected businesses.

ATMs are your best source of cash: they are widely available throughout Turkey, from major cities and airports to smaller towns and tourist destinations, and most machines offer English language options and accept international debit and credit cards. When an ATM or card terminal asks whether to charge you in lira or your home currency, always choose lira, since declining the conversion and choosing to be charged in lira lets your own bank set the exchange rate, which is generally more favorable. Be aware that Turkish banks have layered persistent ATM operator fees onto foreign cards, typically ₺30 to ₺100 per withdrawal.

Tipping is customary but modest: it is customary in Turkey, and it is recommended to tip around 5 to 10 percent of the bill in restaurants, cafes and bars.

## Best time to visit

For most travel purposes, the shoulder seasons win out. April, May, September and October are pleasantly warm, with temperatures of 68°F to 86°F, so are typically the best times to visit Turkey's grand array of ancient sites. These months also mean fewer crowds and more comfortable sightseeing conditions than the height of summer.

If beaches and coastal sailing are your priority, aim for peak summer instead: the best months for sailing in Turkey are generally from May to October, with the peak season being between June and September. Just expect intense heat inland and on the coast during July and August, when high temperatures can soar up to 40°C (104°F) in central and southeastern regions.

For budget travel, go in the depths of winter: the period between January and March is the cheapest time to visit Turkey, as these winter months are considered low season. Note that Istanbul and Cappadocia stay popular year-round, so prices may not drop significantly even in the low season in those two destinations specifically.

## Emergencies & safety

Turkey has unified most emergency services under a single number. Citizens can now seek help in emergencies by dialing the unified number 112, with these "112 Emergency Call Centers" expanded nationwide, unifying fire (110), medical (112), police (155), gendarmerie (156), disaster response AFAD (122), forest fire (177), and coast guard (158) under one umbrella. As a tourist, dial 112 first for any serious emergency: the number designated for all types of emergencies, whether you need police, ambulance, fire department, or coast guard.

Language is not a barrier: the 112 Emergency Call Centers provide service in English, Arabic, German, and Russian in addition to Turkish. For non-emergency theft, crime reports, or traffic incidents specifically, you can also call the dedicated police line: for crimes, theft, security concerns or any situation requiring police assistance, you can dial 155, a number that applies to all urban areas across Türkiye. If you're in a rural area, note that in rural or remote areas, security operations are handled by the Gendarmerie, so dial 156 when you need assistance outside city limits or in villages.

General safety advice: keep photocopies (digital and paper) of your passport and visa/e-Visa, use registered taxis or ride-hailing apps rather than unmarked cars, and stay alert to your surroundings in crowded tourist areas like the Grand Bazaar and Taksim Square, as with any major city. Check your home country's travel advisory before departure for any region-specific advice, particularly for areas near the Syrian border in the southeast.
