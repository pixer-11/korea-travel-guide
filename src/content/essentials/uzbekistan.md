**Quick answer:** Most visitors from the EU, UK, US, Gulf states and dozens of other countries can enter Uzbekistan visa-free for up to 30 days, pay mainly in Uzbek som (cash still rules outside big hotels), and the best window for sightseeing is April to early June or September to early November. Always verify your specific visa category on the official portals before you fly, since eligibility lists change.

## Visa & entry

Uzbekistan has one of the most liberal visa regimes in Central Asia. Uzbekistan has one of the most open visa policies in Central Asia, and citizens of 90+ countries can enter visa-free for up to 30 days, while most others qualify for a fast online e-Visa. Passport holders from the EU, UK, USA, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, Japan, South Korea, Singapore, UAE, Israel, Turkey, and most CIS countries can enter Uzbekistan without a visa for stays of up to 30 days for tourism.

If your nationality is not on the visa-free list, you should apply online at e-visa.gov.uz; the standard e-Visa costs USD 20, is valid for 90 days, and allows a stay of up to 30 days, with processing usually taking 2–3 business days. Some nationalities enjoy longer or shorter windows: for example, citizens of some CIS and neighbouring nations enjoy 60 to 90 days under separate bilateral arrangements. There are also special provisions for certain older travellers and for a handful of nationalities holding qualifying third-country visas: residents of the UAE, along with certain individuals aged 55 and over, are exempt from needing a visa, and citizens of India may enter without a visa for up to 30 days if they hold a valid multiple-entry business or tourist visa issued by Australia, Canada, Japan, New Zealand, the Republic of Korea, a Schengen Area country, the United Kingdom, or the United States.

Practical entry checklist: your passport must be valid for at least 3 months after your planned exit date and have at least one blank page for the entry stamp. Registration is largely automatic for hotel guests: hotels and official guesthouses register you automatically and give a small paper slip, and you should keep every slip until you leave, as border officers may ask for them on exit.

Because visa-free lists, durations and e-visa fees are revised fairly often, do not rely on any single blog, including this one. Confirm your exact category, permitted stay length, and required documents directly on the official State Committee for Tourism Development and immigration/e-visa portals before booking flights.

## Getting around

**Trains** are the most comfortable way to link the main Silk Road cities. The high-speed Afrosiyob and the slower Sharq services connect Tashkent, Samarkand, Bukhara and Khiva; tickets can be bought online at Uzbekistan Railways with credit cards, and it's recommended to buy them online a few weeks in advance since they sell out quickly. A useful comparison from a recent trip: a regular 2nd-class seat from Tashkent to Bukhara on the Sharq train cost 90,000 UZS (about $7), versus 180,000 UZS (about $16) on the Afrosiyob high-speed train.

**Buses and shared minivans** are the budget option between towns: you can check bus schedules and buy tickets online or at local bus stations, though you will need your passport to book a seat and it is better to buy at least a day in advance.

**Tashkent Metro**, opened in 1977, is Central Asia's oldest and remains the easiest way around the capital. It is the rapid-transit system of Tashkent, famous for lavishly decorated stations with marble, mosaics, chandeliers and themed art, and today runs on four lines serving 50 stations, including an elevated Circle Line. Fares are flat regardless of distance: there is one flat fare for any journey, 1,700 soʻm with a contactless ATTO card or the mobile app, or 3,000 soʻm for a cash token. Trains are frequent: trains run every day from about 05:00 to 24:00, every 2–4 minutes at peak times.

For repeat rides, get the **ATTO card**, Uzbekistan's unified transport card. ATTO is Uzbekistan's unified transport card, and the same card works on the metro and city buses, in Tashkent and around twenty other cities. You do not strictly need one, though: you can tap a contactless Visa, Mastercard, Uzcard or Humo card directly on the turnstile and pay the cheaper electronic fare.

**Taxis and ride-hailing** apps (Yandex Go being the most common) work well in Tashkent, and note that acceptance varies: Yandex Go only accepts credit card in Tashkent. Elsewhere, cash rides are the norm.

## Money & costs

The currency is the **Uzbek som (UZS)**. The most common banknotes are the 2,000, 10,000, and 20,000. ATMs are now widespread: Tashkent, Samarkand, Bukhara, and Khiva all have ATMs where you can take out US dollars and/or Uzbek som with a Visa card, and there are also two ATMs at the international airport in Tashkent. Card networks vary by machine: most ATMs accept Visa and Maestro cards, some accept Mastercard, and almost no ATMs accept American Express. Expect a small surcharge: all ATMs in Uzbekistan charge a withdrawal fee between 1% and 3%, with 1.5% being most common.

Despite growing card acceptance in cities, Uzbekistan remains cash-oriented outside the capital. Cards are accepted in many hotels, larger restaurants, shops and tourist areas, but cash is still widely used for markets, taxis, small cafés, local transport and smaller towns. Contactless payments are growing in Tashkent and major cities but are not universal, and international mobile wallets like Apple Pay and Google Pay have limited acceptance. Carry a cash backup, and inform your bank you're travelling; also be aware of the currency choice trap at ATMs: enter your PIN and choose local currency rather than the "convert to USD" option, since it typically costs more.

Customs rules on cash are generous but not unlimited: up to the equivalent of USD 10,000 can be brought in or taken out without declaration, and amounts above this must be declared to customs. Note too that large cash transactions in foreign currency are prohibited and payments must generally be made in UZS.

Budget-wise, Uzbekistan remains inexpensive by international standards. One recent estimate suggested for a modest-to-comfortable trip, around £40 to £60 per person per day can cover simple accommodation, local restaurants, short taxi rides and small extras.

## Best time to visit

Uzbekistan's continental climate makes spring and autumn the sweet spots. For most visitors, the sweet spots are spring (April–May) and fall (September–early November), months that offer mild temperatures, clear skies, and comfortable conditions for exploring cities, deserts, and mountains. April is frequently singled out: April is one of the best months to visit, especially for sightseeing, with average temperatures around 64°F (18°C), ideal for walking around cities like Bukhara and Khiva.

Autumn has its own charm and the crowds thin out: late September and early October is the ideal time to visit the big three, Samarkand, Bukhara and Khiva, as temperatures are still warm but many of the crowds have gone. Autumn (September–November) is a close runner-up, offering pleasant temperatures (21–30°C), golden scenery, and vibrant markets overflowing with melons, grapes, and pomegranates.

Avoid high summer if you dislike extreme heat: summer (June–August) is extremely hot in lowland cities (up to 45°C), though ideal for trekking and escaping to the cooler Nuratau Mountains and authentic rural villages. Winter is the quiet, budget season: winter is a perfect time to visit the historic cities of Samarkand, Bukhara and Khiva on a budget while avoiding the crowds, with prices falling for accommodation and transportation as the weather cools, though temperatures rarely dip below -3°C. If your trip coincides with Navruz (Persian New Year, 21 March), book ahead: if you plan to visit Uzbekistan during Navruz, book well in advance, especially for the high-speed trains to Samarkand and Bukhara.

## Emergencies & safety

Uzbekistan uses both a unified European-style number and separate Soviet-era service lines. The unified emergency number is 112, police is 102, ambulance is 103, fire service is 101, and there is also a dedicated Safe Tourism Call Center at 1173. If something urgent happens, contact the professionals directly: call 112 if there is a threat to life, health, fire, smell of gas, violence, a road accident or a crime, calling the official emergency services first. For non-urgent tourist issues such as getting lost or needing help, for tourist situations without immediate danger, for example getting lost, a conflict, or contact with Tourist Police, use 1173 and inform your hotel, guide or local tour operator.

These lines are free to use: these numbers are free to call from any phone, including mobile devices and landlines, anywhere in Uzbekistan. Response times can vary widely by region, so plan travel insurance with medical evacuation cover, especially outside the main cities: one guide notes ambulance response averages roughly 10–15 minutes in urban areas but 25–40 minutes in rural regions, with a public healthcare system and only limited specialised trauma centers concentrated in Tashkent and Samarkand.

Uzbekistan is widely regarded as a safe destination for tourists, with petty theft and scams being the main practical concerns in busy bazaars and stations rather than violent crime. Save your embassy's emergency contact number, keep digital and paper copies of your passport and visa/registration slips, and register serious incidents with the police (102) before contacting your insurer.
