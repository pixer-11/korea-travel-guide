**Quick answer:** Most visitors from the US, EU, UK, and 90+ other countries can enter Uzbekistan visa-free for up to 30 days, pay mainly in cash (Uzbek som), travel between Silk Road cities on the fast Afrosiyob train, and should visit in April–May or September–October for the best weather — but always double-check entry rules on the official e-visa portal before booking.

## Visa & entry

Uzbekistan has significantly liberalized its entry policy in recent years. For citizens of over 90 countries, visa-free entry is available, with permitted stays of 30 days for most nationalities and extended periods for CIS and bilateral treaty nationals. This visa-free list covers most of the traditional long-haul tourist markets: EU and Schengen zone passport holders can all enter without a visa, as can citizens of the USA, Canada, Australia, New Zealand, Japan, South Korea, Singapore, the UK. Notably, beginning January 1, 2026, U.S. citizens can enter Uzbekistan visa-free for up to 30 days from the date of entry, representing a major shift in the country's approach to American travelers.

Citizens of neighboring and CIS countries generally get longer stays. Citizens of the following post-soviet countries do not need an Uzbekistan visa: with unlimited time: Armenia, Azerbaijan, Belarus, Georgia, Kazakhstan, Moldova, Russia, and Ukraine; up to 60 days Kyrgyzstan.

If your nationality isn't on the visa-free list, you'll need an e-visa. An electronic visa to Uzbekistan can be conveniently obtained through the official portal at e-visa.gov.uz. Typical processing is fast — Uzbekistan's e-visa system allows citizens of 80+ countries to apply online and receive their visa within 2 business days — though some sources cite slightly longer windows depending on nationality, so build in a buffer of at least a week before departure.

Practical entry notes:
- Bring a passport valid for at least 3–6 months beyond your stay, a digital photo, and proof of accommodation for e-visa applications.
- Border and airport security checks are standard; keep your passport handy since it's checked repeatedly, including at train stations.

⚠️ **Visa rules change frequently in Uzbekistan.** Always confirm your specific nationality's requirements, exact stay length, and fees directly on the official e-visa portal (e-visa.gov.uz) and your nearest Uzbek embassy/consulate before booking flights — the summary above can go out of date.

## Getting around

The classic Silk Road route (Tashkent–Samarkand–Bukhara–Khiva) is best covered by rail. Trains 759 to 780 are the Afrosiyob, a 250 km/h high-speed air-conditioned Talgo 250 train, with VIP class, Business class and Economy class plus a restaurant car. Tashkent to Samarkand takes about 2 hours 15 minutes to 2.5 hours, while Tashkent to Bukhara takes around 3.5 to 4 hours. A newer high-speed option has also launched: the Jaloliddin Manguberdi, a modern high-speed train, started operating in May 2026, and the network now extends further west — from 1 May 2026, this train covers the Tashkent–Khiva-area route in approximately 7 hours, versus 14 hours on the classic overnight sleeper.

Book early: tickets typically become available 45 to 60 days before departure and popular routes, especially the Afrosiyob, sell out quickly. Book via the Uzbekistan Railways app/website or a reputable third-party booking agent. Slower, cheaper conventional trains (Sharq, Nasaf) run the same corridor and often have availability when the Afrosiyob is sold out.

Within Tashkent, the metro is both a practical and a sightseeing must-do. Opened in 1977, it is the only metro system in Central Asia and is widely regarded as one of the most beautiful metro systems in the world. Payment is simple and cashless: tap a contactless bank card or your phone straight on the turnstile and ride for 1,700 soʻm — a flat fare for any journey, with no distance bands. ATTO is Uzbekistan's unified transport card, and the same card works on the metro and on city buses, in Tashkent and around twenty other cities. International Visa, Mastercard, Uzcard, and Humo contactless cards can also be tapped directly at metro turnstiles. Taxis (use ride-hailing apps like Yandex Go for transparent pricing), intercity shared taxis, and domestic flights fill in the gaps between rail-served cities.

## Money & costs

The local currency is the Uzbekistani som (UZS), and paper notes come in denominations of 1, 3, 5, 10, 25, 50, 100, 200, 500, 1,000, 5,000, and 10,000, while coins come in denominations of 1, 5, 10, 25, 50 and 100 (many higher-value 20,000/50,000/100,000 notes also circulate). This is still largely a cash economy: cash is still the most commonly accepted form of payment, especially outside big cities and hotels — most everyday needs like markets, taxis, and street food run on cash, with Visa accepted more often than Mastercard.

For withdrawals, ATMs are available in cities and airports, and most accept international cards (Visa, Mastercard), though ATMs dispense UZS only. Expect fees: the majority of bank ATMs in Uzbekistan charge anywhere between 1% to 2% of the transaction amount as an ATM access fee for foreign card holders. When prompted at the machine, decline dynamic currency conversion to get a better rate. Also note that ATMs in Uzbekistan have a maximum withdrawal limit per transaction of UZS 3,000,000.

It's smart to carry backup cash: bring US Dollars (USD) in clean, recent notes when traveling to Uzbekistan, as the local currency is not easily available outside the country. Currency declaration rules are generous for most tourists — as of 2026, you can bring up to $10,000 USD (or equivalent) without declaring it; if you carry more, you must fill out a customs declaration form upon entry and keep it until you depart. Card acceptance is expanding but uneven: cash is widely used, but electronic payments are increasingly accepted in urban areas, with major hotels, restaurants, and shops in cities accepting Visa and Mastercard. Mobile wallets remain limited — international mobile wallets (Apple Pay, Google Pay) have limited acceptance.

## Best time to visit

Spring and autumn are the sweet spots. April, May, September, and October are the most comfortable months for most travellers — days are warm, sightseeing is easier, and the weather is generally at its most agreeable. More specifically, spring arrives early, with apricot trees blossoming by the beginning of March, and from March till early June you get comfortable weather, warm and relatively dry, with temperatures hovering between 14°C and 30°C.

Summer is intensely hot: July and August are the hottest and driest months of the year in Uzbekistan, with temperatures in the mid to high 30s°C (above 80°F) — sightseeing in Samarkand, Bukhara, and Khiva can be gruelling in midday heat, though the Tashkent-area mountains offer relief. Autumn is a favorite among connoisseurs: for many travellers, autumn is the best time of year to visit Uzbekistan, as September and October bring warm days, clear weather, and markets at their most abundant, with grapes from Samarkand and Fergana arriving at their best.

Winter is cold but rewarding for those chasing empty monuments and mountain snow: late November, December, January, and February are usually the quietest months, with the best room availability and lower prices. Skiers should note that nearby resorts are a genuine option — the Amirsoy mountain resort, easily accessible from Tashkent, has made Uzbekistan Central Asia's newest high-end skiing destination. Whatever the season, book Afrosiyob tickets and hotels well ahead if traveling around the Navruz holiday (March) or in peak spring/autumn windows.

## Emergencies & safety

Uzbekistan has rolled out a unified emergency line similar to Europe's 112. The unified dispatch service 112 is now active across all regions of Uzbekistan, combining emergency hotlines of the Ministries of Interior, Emergency, Health, Social Protection Agency and the gas operating company. The older direct-service numbers still work in parallel: existing emergency numbers — 101 (fire department), 102 (police), 103 (ambulance), and 104 (gas) — remain in operation for some time alongside the new system.

For tourists specifically, there's a dedicated line: emergency numbers in Uzbekistan for tourists include 112, police 102, ambulance 103, fire 101, gas 104/1104 and Tourist Police 1173. Use this hierarchy sensibly: for tourist situations without immediate danger — for example, getting lost, a conflict, help for a foreign visitor or contact with Tourist Police — use 1173 and inform your hotel, guide or local tour operator, but for anything urgent, call 112 or the specific service number directly rather than messaging a tour operator first.

General safety: Uzbekistan is considered one of the safer destinations in Central Asia for tourists, with low rates of violent crime against visitors, though petty theft and traffic hazards (aggressive driving, uneven pavements) warrant normal caution. Register with your home country's embassy/consular program if one is available, carry a photocopy or photo of your passport and visa/entry stamp, and keep hospital and consulate contact numbers saved before you travel to remote areas like Nukus or Khiva, where medical facilities are more limited than in Tashkent.
