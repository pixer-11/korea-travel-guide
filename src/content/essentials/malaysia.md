**Quick answer:** Most Western, EU, and many Asian passport holders can enter Malaysia visa-free for up to 90 days for tourism, everyone must submit the free Malaysia Digital Arrival Card (MDAC) online before arrival, and the country runs on the Malaysian ringgit (RM) with cheap, cash-optional public transport via the Touch 'n Go card.

## Visa & entry

Malaysia's entry system is straightforward for most tourists but always double check your specific nationality before booking. As of 2026, most Western passport holders, including US, UK, and EU citizens, can enter Malaysia visa-free for up to 90 days on a standard tourist entry, with no prior application needed. Indian and Chinese nationals have a separate arrangement: Indian nationals benefit from a 30-day visa exemption extended until 31 December 2026, covering tourism, business, social visits, and transit, and similarly, Chinese passport holders qualify for 30 days visa-free entry, part of the Malaysia-China mutual visa exemption arrangement, extended until 31 December 2026. For Chinese nationals specifically, note that this visa exemption now only allows a stay of up to 30 days, with a maximum stay of 90 days within any 180-day period, rather than 90 days per single entry.

If your nationality is not on the visa-exempt list (this typically includes Pakistan, Bangladesh, Nepal, and several other countries), you will need to apply for an eVisa in advance through the official portal.

Regardless of your visa status, nearly everyone must complete the digital arrival form. As stated by official sources, effective January 2024, all foreign nationals are required to apply for the Malaysia Digital Arrival Card (MDAC) within 3 days of your arrival, with limited exemptions for permanent residents and diplomatic passport holders. At the airport, the autogate at arrivals lets travelers scan their passport and walk through an automated gate for immigration clearance in many cases.

A practical warning worth heeding: exceeding your authorized stay or not having a valid visa may result in an entry ban, detention, or fines. Also be aware that for those on the 30-day social visit pass scheme, the 30-day Social Visit Pass cannot be extended inside Malaysia, so if travellers wish to stay longer, they must exit and re-enter, and re-entry is not guaranteed.

**Confirm before you fly:** visa rules, exemption lists, and permitted stay lengths change periodically. Always verify your specific requirements on the official Malaysian Immigration Department website and the MDAC portal before booking flights.

## Getting around

Kuala Lumpur and other major cities have a genuinely useful public transport network, and the key to using it smoothly is a single stored-value card. The Touch 'n Go card is the main solution for a tourist: it is a universal contactless card for all transport except taxis and airport express, and can be bought for 10 ringgit at most metro stations, kiosks, and gas stations.

This card is essential rather than optional for buses: city buses (Rapid KL, Go KL) do not accept cash or cards, so the only way to pay is the Touch 'n Go transport card. The same applies to suburban rail: Suburban trains KTM Komuter have no ticket counters, so you can only pay with a Touch 'n Go card or through self-service terminals that do not accept cash. For the metro system itself, you have more flexibility, as you can buy a single ticket at ticket machines at stations, and they accept cash and Visa/Mastercard cards, except UnionPay.

To reach the city from Kuala Lumpur International Airport (KLIA), the fastest option is the express rail link. The KLIA Ekspres or KLIA Transit gets you from KLIA to KL Sentral in less than 30 minutes. If you want a bundled option, the operator sells a combined pass: the KL TravelPass is a smart payment card issued by Express Rail Link, and includes KLIA Ekspres airport transfer plus 2-day unlimited rides on RapidKL buses and rail services (BRT, LRT, MRT and KL Monorail lines) and a RM5 cash value.

Rapid KL, the main city operator, keeps generous hours: Rapid KL rail and bus operates 7 days a week including public holidays, typically from around 6am to midnight. KL Sentral is the network's beating heart, since it is the main interchange hub, where KTM Komuter, Kelana Jaya LRT, KL Monorail, KLIA Ekspres, and ERL all connect.

For point-to-point trips or late-night travel, ride-hailing apps are widely used and reliable, with Grab being the dominant player. Between cities, intercity buses and the ETS train network are affordable and comfortable alternatives to flying.

## Money & costs

The local currency is the Malaysian ringgit (RM or MYR). Malaysia is generally an affordable destination compared to Singapore or much of Western Europe, and street food, local transport, and budget accommodation remain very reasonable for visitors.

Cards are widely accepted in cities, malls, and mid-range to upscale restaurants, but small hawker stalls, local buses, and rural areas often remain cash-preferred, so carrying some ringgit is wise. ATMs are common in cities and airports for withdrawals, and it's sensible to notify your bank of travel dates to avoid card blocks. As with most Southeast Asian countries, watch for foreign transaction fees on your card, and consider a travel-friendly debit or credit card that waives them.

Budget travelers can expect hostel dorms, local food courts, and public transport to keep daily costs low, while mid-range travelers combining hotels, taxis or Grab rides, and restaurant dining should budget noticeably more. Tipping is not deeply embedded in Malaysian culture; most restaurant bills already include a service charge, though rounding up for good service is appreciated.

## Best time to visit

Malaysia's tropical, near-equatorial climate means it's warm and humid year-round, but the timing of monsoons varies significantly by coast, which matters a lot for trip planning. The monsoon season on the eastern side of the Peninsula is between November and February, which is at the same time when the west coast experiences sunny and dry weather; conversely, the wettest months on the west coast are April to October, which are the driest months on the east coast.

This means there is rarely a single "worst" time to visit the whole country, but timing matters a great deal if you're chasing beaches. For the east coast islands like the Perhentians, Redang, and Tioman, the best time to explore is from March to September, during the dry season. Outside this window, conditions can shut things down entirely: boat schedules to the east coast islands and many hotels on these islands close down completely between the months of November to March.

For the west coast, including Kuala Lumpur, Penang, and Langkawi, the picture is gentler. The Southwest Monsoon from May to September affects the west coast mildly, with just brief showers, meaning west coast destinations can remain viable all year.

Borneo (Sabah and Sarawak) follows its own rhythm: the states of Sabah and Sarawak follow a different rainfall rhythm, with rain more evenly spread throughout the year and no true dry season, just wetter and less-wet months. Generally, the months between March and October are the best time to visit Borneo.

In short: aim for December to March if prioritizing the west coast and Kuala Lumpur, and March to September if the east coast islands are your priority.

## Emergencies & safety

Malaysia uses a unified emergency hotline. The 999 emergency line combines all emergency numbers into one, involving the Malaysian Civil Defense Department, the Royal Malaysian Police, the Ministry of Health, and the Fire and Rescue Department. This number covers police, ambulance, and fire, and importantly, every call through the 999 emergency line is free.

If you're calling from a mobile phone, there's a backup option: 112 is the standard emergency number globally, which can be made from a mobile phone regardless of whether it has credit, is barred, or locked, though calls can only be made if there is a network connection.

Kuala Lumpur also has dedicated support for foreign visitors: Kuala Lumpur has a Tourist Police force specifically there to help visitors, usually stationed in popular tourist areas like Bukit Bintang, KLCC, and Chinatown.

Be aware that misuse of the emergency line carries real penalties: each fake call can be fined RM50,000 or imprisoned for one year or both according to Section 233, Communications and Multimedia Act 1998, so only use 999 or 112 for genuine emergencies.

For everyday safety, Malaysia is generally welcoming to tourists, but standard precautions apply: watch your belongings in crowded markets and on public transport, use registered taxis or ride-hailing apps rather than unmarked cars, and keep photocopies or digital scans of your passport and visa documents. Travel insurance covering medical treatment and evacuation is strongly recommended, since public healthcare for tourists and private hospital care are both paid out of pocket at the time of service.
