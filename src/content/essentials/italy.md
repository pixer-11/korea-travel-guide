## Visa & entry

Italy is a full member of the Schengen Area, which as of 2026 has 29 members: 25 EU countries and 4 countries outside the EU (Iceland, Liechtenstein, Norway and Switzerland). For most Western travelers, US, UK, Canadian, and Australian citizens can still visit Italy visa-free for up to 90 days in any 180-day period. That allowance is shared across the whole zone: one visa (if you need one) covers all 29 countries, and your 90 days are counted across the whole area, not per country. Once you've used up your 90 days within any rolling 180-day window, you must leave and wait before re-entering.

Two border systems have reshaped travel in 2026. The EU's biometric Entry/Exit System (EES) is now fully live: the EU's Entry/Exit System has been fully operational at every Schengen external border since 10 April 2026, and for non-EU passport holders the border step now takes longer than it used to. At first entry, border officers capture your fingerprints and a facial image instead of stamping your passport, with only a fast verification needed on later trips.

ETIAS, the pre-travel online authorization, is still not required and its launch keeps slipping. The Commission's ETIAS page now says only that the system is not in operation, that no applications are being collected, and that the EU will announce the start date several months before launch; as of 19 October 2026, no date has been announced. The long-touted "last quarter of 2026" target has been dropped from the official ETIAS site with no replacement date, and press reports widely expect a launch in 2027 at the earliest, though the EU has not confirmed any date. When it does launch, expect a transitional period during which ETIAS is encouraged but not strictly enforced, and once mandatory it is expected to cost around €20. Don't plan a trip around a specific ETIAS date; check the official EU ETIAS portal before booking.

Travelers from countries that are not visa-exempt still need a traditional Schengen short-stay (Type C) visa, applied for in advance through Italy's official visa portal. **Because EES is new and ETIAS timing keeps shifting, always confirm your exact requirements on the official sites listed below before you travel, especially close to departure.**

## Getting around

High-speed rail is the backbone of Italian travel, with two competing operators, Trenitalia (including Frecciarossa/Frecciargento) and Italo, linking Rome, Milan, Florence, Venice and Naples. For visitors doing multiple long-distance trips, the Trenitalia Pass offers flexible travel across the national network, with seat reservations mandatory and free of charge.

Regional trains work differently: paper tickets must be validated before boarding, though contactless "tap and go" payment is being piloted on select routes, including Florence-Pisa-Livorno and the Venice-Padua-Verona corridor, where the same validators accept a contactless bank card in place of a paper ticket. Booking online in advance pays off on faster trains, with discounted fares released up to six months ahead.

Within cities, Rome, Milan, Naples and Turin run metro systems alongside integrated bus and tram networks. In Rome, your ticket is a Metrebus Roma BIT, which costs €1.50 and is valid for 100 minutes from first validation, covering a single journey on metro or urban train and unlimited journeys on other public transport within that time. You can also tap a contactless card or your smartphone at the turnstiles for the same €1.50 fare, and you'll never spend more than €8.50 within a 24-hour period. If you're driving, watch for ZTL (limited traffic zone) cameras in historic centers, which issue automatic fines by mail.

## Money & costs

Italy uses the euro (€). Contactless cards are widely accepted in shops, restaurants and on transport, but keep some cash for small cafés, markets and tips. Italy's standard VAT (IVA) rate is 22%, which sits in the middle of the EU pack: it is below Hungary's 27% and Finland's 25.5%, Sweden's, Denmark's and Croatia's 25%, yet above the UK's, France's and Germany's rates.

Non-EU residents can reclaim VAT on qualifying retail purchases. The minimum spend is at least €70.01 in a single store on the same day, and Italy eased the process in 2026: since January 1, 2026, the customs-stamped invoice must reach the seller within six months of purchase instead of four, and from July 1, 2026, customs can validate several tax-free invoices issued to the same traveller in one step on the OTELLO 2.0 digital platform, where the refund operators involved take part.

Nearly every Italian city or town charges a separate overnight tourist tax (tassa/imposta di soggiorno), collected by your accommodation and calculated per person, per night, with rates varying by city and hotel category; ask your accommodation for the exact current amount. Some cities also charge separate landmark or day-tripper fees, such as Venice's entry fee on peak days, so budget a little extra. Restaurant bills often include a small "coperto" cover charge, and tipping is appreciated but not obligatory.

## Best time to visit

For pleasant weather with fewer crowds and lower prices, aim for shoulder season: April-May and September-October typically bring comfortable temperatures, manageable crowds, and lower prices than summer. Peak season (June through August) brings warm weather and long days but also higher prices and bigger crowds. Winter (November to March) offers the lowest prices and thinnest crowds in cities like Rome, Florence and Milan, though beach destinations and the Alps run on a different seasonal logic, with ski season peaking December to March.

## Emergencies & safety

Save these numbers before you land: 112 is the general EU-wide emergency number (English available), 113 is police, 115 is fire, and 118 is ambulance. Dial 112 for any emergency, anywhere in Italy; it's free from any landline or mobile phone. For roadside breakdowns, ACI (Automobile Club d'Italia) provides nationwide assistance; look up the current ACI Soccorso Stradale number on their official site before a road trip, since dedicated hotline numbers can change.

Overall, Italy remains a low-risk destination for tourists: violent crime against visitors is rare and the healthcare system is strong. The main practical risk is petty theft and pickpocketing around transit hubs, major piazzas, and historic centers in Rome, Milan, Venice and Florence, so stay alert with bags and valuables. Public hospital emergency rooms (marked "Pronto Soccorso") treat everyone regardless of insurance, but non-EU citizens are typically charged a fixed "ticket" fee plus the cost of tests or medication, so travel insurance covering medical costs is strongly recommended.
