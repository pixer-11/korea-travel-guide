**Quick answer:** As of September 2026, most Western passport holders (US, UK, Canada, Australia, EU/Schengen) can still enter Italy visa-free for up to 90 days in any 180-day period, with no ETIAS yet required; just confirm your specific nationality's rules before you fly, since border systems are actively changing.

## Visa & entry

Italy is a full Schengen Area member, so Italy is one of the 30 Schengen Area member states and a full member of the Schengen Area. For most Western travelers, US, UK, Canadian, and Australian travellers can visit Italy visa-free for up to 90 days in any 180-day Schengen period. That allowance is shared across the whole Schengen zone, not just Italy: the 90-day visa-free period applies to the entire Schengen Area, not just Italy, and a day in France counts against your Italian allowance. Once you've spent 90 days in the Schengen zone within any rolling 180-day window, you must leave and wait another 90 days before you can re-enter. Overstaying is taken seriously, since illegal stay is punishable under Italian law (D.Lgs. 286/1998, art. 10-bis) by a fine of €5,000 to €10,000, plus possible expulsion and an entry ban.

Two big systems have changed how borders work in 2026. First, the EU's biometric Entry/Exit System (EES) is now live: the EES is fully operational across all Schengen countries since 10 April 2026, replacing manual passport stamps with fingerprint and photo capture on first entry. Second, ETIAS, the pre-travel online authorization for visa-exempt nationalities, is still not required. As of the latest reporting, you do not need ETIAS to travel to the Schengen Area in September 2026, and the official EU ETIAS website states that ETIAS is "currently not in operation," with no launch date announced yet. When it does launch, expect a soft rollout: there will be a transitional period of at least six months where travellers should apply but won't be refused entry without one, followed by a grace period of at least six more months. Once mandatory, ETIAS will cost around €20 and last three years from issue, or until the linked passport expires, whichever comes first, for multi-entry stays of up to 90 days per 180-day rolling window.

Travelers from countries that are not visa-exempt still need a traditional Schengen short-stay (Type C) visa, applied for in advance through Italy's official visa portal, generally costing around €80 for adults. **Because these rules are being rolled out in phases through 2026 and 2027, always confirm your exact requirements on the official EU and Italian government sites below before booking, especially if traveling in the final months of 2026.**

## Getting around

High-speed rail is the backbone of Italian travel, with two competing operators, Trenitalia (state-owned, including Frecciarossa/Frecciargento trains) and Italo, linking major cities like Rome, Milan, Florence, Venice and Naples. In 2024, the Italian national rail service Trenitalia was recognized as the best rail passenger operator in Europe. For visitors doing multiple long-distance trips, the Trenitalia Pass offers flexible travel on the national network, and the Trenitalia pass can be used on Frecce high-speed trains, Intercity trains, Eurocity trains, and Italian domestic routes, with seat reservations mandatory and free of charge.

Regional trains work differently: paper tickets must be validated before boarding, though contactless "tap & tap" payment is being piloted on select routes. Routes with Tap & Tap include Florence-Pisa-Livorno and Venice Santa Lucia-Venice Mestre-Padua-Verona, and the same validator machines accept a contactless bank card tap in place of a paper ticket. Advance online booking pays off on faster trains: advance-purchase discounts are available online up to six months ahead for reserved, medium-speed and faster trains, coming in three price levels: "Super Economy," "Economy," and "Base" fare.

Within cities, Rome, Milan, Naples and Turin have metro systems, and most cities run integrated bus/tram/metro tickets. In Rome, a simple option is the BIT ticket, since the single ticket "BIT" (Biglietto Integrato a Tempo) sells for only €1.50, allowing travel on any bus and one trip on the metro or urban trains, with a 100-minute expiry period. If you're driving, watch for ZTL (limited traffic zone) cameras in historic centers, which issue automatic fines by mail.

## Money & costs

Italy uses the euro (€). Contactless cards are widely accepted in shops, restaurants and on transport, but keep some cash for small cafés, markets and tips. Italy's standard VAT (IVA) is high by EU standards: the current standard IVA rate of 22% places Italy among the highest VAT rates in the EU, comparable to Finland and Sweden (both 24%) and above the UK's 20%, France's 20%, Germany's 19%. Non-EU residents can often reclaim VAT on qualifying retail purchases; one commonly cited threshold is that if you're not a resident of the European Union and spend at least 70 Euros in one retail store on one day, you are eligible for a refund of part of the sales tax, though rules and minimums can vary, so ask the retailer.

Nearly every Italian city or town charges a separate overnight tourist tax (tassa/imposta di soggiorno), collected by your accommodation. This local levy applies for overnight stays and is calculated on a per-person, per-night basis, varying significantly from one city to another. Rome, for example, charges an overnight tourist tax in 2026 ranging from €10 per person per night in five-star hotels down to €3 in campsites, generally applied for a maximum of 10 consecutive nights. Some cities also now charge separate landmark or day-tripper fees, such as Venice's entry fee on peak days and Rome's new Trevi Fountain access ticket, so budget a little extra for these. Restaurant bills often include a small "coperto" cover charge, and tipping is appreciated but not obligatory.

## Best time to visit

For pleasant weather with fewer crowds and lower prices, aim for shoulder season. Shoulder season (April-May and September-October) offers pleasant temperatures of 18-25 degrees Celsius, manageable crowds, and lower prices. Peak summer is hot and busy: peak season (June through August) brings warm weather and long days but also higher prices and bigger crowds, with accommodation costs 30-50 percent higher than shoulder season. Winter (November to March) offers the lowest prices and thinnest crowds, ideal for cities like Rome, Florence and Milan, though beach destinations and the Alps operate on very different seasonal logic (ski season peaks December to March).

## Emergencies & safety

Save these numbers before you land. Italy's emergency numbers are 112 general (EU-wide, English available), 113 police, 115 fire, and 118 ambulance. The single European number works everywhere: dial 112 for any emergency, anywhere in Italy, whether police, ambulance or fire; it's free from any landline or mobile phone. For roadside breakdowns, call ACI Soccorso Stradale at 803 116 (24/7, from an Italian mobile or landline) or +39 06 49981 from abroad.

Overall, Italy is a low-risk destination for tourists. Italy is a very safe country to visit: violent crime against tourists is rare, the healthcare system is excellent, and help is genuinely easy to summon. The main practical risk is petty theft: the US travel advisory counsels American visitors to maintain vigilance against terrorism risks and transit-hub pickpocketing, particularly across railway networks, major piazzas, and historic city centers in Rome, Milan, Venice, and Florence. For medical care, public hospital emergency rooms (marked "Pronto Soccorso") treat everyone regardless of insurance, though non-EU citizens are not treated free of charge and will likely be charged a fixed "Ticket" fee for the visit, plus the costs of any diagnostic tests or medications, so travel insurance covering medical costs is strongly recommended.
