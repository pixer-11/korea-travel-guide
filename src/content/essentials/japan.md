**Quick answer:** Most Western passport holders can enter Japan visa-free for up to 90 days, IC cards (Suica/Pasmo) plus a JR Pass (only if doing long-distance travel) cover transport, cash and card both matter for payments, and the safest travel windows are spring (cherry blossoms) and autumn (foliage). Always double-check entry rules before you fly, since Japan is rolling out a new pre-travel authorization system in the coming years.

## Visa & entry

For short tourist trips, Japan currently runs a visa-exemption system rather than a universal e-visa requirement. As of September 2025, the exemption list covers 74 countries and regions, with a default stay of 90 days, though the exact stamp you receive depends on nationality and passport type. The status you're granted on landing is officially called "Temporary Visitor," which does not allow paid work, long-term study, or income-generating activities inside Japan. A handful of nationalities can extend once: UK, Germany, Ireland, Austria, Liechtenstein, Switzerland, and Mexico passport holders can apply at a Regional Immigration Bureau for a second 90 days; everyone else must leave when the stamp expires.

If your country is not on the visa-exempt list, you'll apply for a regular Japanese visa through an embassy or the Japan eVisa portal, since the new authorization system does not replace that process. Japan launched a digital eVisa option in December 2025 for travelers who still need a visa.

Big change on the horizon: Japan's parliament has created the legal basis for **JESTA** (Japan Electronic System for Travel Authorization), a mandatory pre-travel screening for visa-exempt visitors, similar to the US ESTA. Implementation is phased and set by government ordinance, with the main provisions required to take effect no later than March 31, 2029. In practical terms, you do not need JESTA for 2026 or 2027 travel, and no legitimate service can sell you a JESTA today, because the portal doesn't exist yet. Be wary of scam sites claiming otherwise.

Separately, the free **Visit Japan Web** service lets you pre-register customs and immigration details and generate a QR code for faster arrival; it remains optional but is widely recommended.

**Confirm before you travel:** visa-exemption lists, stay lengths, and the eVisa/JESTA rollout can all change. Check the official Ministry of Foreign Affairs and Immigration Services Agency pages listed below shortly before departure.

## Getting around

Japan's rail network is excellent, and most visitors use a mix of a rechargeable IC card and, for longer routes, a Japan Rail Pass.

For local trains, subways, and buses, get a **Suica** or **Pasmo** IC card (fully interchangeable, along with regional cards like Icoca): they're issued by different companies but work interchangeably across almost every train, bus, and store in Japan, and for tourist use Suica and PASMO are functionally identical. Tourist versions simplify things further: Welcome Suica is a tourist-only version of Suica with 28-day validity and no deposit, available at major airports and stations in Tokyo. A comparable Tourist PASMO also exists. Mobile wallet versions (Apple Pay/Google Pay) are increasingly popular alternatives to plastic cards.

For intercity travel, weigh up the **Japan Rail Pass** carefully rather than buying it by default. The 7-day nationwide JR Pass costs ¥50,000, so you'd need distance equivalent to a Tokyo–Hiroshima round trip to break even, and for most travelers sticking to the classic Tokyo–Kyoto–Osaka route, individual tickets are now cheaper. It pays off on bigger multi-city itineraries: a Tokyo → Kyoto → Hiroshima → Fukuoka → Tokyo itinerary bought as individual tickets runs roughly ¥57,000, versus the pass's flat fee plus unlimited extra JR rides. Note that the fastest Nozomi and Mizuho Shinkansen services are excluded from the pass, though the slightly slower Hikari and Kodama trains are covered. Many travelers now book reserved Shinkansen seats directly through the SmartEX app instead of queueing at a counter. Whichever you choose for long trips, plan to carry a Suica/Pasmo IC card too, since it covers local subways, buses, and non-JR lines that the JR Pass doesn't touch.

## Money & costs

Japan's currency is the yen (¥/JPY). The country is genuinely moving toward cashless payments, but hasn't fully arrived: Japan's cashless payment ratio hit 58% in 2025, with credit cards making up the overwhelming majority, followed by QR-code payments, electronic money and debit cards. Even so, shrines and temples, offerings, omamori charms, and entry fees are almost always cash, and small eateries, rural shops, and older ryokan can be cash-only too.

For cash, skip airport exchange counters. Most Japanese bank-branch ATMs don't accept foreign cards, but Seven Bank (inside 7-Eleven) and Japan Post Bank at post offices reliably do. 7-Eleven's Seven Bank network has over 20,000 ATMs across Japan, available roughly 24/7 in 12 languages. Always choose to be charged in yen at terminals and ATMs rather than accepting "dynamic currency conversion" in your home currency, which carries a worse rate.

Japan has essentially no tipping culture, so budget for goods and services at face value. A general daily cash buffer of around ¥15,000–30,000 for a few days is a reasonable starting point, topped up as needed, alongside a no-foreign-transaction-fee card for larger purchases.

## Best time to visit

Spring (roughly late March into April) draws crowds for cherry blossoms, while autumn (November) is prized for foliage; both are peak season with higher hotel prices and advance-booking pressure. Early summer brings a rainy season in most of the country, followed by hot, humid summer weather and a typhoon-prone window that typically runs from late summer into autumn, so travelers visiting August through October should keep an eye on storm forecasts. Winter is quieter and drier in much of the Pacific side of Japan and is peak season for skiing in Hokkaido and the Japan Alps. Shoulder seasons (late spring before the rains, or early winter) often balance milder crowds with good weather.

## Emergencies & safety

Japan uses separate numbers for police and for fire/medical emergencies, not a single unified line. Ambulance and Fire are both dialed as 119, while Police is 110. For emergencies at sea, dial 118 to contact the Japan Coast Guard. These numbers work from any phone, mobile, landline, or public payphone, at no charge, 24 hours a day.

Language can be a barrier: operators may answer in Japanese first, though the Tokyo Fire Department's 119 operator staff has English-speaking staff available, and other municipalities can connect callers to third-party interpreters. If you're unsure whether a situation needs an ambulance, some areas offer a non-emergency nurse advice line at **#7119**.

For general tourist assistance rather than a true emergency, the Japan National Tourism Organization runs a multilingual visitor hotline. Japan is a low-crime country overall, but standard precautions still apply: watch belongings in crowded tourist areas and train stations, and be aware that natural hazards like earthquakes and typhoons are a real part of life here; downloading a disaster-alert app such as Japan's official "Safety tips" app is worth doing before you arrive.
