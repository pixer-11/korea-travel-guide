**Quick answer:** Most Western passport holders can still enter Japan visa-free for up to 90 days, use an IC card (Suica/Pasmo) plus some cash for daily spending, and dial 110 for police or 119 for fire/ambulance in an emergency; note that Japan's departure tax tripled to ¥3,000 in July 2026, and a future "JESTA" pre-travel authorization is coming but will not affect trips before fiscal year 2028.

## Visa & entry

Japan still runs a straightforward visa-waiver system rather than a mandatory pre-clearance program. As of late 2026, the Ministry of Foreign Affairs' visa-exemption list, as of its 1 September 2025 update, has 74 entries, covering most of the US, Canada, the UK, the EU, Australia, New Zealand, Singapore and South Korea. Under Japan's visa exemption, most of the 74 visa-exempt nationalities receive Temporary Visitor status for up to 90 days per entry. Indonesia and Thailand get 15 days, Brunei and Qatar 30 days. A handful of nationalities, including the UK, can apply locally for an extension: the United Kingdom is also among the countries whose nationals may apply in Japan for an extension beyond 90 days under a bilateral arrangement; the extension is not automatic. Visa-free entry is never a guarantee of admission; an immigration officer can still refuse entry if you cannot show onward travel, sufficient funds, or a credible reason for visiting, so carry proof of accommodation and a return or onward ticket.

Two changes are worth watching. First, the departure levy has tripled from 1,000 yen to 3,000 yen from 1 July 2026, and it applies to both international visitors and Japanese residents leaving Japan and is usually included in the cost of airline and cruise tickets, meaning most travellers will not pay it separately. Second, Japan's parliament has now passed the legal basis for "JESTA" (Japan Electronic System for Travel Authorization), its equivalent of the US ESTA. On May 29, 2026, the Japanese Parliament adopted a revision of the Immigration Control and Refugee Recognition Act, creating the legal basis for the future system known as JESTA, which was then signed into law on June 5, 2026, as Law No. 32 of 2026. Unusually, the new JESTA system requires travelers to obtain authorization for every entry into Japan, unlike the US ESTA program. The system is not yet operational: for the main provisions related to the electronic authorization, implementation must take place no later than March 31, 2029, with a government target of launching during fiscal year 2028, and trips in 2026 and 2027 do not require it, and visa-free entry continues as normal until then. Be wary of any third-party site claiming to sell JESTA authorization now; it cannot yet be issued. Because details on fees are still unconfirmed, **always check the latest requirements on the official Ministry of Foreign Affairs, Immigration Services Agency, and Visit Japan Web sites before booking or departing** (links below).

## Getting around

Japan's rail network is excellent, and most visitors combine two tools. For intercity travel, the Japan Rail Pass covers unlimited rides on JR trains, including most shinkansen. Pricing changed in October 2026: on the official online reservation site, a 7-day Ordinary pass is still ¥50,000 and a 7-day Green Car pass ¥70,000, but Japan Rail Pass price rises of 5-6% took effect on 1 October 2026, and the 7-day pass now costs 53,000 yen when bought through overseas JR-designated agencies (Green Car rising to ¥74,000). The pass does not cover private railway companies or city subways, so you still need an IC card for many urban lines, and with higher prices the math has shifted: breaking even generally now requires several long-distance shinkansen rides in one week rather than a single Tokyo-Kyoto round trip.

For local transport, get an IC card on arrival. Suica and Pasmo are rechargeable tap-to-pay cards covering local trains, subways, buses and convenience-store purchases. Tourist versions exist for visitors: physical Welcome Suica and TOURIST PASMO cards are designed for visitors, and both are valid for 28 days from purchase, with unused balances not refunded when you finish your trip. You can also add Suica to Apple Wallet or Google Wallet to top up from your phone. For long-distance seat reservations without queueing, apps like SmartEX work for foreign visitors, and for budget travel between cities, overnight buses and low-cost carriers such as Peach or Jetstar Japan are worth comparing against rail fares.

## Luggage storage

Coin lockers are the default option, found at nearly every JR, subway and private-railway station, as well as at airports and shopping areas. JR East's own network, Multi Ecube, prices its lockers from about ¥300–400 for the smallest size up to ¥800–1,000 for the largest; Tokyo's official tourism board quotes a similar citywide range of roughly ¥400–800 for small-to-large lockers, with an extra-large size around ¥1,200. Older machines take only ¥100 coins; newer touch-panel units also accept an IC card like Suica or PASMO. Fees are charged per calendar day, so a bag left overnight is billed for two days.

Airports run their own separate lockers and staffed baggage counters for anything too big to fit: at Narita, coin lockers charge ¥400 for a small bag, ¥600 for medium and ¥800 for large per day, and staffed counters take oversized items the lockers can't. Most hotels, ryokan and hostels will hold bags before check-in or after check-out at the front desk, and larger hotels can arrange to have bags forwarded ahead to your next stop or back to the airport.

## Money & costs

The currency is the Japanese yen (¥), and Japan is increasingly cashless but not fully so. According to Japan's Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry, the cashless payment ratio in 2025 steadily increased to 58.0%, and cards are still the backbone of the Japanese payment market, making up 82.7% of Japan's cashless spending, with QR-code payments like PayPay a fast-growing second category. Even so, shrines, small rural eateries, traditional market stalls, some taxis and ramen-shop ticket machines are still cash-only, so carry a modest buffer, lead with cards, and top up cash from a 7-Eleven ATM when needed.

For withdrawals, stick to reliable networks: Seven Bank states that the withdrawal limit for overseas cards is 100,000 yen per withdrawal, with a lower 30,000 yen limit for magnetic-stripe transactions, and these machines run close to 24 hours at most 7-Elevens. Japan Post Bank ATMs also accept foreign cards and have English menus, though they cap withdrawals lower and keep shorter opening hours than Seven Bank, so confirm current hours locally. Many ordinary bank-branded ATMs still reject foreign cards. Visa and Mastercard have the widest acceptance in shops and restaurants, JCB is widely usable, and Amex acceptance is patchier. Tipping is not customary in Japan and can feel awkward, since service charges are usually built into the bill.

## Best time to visit

Japan rewards planning around its seasons. Spring (late March to early April) brings cherry blossoms and mild weather, making it one of the busiest and priciest windows for hotels and shinkansen. Autumn (mid-November) offers vivid foliage in Kyoto, Nikko, and the Japanese Alps with similarly pleasant, dry conditions. Summer (June to August) is hot and humid, with a rainy season in June and typhoon risk peaking from August through October, so build flexibility into itineraries during those months. Winter (December to February) is cold but generally dry and sunny on the Pacific coast, ideal for hot springs, illuminations, and skiing in Hokkaido or Nagano, though the Sea of Japan side sees heavy snow. For fewer crowds and reasonable weather, late spring (May) and early autumn (October) are good compromise windows.

## Emergencies & safety

Japan uses separate numbers for police and medical/fire emergencies rather than a single hotline. Ambulance and fire: dial 119. Police: dial 110. Maritime emergencies use 118 for the Japan Coast Guard. These calls are free from any landline or mobile phone with an active SIM; a phone with no SIM is not reliable for emergency calls in Japan. If you're unsure whether a situation needs an ambulance, many prefectures and cities, including Tokyo and Osaka, run a #7119 advice line, though it is not available everywhere.

Language can be a barrier: few police or fire department staff speak English, though interpreters can often be connected. For general visitor assistance in English (lost items, illness, disasters, general guidance), the Japan Visitor Hotline, run by the Japan National Tourism Organization, is a 24-hour line that handles these issues in English, Chinese, and Korean; confirm the current number on JNTO's official site before you travel, since contact details can change. For non-urgent help such as directions or a lost item, visit a neighborhood police box (koban). Japan remains statistically very safe, but standard precautions around natural disasters (earthquakes, typhoons) and pickpocketing in crowded tourist areas still apply.
