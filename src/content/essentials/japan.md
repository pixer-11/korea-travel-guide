**Quick answer:** Most Western passport holders can visit Japan visa-free for up to 90 days using just a passport and the Visit Japan Web portal, get around easily with an IC card (Suica/Pasmo) and/or Japan Rail Pass, and should carry some cash alongside a no-foreign-fee card — but always double-check current rules on official sites before you fly, since Japan's entry system is changing (a new ETA called JESTA is coming, though not yet required).

## Visa & entry

To enter Japan in 2026 as a tourist, most travelers from visa-exempt countries need a valid passport, a completed registration on the Visit Japan Web portal (to obtain Immigration and Customs QR codes), and a confirmed onward or return ticket, with stays typically granted for up to 90 days. As of September 1, 2025, the visa-exemption list covers 74 countries and regions, with the default period of stay of 90 days, though the actual stamp depends on your nationality, passport type, and whether you completed pre-registration.

Visa-free entry grants "Temporary Visitor" status, which is strictly for tourism, visiting friends or family, or attending unpaid conferences — you cannot engage in any paid work, remunerated activities, or local employment under this status. Extensions are generally not available, except for nationals of the UK, Germany, Ireland, Austria, Liechtenstein, Switzerland, or Mexico, who can apply at a Regional Immigration Bureau for a second 90 days.

Citizens of countries not on the waiver list — including China, India, Vietnam, and others — need a traditional or eVisa. Japan's eVISA system became available for Indian residents on September 1, 2025, offering a single-entry, 90-day visa processed entirely online with no embassy visit required.

**Important 2026–2029 heads-up:** Japan is building a new mandatory pre-travel screening system called **JESTA** (Japan Electronic System for Travel Authorization), similar to the US ESTA. JESTA does not exist as a working system yet — you do not need to do anything extra to fly to Japan in 2026 or 2027 beyond having a valid passport and, if your country is not visa-exempt, a Japanese visa. On May 29, 2026, the Japanese Parliament adopted a revision of the Immigration Control and Refugee Recognition Act, creating the legal basis for JESTA, but it is expected to launch by the end of March 2029.

**Because these rules evolve quickly, always confirm your specific visa/entry requirements on the official MOFA and Immigration Services Agency sites before booking.**

## Getting around

Japan's rail network is the backbone of getting around, and visitors typically choose between a prepaid IC card, a rail pass, or a mix of both.

- **IC cards (Suica / Pasmo / ICOCA):** You tap the card at the gate when you enter and tap again when you exit — the correct fare is deducted automatically, no ticket, no thinking. All major convenience-store chains, such as 7-Eleven, Lawson, and FamilyMart, accept IC card payments — just tap at the register. For tourists, dedicated versions exist: the Welcome Suica and Pasmo Passport are valid for 28 days, after which they expire, require no deposit, and require showing your passport to prove you're a non-resident. Note that Tourist PASMO launched on 20 May 2026, replacing the previous PASMO PASSPORT program. iPhone users can also skip the counter entirely: Welcome Suica Mobile is a free, iPhone-only digital tourist card from JR East, with 180-day validity and no airport counter line.
- **Japan Rail Pass:** Best for multi-city trips. The Japan Rail Pass is a flat-rate unlimited-travel ticket for JR lines across the country, including most Shinkansen (Nozomi and Mizuho require a surcharge). As a rule of thumb, it's worth buying if you're visiting 3+ cities in 7 days; for single-city deep dives, a Suica IC card is all you need. Regional JR passes can also be cheaper alternatives depending on your itinerary.
- **Local passes:** City-specific options like the Tokyo Subway 24/48/72-Hour Pass are handy for concentrated sightseeing in one metro area.
- IC cards cover local trains, subways, buses, and convenience stores, but for most tourists it's simplest to buy a Shinkansen ticket separately (or use a JR Pass) and use your IC card for local travel.

Trains run on time, English signage is widespread in major cities, and Google Maps/Japan Transit apps handle route planning well.

## Money & costs

Japan's currency is the Japanese yen (¥). While cashless payment is rising fast — cashless payments reached 58.0% of consumer spending in 2025, according to Japan's Ministry of Economy, Trade and Industry, and in big cities you can tap a card or phone almost everywhere — cash still matters: cash is essential at small family-run restaurants, temples and shrines, market stalls, festival food carts, older ryokan, and just about anywhere off the tourist trail.

For getting yen, avoid airport exchange counters where possible. The Seven Bank ATM (inside 7-Eleven) and Japan Post Bank (Yucho) ATM both explicitly accept overseas-issued debit and credit cards, with over 28,000 machines across Japan running 24 hours a day, and they accept Visa, Plus, Mastercard, Maestro, Cirrus, UnionPay, American Express and JCB cards issued abroad. The withdrawal cap is 100,000 yen per withdrawal for overseas cards (30,000 yen for magnetic-stripe transactions). A key catch: most ordinary Japanese bank ATMs — the ones inside bank branches — simply won't accept a card issued outside Japan, so stick to convenience-store or post-office machines.

For cards, Visa and Mastercard are accepted across city hotels, department stores, chain restaurants and attractions; American Express and JCB are more variable. When paying by card, always pick yen rather than your home currency, since dynamic currency conversion bakes in a poor exchange rate. Tipping isn't customary in Japan.

For tax-free shopping, it's currently an immediate exemption — consumption tax is taken off at the register in tax-free shops if you spend ¥5,000 or more (pre-tax) at the same store on the same day and show your passport — but this version runs only through October 31, 2026, after which a refund-at-departure model is planned.

## Best time to visit

Japan has four distinct seasons, and the "best" time depends on your priorities:

- **Spring (late March–early April):** Cherry blossom season is the most popular time to visit, drawing huge crowds to Tokyo and Kyoto — book flights and accommodation well in advance.
- **Autumn (October–November):** Comfortable temperatures and vivid fall foliage make this a favorite alternative to spring, with somewhat fewer crowds.
- **Summer (June–August):** Hot, humid, and rainy (June rainy season/tsuyu), followed by intense heat; useful to know for safety planning, since Tokyo has seen record heat-related ambulance transports in recent summers.
- **Winter (December–February):** Cold but dry in most cities, and prime time for skiing in Hokkaido and the Japanese Alps, plus lower hotel rates outside New Year holidays.

Whichever season, expect major domestic travel congestion around Golden Week (late April–early May), Obon (mid-August), and New Year — book transport and lodging early if traveling then.

## Emergencies & safety

Japan uses separate numbers for police and medical/fire emergencies rather than a single hotline:

- **Police: 110** — call this number to report crimes or accidents.
- **Fire/Ambulance: 119** — use this for medical emergencies or to report a fire. Ambulances do not charge for transportation to hospitals, but patients are responsible for medical expenses, so travel insurance is strongly advised.
- **Coast Guard: 118** — for maritime and waterway emergencies: boat accidents, drowning at a beach or river, suspicious activity on the water.
- **Non-urgent medical advice: #7119** — if you're not sure whether you need an ambulance, a trained nurse answers, asks about your symptoms, and tells you whether to call 119, go to the ER, or wait, though this service is largely Japanese-only in most areas.
- **Japan Visitor Hotline (JNTO): 050-3816-2787** — for non-life-threatening situations where you need guidance, this hotline operates 24 hours a day, 365 days a year, offering assistance in English, Chinese, Korean, and Japanese.
- **Non-emergency police advice: #9110** in many areas, or visit a local *koban* (police box) for directions and help.

Japan is generally very safe for travelers, but it's wise to install the "Safety tips" app and monitor NHK WORLD-JAPAN, both recommended in official disaster guides for push urgent warnings about earthquakes, typhoons, and other natural events.
