**Quick answer:** Most nationalities need a Cambodian e-Visa or visa on arrival for a 30-day stay (plus a free e-Arrival Card for all air arrivals), prices are mostly quoted in US dollars with riel for small change, and the cool, dry months of November to February are the best window for comfortable temple-going.

## Visa & entry

Most visitors to Cambodia must obtain a visa, either a visa on arrival or an e-Visa, unless they are citizens of one of the visa-exempt countries, which include several ASEAN nationalities with durations that vary by passport. For most other nationalities, the standard tourist e-Visa or visa on arrival grants a 30-day stay.

The e-Visa, applied for only at the official evisa.gov.kh, costs $30 for the visa plus a $6 processing fee, for about $36 total, is valid for 3 months from the date of issue, with normal processing taking about 3 business days. The visa on arrival, available at airports and major land borders, costs US$30 for a tourist visa, paid in US dollars cash, with one passport photo; if you forget a photo, officials can scan one for a small extra fee.

A newer, separate requirement: anyone flying into Cambodia must also submit the free Cambodia e-Arrival Card. All air passengers entering Cambodia must complete the e-Arrival Card before entry, regardless of their nationality or visa type, applying equally to foreign visitors, e-Visa holders, visa-on-arrival travelers, and Cambodian passport holders. It can be submitted within 7 days before arrival at arrival.gov.kh, and it is free; avoid third-party sites that charge for it. Importantly, as of 2026, the digital e-Arrival Card system is only mandatory for arrivals at international airports, so overland crossings do not currently require it.

Basic entry conditions still apply across the board: a passport valid for at least six months from arrival, undamaged, with a blank page, plus proof of onward or return travel.

**Confirm before you fly.** Visa fees, exemptions and the e-Arrival rules have changed more than once recently, so always re-check the official e-Visa portal, the e-Arrival site and the General Department of Immigration before you travel, and double-check your specific nationality's exemption status with your nearest Cambodian embassy.

## Getting around

**Between Phnom Penh and Siem Reap:** road, air and river options all exist. A bus takes roughly six hours and costs around $9 to $12 with several reputable operators. Flights average about 45 minutes, with fares varying by season and carrier. Ferries run seasonally and typically pause around the height of the wet season.

**Within cities:** Tuk-tuks remain the classic way to get around, and negotiating the fare up front (or carrying small dollar bills or riel) still helps. Ride-hailing apps have taken over much of the haggling-free market, with PassApp dominant in Phnom Penh and Siem Reap, alongside Grab and other apps. Motorbike taxis (motos) are common for short hops. Phnom Penh's formal city bus network keeps expanding, though it remains more commuter-oriented than tourist-friendly.

**Angkor Archaeological Park:** buy passes only through the official angkorenterprise.gov.kh portal, the ticket office on Apsara Road in Siem Reap, or official kiosks. Current 2026 pricing is $37 (1-day), $62 (3-day), or $72 (7-day), with children under 12 entering free with a valid passport. The pass covers over 50 accessible sites, from Angkor Wat itself out to Banteay Srei in the countryside, and multi-day passes don't need to be used on consecutive days, so you can space out visits.

## Money & costs

Cambodia runs on two currencies at once: US dollars for anything over roughly $10 to $20, Cambodian riel for small change, at an exchange rate of about 4,000 KHR to $1. Most tourist-facing prices (hotels, tours, menus) are quoted in dollars, with riel handed back as change on smaller purchases.

Bring clean, undamaged notes: Cambodian cash-handlers are notoriously strict about torn, marked or very worn dollar bills, and coins aren't used at all, so stock up on crisp notes before you arrive.

ATMs are widely available in Phnom Penh, Siem Reap and Sihanoukville but carry real fees. ATMs are common in cities, dispense mainly USD $100 bills, and usually charge $4–$6 per withdrawal, with some banks charging more and others offering riel as an alternative. Shopping around pays off: PPCBank, for example, lists a $5 fee per withdrawal of up to $1,000 on overseas-issued cards, and your own bank may add its fee on top; fewer, larger withdrawals minimize the proportional cost. In rural areas and on islands, ATMs are rare, so withdraw cash before leaving the city.

Cards and digital payments cover a lot of ground now. Most hotels and tour operators in Phnom Penh and Siem Reap accept Visa, Mastercard, Apple Pay and Google Pay, and KHQR codes are accepted at around 4.5 million merchant locations, with the Bakong Tourist App working without a Cambodian bank account. Fintech travel cards generally work fine too, including Wise and Revolut. Still, don't go fully cashless: tuk-tuks, markets and rural areas still run largely on cash.

## Best time to visit

The best time to visit is the dry season, roughly November to April, when humidity drops and the temples stand against clearer skies. Within that window, November to February is the sweet spot: cool, comfortable, and the most pleasant stretch for long days at Angkor, though also the busiest and priciest.

March through May shifts into a hot, dry stretch, with temperatures climbing uncomfortably high by April and May, the most physically demanding months to visit. This period does include the Khmer New Year, which falls on April 14–16, 2026, a 3-day public holiday with nationwide celebrations and water fights.

The wet season runs roughly June through October, bringing afternoon downpours but also lush green landscapes, thinner crowds and lower prices. If traveling in the shoulder period, note that Pchum Ben falls on October 10–12, 2026, a major ancestor-remembrance festival during which many Cambodians travel home to their provinces or to pagodas, so intercity buses and shared taxis can fill up fast around those dates.

## Emergencies & safety

Cambodia's core emergency numbers are simple three-digit codes: Police 117, Fire 118, Ambulance 119.

If you need to report a crime as a tourist, go through the tourist police rather than a regular precinct where possible. Reporting locations include the Tourist Police in Phnom Penh at phone 012 942 484, address St. 598, and in Siem Reap at phone 012 402 424. A police report for insurance purposes should be free; if an officer asks for a fee, contact your embassy in Phnom Penh with details of the station.

Language can be a barrier outside the capital and Siem Reap, so having a translator, friend or hotel staff member on hand helps when dealing with provincial police. If you've been the victim of a crime, loop in your embassy early, as they can help replace a passport, connect you with local attorneys or translators, and assist with medical referrals.

For medical emergencies in the capital, Calmette Hospital's ambulance service can be reached at 119, 023 724 891 or 023 426 948. Save local numbers as you travel beyond Phnom Penh, since province-specific hotlines exist, and dialling in from abroad requires the country code 855.
