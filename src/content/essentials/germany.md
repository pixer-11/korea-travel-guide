**Quick answer:** Most visitors from the US, UK, Canada, Australia, and similar countries can still enter Germany visa-free for up to 90 days in any 180-day period; border checks now record biometrics under the EU's new Entry/Exit System, but the planned ETIAS travel-authorisation requirement has been delayed with no confirmed start date. Always verify your specific requirements before flying.

## Visa & entry

Germany is part of the Schengen Area, so entry rules are set at the EU level, not by Germany alone. If you hold a passport from the US, Canada, Australia, Japan, or another country with a visa liberalisation agreement with the EU, you can visit for up to 90 days in any 180-day period without a visa. Citizens of many other countries, including India, China, and South Africa, still need a Schengen Type C visa before arrival; this short-stay visa has a maximum validity of 90 days in a 180-day period and costs €90 for adults, with children ages 6 to 11 paying €45 and children under 6 able to apply for free. Expect to pay an additional service fee of roughly €30 to €50 if you apply through a visa centre such as VFS Global.

Two EU-wide border systems are relevant in late 2026, and they are easy to confuse:

- **The Entry/Exit System (EES)** is live. It began a phased rollout on 12 October 2025 and became fully operational from 10 April 2026. It captures a facial image and four fingerprints on your first entry and links them to your passport and entry/exit dates, replacing manual passport stamps for non-EU travellers.
- **ETIAS**, the planned €20 pre-travel authorisation for visa-exempt nationals, is **not** required yet and has no confirmed launch date. The EU had targeted the last quarter of 2026, but until the middle of 2026 the European Commission said ETIAS would start in the last quarter of 2026; that line is gone, and the Commission's ETIAS page now says only that the system is not in operation. As of late October 2026, ETIAS is not yet in operation and no applications are being collected, and a 2027 launch is now widely expected. Be wary of any site asking you to pay for ETIAS now; the official EU site states that ETIAS is currently not in operation and no applications for travel authorizations are collected at this point.

Because these systems and their launch dates keep shifting, treat everything above as a snapshot, not a guarantee. Before booking or flying, confirm your personal requirements on the official Auswärtiges Amt (German Federal Foreign Office) visa pages and the EU's official ETIAS/EES information portal.

## Getting around

Germany has a dense, reliable public transport network: city U-Bahn (subway) and S-Bahn (suburban rail), trams, and buses in most cities, plus regional trains connecting towns, and high-speed ICE/IC trains linking major cities.

For visitors planning more than a few local trips, the Deutschlandticket is worth knowing about. It is a rolling monthly subscription valid on all local and regional public transport across Germany, including buses, trams, subways, S-Bahn lines, and regional trains (RE/RB) in every federal state. The Deutschland-Ticket gives you unlimited travel across Germany for just €63 a month as of January 1, 2026. Note that the price is set to rise again: in January 2027 the price of the Deutschlandticket increases from €63 to €66.80 per month. The ticket does **not** cover long-distance trains; it is not valid on long-distance trains such as the ICE, IC, or EC trains. It is sold as an auto-renewing monthly subscription, and it can be cancelled from one month to the next if done before the 10th of the month.

For single or short trips, local transport associations (Verkehrsverbünde) sell single tickets, day passes, and multi-ride tickets; prices vary by city. Long-distance travel between cities is best done via Deutsche Bahn's ICE/IC network, or by budget coach companies like FlixBus for cheaper, slower journeys. Renting a car gives flexibility for rural regions like the Black Forest or Bavarian Alps, but note Autobahn sections without speed limits and strict drink-driving laws.

Trains and buses are generally punctual but can suffer delays; apps like DB Navigator (for trains) and city-specific transit apps are essential for real-time planning and ticket purchases.

## Money & costs

The currency is the euro (€). Germany remains more cash-reliant than some neighbours, especially in smaller shops, markets, and some restaurants or cafés, though contactless card and mobile payments (Apple Pay, Google Pay) are now widely accepted in cities, supermarkets, and chain stores. It is still wise to carry some cash for small purchases, bakeries, or rural areas.

Visa and Mastercard debit/credit cards work at most ATMs (Geldautomaten) and larger retailers; American Express is accepted less widely. If your card is lost or stolen, use the nationwide blocking hotline: Germany has a central card-blocking service called the "Sperrnotruf," reachable on 116 116 from within Germany, working 24 hours a day, 7 days a week, and covering most German bank cards and credit cards. From abroad, dial +49 116 116.

Typical daily budgets vary widely by city and travel style: budget travellers can manage on roughly €60 to €90 a day covering hostel accommodation, public transport, and simple meals, while mid-range travellers might spend €120 to €200 a day for a private hotel room, restaurant meals, and sightseeing. Expect higher costs in Munich, Frankfurt, and Berlin compared with smaller towns.

## Best time to visit

Germany has a temperate climate with four distinct seasons, so the "best" time depends on your priorities.

Late spring to early autumn (May through September) brings the warmest, sunniest weather, making it ideal for outdoor sightseeing, hiking in regions like the Black Forest, and enjoying beer gardens. This is peak tourist season, so expect higher prices and more crowds at major sights.

Autumn, especially September and early October, offers mild weather and is prime time for Oktoberfest in Munich and for enjoying the change of colours in forests and vineyards. Winter (December) transforms German cities with famous Christmas markets (Weihnachtsmärkte), though temperatures can be cold, particularly in the north and in the Alps. Shoulder seasons (April, late October, November) tend to offer lower prices and thinner crowds, at the cost of less predictable weather.

For a balance of pleasant weather, manageable crowds, and lower costs, many travel guides recommend visiting in May, June, or September.

## Emergencies & safety

Germany is considered one of the safest countries in Europe for travellers, though normal city precautions against pickpocketing apply in crowded tourist areas and on public transport.

The official emergency numbers in Germany are 112 for ambulance and fire services, and 110 for police. These numbers are free and work from any phone, even without a SIM card. For urgent-but-not-life-threatening medical help outside GP hours, call 116117, the on-call doctor service (ärztlicher Bereitschaftsdienst), which is free.

If you're ever unsure between 112 and 110 in a genuine emergency, call 112, as operators coordinate with police too. Operators at both numbers can typically switch to English if needed. On motorways, do not stop except in a genuine emergency, and look for the orange roadside emergency phones if you cannot reach help by mobile. If you need non-urgent police assistance, each city has its own non-emergency contact line; check local tourism resources for the number in the city you are visiting.

Travel insurance covering medical treatment is strongly recommended for all visitors, since German healthcare, while excellent, is not free for tourists without applicable insurance (EU/EHIC arrangements only cover EU nationals).
